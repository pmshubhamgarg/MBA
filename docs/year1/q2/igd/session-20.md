# Session 20: The Mirror Test — Knowing Yourself Needs a Second Opinion

*The last IGD class isn't a lecture — it's a showcase. One study group after another holds up the Big Five mirror and reports what it saw. The closing lesson of the whole course: you cannot fully know yourself alone. Measure your traits, let your peers catch your blind spots, and treat personality as a system you keep working on.*

## Setting the Stage: The Final Class Is a Mirror, Not a Lecture

This is **Session 20 — the last IGD session**, run over Zoom. There is no new theory on the board. Instead, the **study groups present their Big Five personality project**, the capstone that the whole "understand yourself" half of the course was building toward. Each group walks through how its members rated one another, how those ratings compared with each person's own test score, and what everyone learned.

So the exam-useful content here is not a fresh framework — it is the course's central idea **cashed out in practice**: people are unique bundles of traits, and the only honest way to read yourself is to combine the test, your own reflection, and what others see.

---

## What the Project Actually Was: Two Sources, One Portrait

Every group followed the same design. First they **interviewed each other** about personality; then they each **took the Big Five test** and compared the two. That comparison is the heart of the exercise.

| Stage | What happened | Nature | What it revealed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1 — Peer interviews** | Each member interviews teammates on a trait; semi-structured, narrative, sometimes recorded | **Qualitative** | How others see you, and your behaviour *in context* |
| **Stage 2 — Self-test + compare** | Take the Big Five (OCEAN) self-assessment, then line your score up against the peer reviews | **Quantitative + comparison** | Where the two **agree**, where they **diverge** — the blind spots. Students called this the "**reality check**" or "**mirror test**" |
| **Stage 3 — Consolidated report** | Write up the combined picture and submit for marks | — | The validated, **triangulated** portrait of each person |

The repeated phrase across almost every group was some version of: *don't trust one number.* A peer review alone is one person's opinion; a test score alone is just how you rate yourself on a given day. Put together, they "**reveal a full picture and help us overcome the limitations**" of either source.

> **A student:** "Never rely on a single data point — use a multi-dimensional approach: the qualitative interviews *plus* the quantitative test."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Triangulation beats guesswork.** A reliable read of personality needs **more than one source**: your **self-rating** (quantitative test), **others' observations** (qualitative peer feedback), and honest **self-reflection** on the gap. If an exam case gives you only one data point — one appraisal, one self-claim — the correct move is to say it is **insufficient** and ask for a second, independent view before judging a person.
:::

## The Five Traits, One Last Time (OCEAN)

Because every group leaned on it, the **Big Five / OCEAN** model is the thing to have at your fingertips. Each trait sits on a spectrum — there is no "good" or "bad" end, only a profile.

| Trait (OCEAN) | What it captures | High looks like | Low looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **O — Openness** | Curiosity, imagination, appetite for new ideas | Idea generator; tries new things; "curiosity catalyst" | Prefers the familiar; sticks to what already works |
| **C — Conscientiousness** | Discipline, organisation, reliability, planning | Deadlines are "sacred promises"; dependable; detail-oriented | Spontaneous; loose with plans; "I'll wing it" |
| **E — Extraversion** | Sociability, assertiveness, where you draw energy | Outgoing; energises the room | Quiet contributor; recharges **alone** (often an **ambivert**) |
| **A — Agreeableness** | Empathy, cooperation, trust, harmony | Accommodating; avoids turning discussion into debate | Direct; prioritises logic over social harmony |
| **N — Neuroticism** | Tendency to stress, worry, overthink | Overthinks small issues; feels pressure | Calm under pressure; **composed** even in chaos |

A memorable touch from one group (Group 18) reframed the five traits as the **Pancha Tatwa** — the five elements:

- **Space → Openness** (the dreamer)
- **Earth → Conscientiousness** (the planner, grounded)
- **Air → Extraversion** (the social flow)
- **Water → Agreeableness** (the harmoniser that adapts and fits in)
- **Fire → Neuroticism** (the inner intensity that, left to linger, burns)

:::tip[Memory Hook — "OCEAN = Space, Earth, Air, Water, Fire"]
Can't recall the Big Five in the exam? Walk the **five elements**: **Space** (Openness, the dreamer), **Earth** (Conscientiousness, the planner), **Air** (Extraversion, the social flow), **Water** (Agreeableness, the harmoniser), **Fire** (Neuroticism, the inner heat). Five elements, five traits — and **low fire** means you stay cool under pressure.
:::

## Self vs Perception: Where Blind Spots Live

The sharpest learning was not any single score — it was the **gap** between how students saw themselves and how their peers saw them. Over and over, someone rated themselves low on a trait while the whole group saw it clearly (very often **agreeableness**), or thought they were organised while peers never noticed it.

One student put the paradox beautifully, borrowing a line said to be Ravana's — *"I am the only one; there is no second"* — and then turning it on himself:

> **A student:** "If there's only one me, which one is it — the me I think I am, or the me in my peers' perception?"

That gap has a name you already met earlier in the course: a **blind spot**. The peer feedback is valuable precisely because **others can see what you miss**. And the fix is not just to *know* yourself but to be *seen* accurately.

> **A student:** "Self-awareness is something, but visibility matters too. If I'm organised, people should actually notice it."

Two other honest admissions worth banking as exam lines: **"the test didn't just measure traits, it held up a mirror,"** and the reminder that **intent and action don't always match** — how you *think* you'll react in a situation can differ from how you actually do.

:::tip[Important — Self-Awareness Plus Visibility]
Being self-aware is only half the job. If your strengths are **invisible** to the people around you, you won't get credit or trust for them. The practical takeaway students landed on: make your good traits **observable** (do the organising *where people can see it*) — but "without turning up the volume too much."
:::

:::note[Good to Know — "Personality 3.0"]
One group framed the whole project in software language: Stage 1 was reading the "base code," Stage 2 was the "system test" of how others experience your interface, and the blind spots your peers surfaced were "features you didn't know you had." Their sign-off — **"personality isn't a fixed label; it's a life system of continuous self-updates"** — is a memorable way to say personality is **dynamic**, shaped by feedback, reflection and experience.
:::

## Traits Are Enduring — Behaviour Is Workable

The students stressed that personality is *dynamic*. The professor added the important counter-balance when a student asked, near the very end, how to **improve a trait** before stepping into leadership roles.

Her answer draws a careful line: your underlying **traits are stable**, but you can still choose which behaviours to build, once you know the target.

> **The professor:** "Personality traits are enduring characteristics. First you should know what are the **desirable traits in the workplace**, and accordingly you can **match your traits with the desirable traits**, and then work on how to improve yourself."

She also tied it back to leadership: a good leader eventually needs a bit of **every** trait, so **every trait's improvement matters** — you don't get to coast on the two or three that come naturally. The student summarised his own version of this perfectly:

> **A student:** "Leadership isn't about always being composed — it's about being *approachably* composed."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Enduring vs changeable, stated cleanly:** personality **traits** are stable over time, so you can't "swap" someone's temperament — but **behaviour and self-management can be developed**. The method the professor gave is a tidy three-step answer for any "how do I grow this person / myself?" question: **(1)** identify the **desirable traits** the role or workplace demands → **(2)** **compare** your current profile against them → **(3)** deliberately **work on the gap**. The test tells you where you stand; effort closes the distance.
:::

## The Professor's Send-Off, Exam and Grading Guidance

Because this is the final class, the professor closes the course and shares the last logistics — all worth noting.

> **The professor:** "Finally, we are done with IGD. It has been a wonderful journey teaching you this course. I hope whatever you have learned goes beyond the classroom… so until then, goodbye and all the very best."

She also previews that she'll teach **Strategic HRM (SHRM) next term** and a **leadership subject next year**, and a student captures the spirit of the whole course in one line: *"It was a detox to our personality — we came to know what we actually are."*

**On the exam and marks (capture this):**

- The professor **shared her PPT slide decks** as the material to revise from for the exam — so your revision source is **her slides**, not outside reading.
- **Class Participation (CP)** marks are **normalised to a 6-to-9 range**: the **maximum is 9**, the **minimum is 6**.
- You essentially **don't get a perfect 10** in IGD, because the subject is **highly subjective** ("10 means perfect, and that's very tough to give").
- **Stage 2** and **Stage 3** project marks were to be released right after the session.

:::note[Good to Know — Why No 10 out of 10]
IGD is a "soft," **subjective** subject — judgement, empathy and self-reflection don't have one right answer. That's *why* the grading band is compressed to **6 to 9**: it rewards solid, thoughtful work without pretending any human-behaviour answer is flawless. Translation for your own answers: aim for **well-reasoned and well-evidenced**, not "textbook-perfect."
:::

## Key Terms Table (Quick Revision)

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Big Five / OCEAN** | The five-trait personality model: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism |
| **Qualitative assessment** | Narrative, semi-structured read of personality — e.g. a **peer interview** |
| **Quantitative assessment** | A structured **self-rating test** that gives numeric scores |
| **Triangulation** | Combining multiple sources (test + peers + self-reflection) so no single data point misleads you |
| **Blind spot** | A trait others see clearly in you that you miss in yourself |
| **Self-awareness vs visibility** | Knowing your own traits vs those traits being *noticed* by others |
| **Intent-vs-action gap** | How you think you'll behave can differ from how you actually behave |
| **Enduring traits** | Personality traits are stable over time; behaviour can still be developed |
| **Desirable-traits matching** | Improve by identifying the traits a role needs, then closing the gap |
| **Ambivert** | Neither clearly introvert nor extrovert — social when needed, recharges alone |
| **CP normalisation (6–9)** | Class-participation marks banded between a minimum 6 and maximum 9 |

## The Recipe Chain (Where Session 20 Sits)

**Session 20 is the finish line — the course's "know yourself" promise, delivered.**

> Session 1 → *Why* people differ: the **P-factors**, and the zoom-out plan **Self → Others → Organization**.
> The middle sessions → built out **Self** (values, attitudes, **personality**, biases, motivation) and then **Others / Organization** (emotions, teams, conflict, power, leadership).
> **Session 20 → the capstone project: measure your own Big Five, let peers mirror you, triangulate the two, and own your blind spots — then carry that self-knowledge beyond the classroom.**

The course that opened by asking *"why do two people behave differently in the same situation?"* ends by handing each student a validated, peer-checked answer about **their own** behaviour — and the professor's parting nudge that the real work (improving the traits a workplace rewards) starts now, continuing into **SHRM** next term.

---

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 20 proves the whole subject in miniature: you can't manage people — or yourself — from a single viewpoint. **Run the test, ask the peers, sit with the gap.** The traits you're born with are enduring, but the behaviours a workplace rewards are learnable once you know the target. That loop — **measure, get mirrored, work on the gap** — is the habit IGD wants you to keep long after the exam.
