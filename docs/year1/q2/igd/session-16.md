# Session 16: The Star Who Was Told She Couldn't Lead

*McKenna Lane hits every target but can't win her own team — so her company bets a full year of executive coaching on her. Now her boss has to answer the hardest question in leadership: did she really change, or did she just learn to look the part?*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** What **executive coaching** is — "**unblock potential, don't fix problems**"; the **steps** of a coaching program; the coach's **three practices** — **Relational, Independent, Self-generation** — and why the last two prove **authentic (not superficial) transformation**; the **three pillars** a coached leader builds — **self-awareness, collaboration, authenticity**; **behavioral theory** (leaders are *made*, by balancing **task + relationship**); **manager vs leader** (practicality vs possibility).

**Important (supporting):** The **Birkman Method** (four lenses) and its stand-ins — **Big Five, Career Anchors, Strengths Profile**; **360-degree feedback**; **Total Leadership** (four life domains); **transactional → transformational** shift; **referent power**; **self-care → leadership presence**; "respond, don't react"; "small changes, big difference."

**Context (colour for answers):** McKenna Lane's grit (born in the Cambodian genocide, HBS, McKinsey); **365 Emporium**'s traditional, close-knit culture; boss **John Mills**; mentor **Alec Fernandez** (his exit = loss of political cover); coach **Betsy Hale**; engagement lifted **36% → 72%**, **5%** year-on-year growth; the recurring IGD cases — **Michael Peterson, Amalia Rosas, McKenna Lane**.
:::

## Quick Recap: The Pattern You've Already Seen

The professor opened by tying this case back to leadership theory. Two theories matter here:

- **Trait theory** — leaders are *born* with certain qualities; leadership is innate.
- **Behavioral theory** — no, leaders can be *trained*, by learning to balance **task and relationship**.

> **The professor:** "Leadership is not innate. People can be trained to be good leaders — and who is a good leader? The one who can create a balance between task and people."

And she flagged the pattern running through *every* IGD case: **Michael Peterson, Amalia Rosas, and now McKenna Lane** — all star performers, all held back by the *same* weakness.

> **The professor:** "A star performer, but denied promotion because of lack of interpersonal skills. And since she was a star performer, she was given this opportunity of executive coaching."

## Setting the Stage: One Case, Two Problems

Session 16 is a **case discussion** of the Harvard Business School case **"Coaching McKenna Lane,"** run through study-group presentations with the professor threading the theory. It sits right before the course's **Leadership** session, and it answers a very practical question: *when a brilliant performer can't connect with people, can coaching actually turn her into a leader — and how would you ever know it worked?*

**Meet McKenna Lane.** A visionary, data-driven marketing and strategy executive. Her backstory is pure grit — born during the **Cambodian genocide**, her parents fled to Thailand and then Australia even after being told the baby might not survive. Raised in Melbourne, first-class honours from the University of Melbourne, then **A.T. Kearney**, an **MBA from Harvard**, then **McKinsey** (consultant → associate principal in record time), and finally **365 Emporium**, a traditional Australian retail-and-pharmacy chain.

At 365 Emporium she delivered: **5% year-on-year growth** and engagement scores up **from 36% to 72%**. But she was also seen as oppressive and demanding — she interrupted people, made them feel judged and unheard, worked in a **silo**, and never built cross-functional bonds. **High technical skill, low relational intelligence.** So she was **passed over for promotion**.

**And here's the twist — who is the protagonist?** The case is *about* McKenna, but the person facing the decision is her boss, **John Mills**. After a year of coaching, Mills sees changes — but must decide whether they are real.

> **The professor:** "As Mills, you have to find out if the changes in McKenna are because of **authentic transformation** or just **superficial changes** to get the leadership position. The stakes are high — she's a star performer, and if denied promotion this time, she might leave."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The IGD case formula:** a *star performer* whose weak *interpersonal skills* block their rise — Michael Peterson, Amalia Rosas, McKenna Lane. The exam move is always the same: **high performance alone is not enough.** A manager needs a balance of **technical + decision-making + interpersonal** skills, and the "soft" one is what breaks careers at the leadership door.
:::

## Managers vs Leaders: Possibility Before Practicality

The case opens on a line McKenna lives by, and the professor used it to separate the two mindsets:

> *"I can always think about what's practical later. Let's start with what's possible."*

> **The professor:** "Leaders start with **possibility** and then move towards practicality. Managers start with **practicality** and then move towards execution and optimization."

**Why it matters:** that one sentence signals a *leadership orientation*. A manager optimises what already exists; a leader first asks what *could* exist, then makes it practical. McKenna's instinct was already leader-shaped — her gap was the *human* half, not the vision.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Possible → Practical = Leader; Practical → Polish = Manager"]
**Leaders** open with **what's possible**, then make it practical. **Managers** open with **what's practical**, then polish and execute. Same company, two different starting questions.
:::

## What Executive Coaching Actually Is

This is the heart of the session. **Executive coaching** is a *personalised* program where a trained coach works with a senior professional to grow their leadership potential. The single most important line:

> **The professor:** "Coaching is not about fixing problems. Coaching is more about **unblocking your leadership potential**."

So the coach isn't a mechanic repairing a fault — she's removing the blocks that stop a capable person from leading. The program follows a recognisable sequence:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Goal setting** | Agree what leadership potential to unlock |
| **2. Assessment & self-awareness** | Self-assessment tools (here, the **Birkman Method**) |
| **3. 360-degree feedback** | Collect honest data from peers, team and stakeholders |
| **4. Identify developmental areas** | From reflection + feedback, name the gaps and **blind spots** |
| **5. Action plan + practice** | Deliberate skill-building, practised over time |
| **6. Evaluation & closure** | Check what actually changed; close the loop |

McKenna's **blind spot**: she was so performance-driven she couldn't see *how her behaviour landed on others*. The coach surfaced it through **self-reflection** questions — *How would you describe yourself? What are your gifts? What are your challenges? What are you longing for?*

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Executive coaching = unblock leadership potential, not fix problems.** It is the practical proof of **behavioral theory** (leaders are *trained*). Learn the step chain: **goal → assessment/self-awareness → 360-degree feedback → developmental areas → action plan & practice → evaluation.** If a case gives you a talented-but-abrasive manager, *coaching to balance task and relationship* is a textbook intervention.
:::

## The Diagnostic Toolkit: Birkman and Its Stand-ins

To build self-awareness, the coach used the **Birkman Method** (a self-assessment tool, from US psychologist Roger Birkman). Its power is that it gives a **360-degree view of a person** across four lenses — not just the outside behaviour, but the inner wiring:

| Lens | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Usual behaviour** | How do I normally show up? |
| **Motivation** | What drives me? |
| **Needs** | What do I expect from others and my environment? |
| **Stress behaviour** | How do I behave when my needs are *not* met? |

It's used across **leadership, team-building, career development, conflict management, recruitment and talent optimisation** — but it's a **paid** tool. The professor shared cheaper stand-ins you can actually take:

| To measure | A more accessible tool |
|---|---|
| **Usual behaviour** | **Big Five / OCEAN** personality test |
| **Motivation & needs** | **Career Anchors Inventory** — eight anchors (technical, general-managerial, autonomy, security, entrepreneurial, service, pure challenge, lifestyle); widely credited to Edgar Schein |
| **Strengths & blind spots** | **Strengths Profile** — maps your *realised*, *unrealised*, *learned* strengths and *weaknesses* |

:::tip[Important — Why 360-Degree Feedback Matters]
Self-assessment alone is biased — remember unconscious bias from Session 1. **360-degree feedback** pulls honest data from *multiple sources* (peers, team, stakeholders), so the developmental areas are grounded in how people *actually* experience you, not how you imagine you come across. That's how McKenna's blind spot got named.
:::

## Pre-Coaching Check: The Total Leadership Lens

Before changing behaviour, the coach mapped McKenna across the **four domains of life** — a framework the professor called the **Total Leadership principle** (associated with Wharton's Stewart Friedman):

| Domain | |
|---|---|
| **Self** | Your health, mind, values |
| **Family** | Home and relationships |
| **Work** | Career and performance |
| **Community** | The wider world you belong to |

> **The professor:** "A good leader is one who can create a balance among all four domains of life. If you create that balance, you excel in all the domains."

**Why it matters:** McKenna had maxed out **Work** while starving **Self** (and the relationships that leadership runs on). Real growth meant rebalancing, not just working harder.

## How Coaching Changed McKenna: Three Pillars

After a year, McKenna's own one-pager named three areas of change — the **three pillars of effective leadership**:

1. **Self-awareness** — you genuinely know yourself.
2. **Collaboration** — you focus on others and work *with* the team.
3. **Authenticity** — your inner values and your actions are aligned.

Her shift reads like a leadership checklist. These are the moves of a **transformational** leader (who empowers and seeks input) versus a **transactional** one (reward-for-work):

| From (old McKenna) | To (coached McKenna) |
|---|---|
| Changing my *thinking* | Changing my *behaviour and outcomes* |
| Reacting | Controlling my body and emotions |
| Doing more | Realising **less is more** |
| Victim of the environment | **Stable against** the environment |
| Giving feedback | **Seeking input** from my team |
| Influencing the *answer* | Influencing *people* |
| Asking questions to prove my point | Asking questions to **understand others** |

She started investing in **referent power** (influence through being admired, not through authority — see Session 1) and accepted that **collective intelligence beats individual intelligence** — the lesson buried in the earlier desert-survival team activity. In short: *manage yourself, manage your network, manage your team.*

## The Crux: Three Practices — and the Test for "Is It Real?"

Here is the exam's centre of gravity. The coach built McKenna's change on **three practices**, and the pattern among them is exactly how Mills (and you) can tell **authentic** change from **superficial** change.

| Practice | What it looks like | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Relational practices** | Small *outward* gestures: **respond, don't react**; listen and align; informal coffee check-ins; **let others shine** (invite the team to present, acknowledge them) | Better collaboration — but could be surface-level |
| **Independent practices** | Strengthen yourself *from within*: **self-care** (sleep, nutrition, exercise) + **mindfulness/meditation** | Change coming **from within** |
| **Self-generation (sustainability)** | **Sustain the growth with no coach**: *dedication* (do it without help) + *authenticity* (walk the talk) | **Authentic, lasting** transformation |

The logic: **relational practices alone can be faked** for a promotion. But **independent practices** (change from inside) plus **self-generation** (you keep it going with no external hand) are what prove the change is real. McKenna sustained it for a **full year** and moved from purely *task-focused* to *task-and-relationship* focused.

> **The professor:** "When the change comes from within and you can **sustain it without any external support**, the change becomes **sustainable instead of temporary** — and that's our problem statement to find out."

On the relational side, the professor hammered that it needn't be dramatic:

> **The professor:** "You need not make big changes in your approach. **Small changes can make a big difference** — listening, a small informal conversation, acknowledging their contribution, seeking their inputs."

And a warning about fake listening:

> **The professor:** "Listening doesn't mean *acting* like you're listening. You need to show **care and consideration** for that particular person."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Relational · Independent · Self-generation.** Relational = small outward gestures (collaboration). **Independent = change from within; Self-generation = sustaining it alone.** Those last two are the signature of **authentic transformation** — the answer to Mills's dilemma. If an exam asks "is the change genuine or cosmetic?", point to **inner change that is self-sustained over time**, not polished behaviour under supervision.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Relational is the mask; Independent + Self-generation is the face"]
Outward gestures (**Relational**) can be put on for a promotion. Change you grow **from within** (**Independent**) and keep running **on your own** (**Self-generation**) is the real face underneath. **R** = looks real; **I + S** = *is* real.
:::

## The Student Question That Sharpened It

A student pushed the professor on the obvious worry: *how is this different from a New Year's resolution that fizzles out? How do you actually gauge, at a real workplace, that a change will last?*

The professor's honest answer: **you can't measure it in the short run.** There's no instant test. You do two things over time:

1. Run a **self-assessment test** as a baseline.
2. **Observe the person across different situations**, over a period, to see if the new behaviour holds under pressure — not just when the coach is watching.

In McKenna's case, the evidence was the **one-year** span and the fact that she rebuilt her *interpersonal style*, not just her tactics.

:::note[Good to Know — "From a Machine to a Human Being"]
One student offered the line that captures every IGD case: it's *"the transformation from a machine to a human being."* We're trained to follow SOPs and processes — a "programmed version" of ourselves — and the journey is toward **empathy, relationship-building, and reading how others think about us.** A closing presenter put it well: real change *"starts when you finally believe in your own capacity."*
:::

## The Quiet Theme: Self-Care Is a Leadership Skill

The professor kept returning to one underrated point — **focus on self**. Leaders ignore their own health and pay for it in judgement and temper.

> **The professor:** "A sound body supports a **resilient mind**, promotes clarity of thoughts, and helps you control your impulses. It enhances your emotional intelligence — and that increases your **leadership presence**."

So the full leader equation here is **technical + interpersonal + decision-making skills — *plus* self-care**. Mindfulness is the tool that turns self-care into behaviour: it helps you **respond, not react**, by letting you watch an emotion as an observer before you act on it.

:::tip[Important — Self-Care Is Not Indulgence]
Chasing performance non-stop leads to **burnout** and bad decisions. Managing your body and emotions *shows up in your work*. In an answer on leadership effectiveness, name **self-care and mindfulness** as drivers of EQ and presence — examiners rarely expect it, and it signals depth.
:::

## The Decision: Promote Her or Not?

The groups weighed Mills's call. The balance sheet is a clean exam table in itself:

| Promote her (pros) | Hold back (cons) |
|---|---|
| Proven **performance & continuity** — crucial as mentor Fernandez exits | Is the change **authentic or temporary**? |
| **Demonstrated coachability** — a year-long program, better listening, stakeholder outreach, calmer tone | Promotion **pressure** could revert her to abrasive habits; self-awareness is *recent* |
| **High retention risk** — rivals are poaching; losing her hurts momentum and discourages other performers | **Loss of political cover** — Fernandez, who mediated her conflicts, is leaving; any misstep now isolates her |

One group's pragmatic answer: **promote, but with conditions** — hold her to the one-pager, run a **6-month peer-based performance review**, appoint a **mentor**, and bake **collaboration and team skills into her KPIs**. Their logic: *"Leadership is not about delivering in a day or a month — it's about long-term consistent performance."* That matches the professor's own test: change is real only when it **sustains**.
