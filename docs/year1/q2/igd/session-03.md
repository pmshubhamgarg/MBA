# Session 3: Ten Percent Visible, Ninety Percent Hidden

*You can watch exactly how a colleague acts, but never see why. This session dives below the waterline — into the VABs and attitudes that quietly steer behaviour — and ends with the four moves every one of us makes when we're fed up at work.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **behaviour iceberg** (10% visible, 90% invisible **P factors**); the behaviour equation **Nature + Nurture + Memes**; **VABs** (Values, Assumptions, Beliefs, Expectations) and the **core vs peripheral** split with its manager implication; **attitude vs behaviour** (the invisible drives the visible); the **ABC model** of attitudes; the **EVLN typology** (Exit · Voice · Loyalty · Neglect) on the **active/passive × constructive/destructive** grid.

**Important (supporting):** Inherited / mutated / uniquely-developed VABs; the job-attitudes vocabulary (satisfaction, involvement, commitment, engagement); turnover intention vs turnover and creativity vs innovative work behaviour; EVLN responses as **combinations and sequences**; the two practical uses of EVLN (know your own style; spot early disengagement).

**Context (colour for answers):** the "don't-trim-your-nails" habit, veg-to-eggetarian mutation, "look at me — am I satisfied?", the professor's own high-agreeableness loyalty, Raab escalating feedback three levels, quiet quitting, HR engagement surveys and social-desirability bias.
:::

## Quick Recap

The professor opened by tying today back to last class. Session 2 was about **human behaviour** — the way we act and react — and the big idea that behaviour comes from a **complex interplay of nature and nurture**. We also separated **behaviour in general** from **behaviour in organisations** (task performance, voice, citizenship behaviour, and the darker counterproductive behaviours like absenteeism, time theft and turnover).

The anchor to carry forward is **Kurt Lewin's equation**: Behaviour = f(Person, Environment). Your **P factors** (personal) meet the **E factors** (organisational culture, climate, leadership, power, politics) and out comes behaviour. That is why two employees under the *same* boss behave differently — their P factors differ.

> **The professor:** "Given the environment is same, leadership is same, two individuals will behave in different ways because of difference in their P factor — their personality, motivations, emotions, biases."

---

## The Iceberg: You Only Ever See 10% of a Person

Here is the organising image for the whole pre-midterm course. An iceberg shows only about **10% above the surface**; the other **90% sits below**, unseen. People are the same. **Behaviour is the visible 10%.** The invisible 90% underneath is your bundle of **P factors** — the hidden drivers.

Those P factors are: **VABs, attitudes, personality, perceptions and biases, motivation, and emotions.** The entire pre-midterm block drills into them for one simple reason — **self-awareness**. Understand yourself, and you understand your colleagues and your organisation, and only then do you manage well.

> **The professor:** "Only 10% of the iceberg is visible; the remaining 90% is below the sea level, we cannot see it. The same applies to human beings — behaviour is the top of the iceberg, driven by several invisible components."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Behaviour is observable; its drivers are not.** Just by looking at a colleague you cannot read their personality, values or motivation (emotions leak a little — we wear them on our face). The exam-ready framing: the visible 10% (behaviour) is produced by the invisible 90% (P factors), and the course exists to make that 90% visible to you.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "10 up, 90 down"]
**10% behaviour you can see, 90% P factors you can't.** Picture the iceberg before every OB answer: whatever someone *does* is only the tip of what's driving them.
:::

## The Behaviour Equation Gains a Third Term: Memes

Last class said Behaviour = **Nature + Nurture**. Today the professor adds one more: **Memes** (memetic endowments). Memes are the **mental analogues of biological genes** — ideas and beliefs passed from one generation to the next: traditions, habits, social norms.

So the updated line is **Behaviour = Nature + Nurture + Memes**. Touching elders' feet, removing footwear at a temple — these are transmitted ideas, not instincts. And these memes are exactly what **constitute our VABs**.

---

## VABs: The Rulebook You Never Knew You Were Following

**VABs = Values, Assumptions, Beliefs, Expectations.** They are the **judgment filters** about how we should behave and how others should behave, and they quietly drive day-to-day action. The catch: they mostly run at a **semiconscious or unconscious level**. We each carry **thousands** of them and rarely notice.

Where do they come from? Parents, family, upbringing, the people around us, culture, and our own life and work experience. Everyday examples the professor rattled off: respecting elders, never wasting food, waking early, "professionals should wear a suit and tie," and even **stereotypes** ("boys who cry are weak," "girls are bad at maths") — overly generalised beliefs that may not be true but still get passed down.

> **The professor:** "Don't trim your nails on Monday, Tuesday and Saturday — do you ever wonder why? No. Your parents taught you. That is a VAB."

:::note[Good to Know]
The professor was candid that **VABs have limited practical implications** compared with personality, emotions and motivation — which are the heavy hitters among the P factors. VABs earn only a **basic understanding** here because they are genuinely part of your personal make-up, not because they'll dominate the exam.
:::

---

## Two Ways to Slice a VAB

A VAB can be classified **by origin** and **by strength** — and the strength cut is the one that matters for managers.

**By origin:**

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Inherited** | Learned from parents and never changed |
| **Mutated** | Learned from parents but changed by your own experience |
| **Uniquely developed** | Built fresh from your own life or work experience |

**By strength:**

| Type | What it is | Can it change? |
|---|---|---|
| **Core VAB** | Deep, rigid, your central way of living | No — never |
| **Peripheral VAB** | Weaker, situational | Yes — with experience |

A clean class example of **mutation**: you start with "people should not be greedy," join an organisation where greed is rewarded, and your VAB softens to "people can be greedy depending on the situation." Because it *changed*, it was **peripheral**. If you held your ground and refused to change, it was a **core** VAB. The professor's own example: taught that "people who drink are bad," then learning through experience that drinking has nothing to do with character — a VAB that mutated.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The manager's payoff from core vs peripheral.** If an employee resists a change and that resistance sits on a **core VAB**, the person will not budge — better to **change the team**. If it sits on a **peripheral VAB**, you can **influence, motivate and shift** them. Diagnosing which kind of VAB a behaviour rests on tells you whether a person is movable.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Core = concrete, Peripheral = putty"]
**Core** VABs are set in concrete (unwavering faith, lifelong principles). **Peripheral** VABs are putty (food habits, situational rules) — they reshape with experience.
:::

## From VABs to Attitudes: The Second Invisible Driver

An **attitude** is an **evaluative statement** — your mind's predisposition toward a job, a person or a thing. "I'm not satisfied with my job," "I don't like Sakshi," "I feel my supervisor is unfair" — all attitudes. Like VABs, they are **invisible**, and they **drive the visible behaviour**.

The professor made it physical: *look at me.* If she's excited and happy, that's **behaviour** you can see — but you cannot tell *from looking* whether she is satisfied, committed or engaged. Those are the hidden evaluations underneath.

> **The professor:** "Just by looking at me, can you tell whether I'm satisfied or dissatisfied with my job? No — because that's the invisible component."

### Attitude vs Behaviour — don't mix them up

Two pairs make the distinction exam-ready:

- **Turnover intention** (attitude — the *thought* "I will leave") vs **turnover** (behaviour — you *actually* leave, and others see it).
- **Creativity** (attitude — generating ideas inside your head) vs **innovative work behaviour** (behaviour — you *share and implement* those ideas). Creativity is just the first stage of innovative work behaviour.

:::tip[Important]
The rule of thumb: **what you think, feel, or intend about your job = attitude; how you act = behaviour.** The invisible attitude is the cause; the visible behaviour is the effect. If a term describes something going on *inside the head*, it's an attitude; if others can *observe* it, it's behaviour.
:::

---

## The ABC Model of Attitudes

Every attitude has three parts — remember **C-A-B: Cognitive, Affective, Behavioural** (head, heart, hand). The professor used an **abusive supervisor** to show all three:

| Component | Seat | In the abusive-supervisor example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | Head — what you *think* | "My supervisor is not fair." |
| **Affective** | Heart — what you *feel* | "I dislike my supervisor." |
| **Behavioural** | Hand — what you *do / intend* | "I may not build a relationship with him — or I may quit." |

### The job-attitudes vocabulary

These are the attitude terms that show up constantly in organisations — all invisible, all drivers of behaviour:

| Attitude | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Job satisfaction** | A positive feeling about your job |
| **Job involvement** | How actively you throw yourself into the work |
| **Organisational commitment** | How strongly you identify with and want to stay in the org |
| **Perceived organisational support** | Belief that the org values your contribution and cares about you |
| **Employee engagement** | A positive, fulfilling state — **vigour, absorption, dedication** |
| **Psychological empowerment** | Feeling empowered in your role |
| **Creativity** | Generating ideas (attitude — not yet behaviour) |
| **Turnover intention** | The intention to leave (not the leaving itself) |

## EVLN: The Four Things Everyone Does When Fed Up

This is the centrepiece of the session. Pick one attitude — **job dissatisfaction** — and ask: *what do people actually do about it?* The professor crowd-sourced the class's real responses (job-hunting, one-to-ones with the boss, quiet quitting, confronting, ranting at home, disengaging) and mapped them onto the **EVLN typology** (**Albert Hirschman, 1970**).

Four responses, placed on two axes — **active vs passive** and **constructive vs destructive**:

| | **Constructive** (helps the org) | **Destructive** (hurts the org) |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** (you do something) | **Voice** — raise concerns to fix the situation (a one-to-one with your boss, escalating feedback) | **Exit** — leave the job, team or boss |
| **Passive** (you sit tight) | **Loyalty** — give 100%, patiently wait, trust management to fix it | **Neglect** — ignore work, do the bare minimum, deviant behaviours |

- **Exit** is **destructive** because turnover is costly — the firm spent money hiring and training you.
- **Voice** is constructive — you're trying to *improve* things.
- **Loyalty** means you keep performing fully but **wait optimistically**, saying nothing, trusting the system.
- **Neglect** is passive sabotage: coming late, longer breaks, daydreaming, quiet quitting — and the professor swept broader damage (grapevine rumours, influencing others to quit, even sabotage/corruption) into this corner too.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**EVLN = Exit · Voice · Loyalty · Neglect, on active/passive × constructive/destructive.** Voice = active-constructive, Exit = active-destructive, Loyalty = passive-constructive, Neglect = passive-destructive. Being able to drop a case's dissatisfied employee into the right quadrant — and justify it on both axes — is a high-value exam move.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Loud or quiet × help or harm"]
**Voice** = loud + helpful. **Exit** = loud + harmful. **Loyalty** = quiet + helpful. **Neglect** = quiet + harmful. Two ways to *act* (Voice/Exit), two ways to *sit* (Loyalty/Neglect); left column helps the firm, right column hurts it.
:::

### They're not one-and-done — people run sequences

Nobody is locked into a single box. The responses combine and unfold over time: **Voice → Neglect** (spoke up, ignored, so switched off), **Voice → Loyalty** (spoke up, then waited), **Loyalty → Exit** (waited, then left). Most people *start with Voice* — it's the natural first move — but when the organisation ignores or mislabels it as "challenging authority," dissatisfaction grows and they slide to another response.

> **The professor:** "Most of us, the first step is Voice — we want to improve our situation. But since organisations don't give due consideration to our voices, we move to other responses. That's why employees leave, and that's why some engage in deviant behaviours."

### Why EVLN is useful to a manager

1. **Know your own default.** Your response style is tied to personality — the professor admitted she's **high on agreeableness**, so she almost always chooses **Loyalty**: give 100%, stay positive, let management sort it out.
2. **Catch disengagement early.** Repeated **Neglect** is a red flag that someone is disengaged and will drag down team productivity; a reliable **loyal** performer who suddenly exits or starts neglecting is signalling hidden dissatisfaction you must investigate.

How do you read these signals as a manager? A **combination** of methods — self-assessment tests, one-to-one reflective conversations, and plain observation over time.

:::note[Good to Know]
This is also why HR runs **engagement surveys** — to capture attitudes. But beware **social-desirability bias**: teams quietly tell each other to "write good things," so survey data can be rosier than reality. Also don't confuse EVLN with the **Thomas-Kilmann** conflict-style model (competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating) — that's about *conflict*, covered later in the conflict-and-negotiation session; EVLN is specifically about *dissatisfaction*.
:::

## A Real Class Story: Raab Goes Three Levels Up

One student, Raab, made EVLN concrete. One year into a new firm, he gave honest feedback in his mid-year review — **Voice** — to his manager, then repeated it to the manager's manager (visiting from Singapore), then to HR in a 90-minute Friday meeting. He flagged that **seven of ten teammates had left in a year** and said he wanted to stay if his skills could be re-aligned. HR asked him not to act in haste and noted that earlier leavers had said the same.

His verdict on himself: he had **voiced**, and was now in the **loyalty phase** — waiting, still performing, partly because LinkedIn posts of colleagues hitting 5- and 10-year anniversaries made him believe the firm does right by its people. The professor's label: **Voice → Loyalty**, with Exit or Neglect as the possible next step. That single story walks the whole typology.
