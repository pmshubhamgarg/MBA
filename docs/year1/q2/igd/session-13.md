# Session 13: Smart People, Failing Teams — the Three Tests That Explain Why

*A team of brilliant individuals can still lose. This session hands you three validated instruments — Belbin, FIRO and the Five Dysfunctions — one for each "C" of team effectiveness, so you can diagnose exactly where a team breaks and fix it.*

## Quick Recap: Why Competent Teams Still Fail

Last class ended on a puzzle: put together a team where **every member is highly competent** — will the team automatically perform? The professor's answer is **no**, and Session 13 is built to explain why and what to do about it.

The spine is the **Four C's of team effectiveness** — the levers a manager pulls to build a working team:

| The "C" | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Competence** | Members have the technical knowledge to do the job |
| **Compatibility** | Members can actually work *together* |
| **Capabilities** | Alignment between **team roles** and each person's **individual capabilities** |
| **Cohesion** | Built on **trust and commitment** — members trust each other, feel committed and accountable |

Competence you can hire for. The other three you have to *diagnose* — and that is what today's three instruments do, one per C:

| Instrument | External scholar | The "C" it measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Belbin Team Role Inventory** | Meredith Belbin | **Capabilities** (right person, right job) |
| **FIRO** | William Schutz | **Compatibility** |
| **Five Dysfunctions of a Team** | Patrick Lencioni | **Cohesion** (trust) |

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Competent people, Compatible roles, Capable fit, Cohesive trust"]
Four C's: **Competence → Compatibility → Capabilities → Cohesion.** Competence gets people in the room; the other three decide whether the room works. Each has its own test.
:::

## First, a Word on These Tests (Psychometric Hygiene)

Belbin, FIRO, MBTI and Big Five are all **psychometric tests** — self-assessment tools that measure how you think, feel and behave in a **reliable, scientific** way. Before taking any of them, the professor set three rules, and they are quietly exam-relevant.

1. **Be authentic.** Give honest responses, because these tests carry **in-built consistency checks** — rephrased or **reverse-coded items** (same idea, opposite wording). If you *strongly agree* with both "I enjoy being the centre of attention" **and** "I prefer to stay in the background," the test flags you as inconsistent.
2. **Don't overthink.** Give your first natural response; these capture **natural tendencies**, not right or wrong answers.
3. **Answer honestly, not ideally** — how you *usually* behave, not how you think you *should*.

> **The professor:** "Answer honestly, not ideally — that is, how you usually behave, not how you should behave in an ideal scenario."

:::note[Good to Know — Why the Repeated Questions]
Students complained that FIRO felt "extremely repetitive." That repetition is deliberate: reverse-coded and rephrased items are the test's lie-detector. If your answers contradict each other, the scoring catches it. So the boring repetition is a feature, not sloppy design.
:::

## Instrument 1 — Belbin Team Roles: Right Person, Right Job (Capabilities)

**Concept.** Professor **Meredith Belbin** spent **nine years** watching real teams and found that people naturally slip into distinct **roles**. A team wins not when everyone is identical, but when it has a **balance** of role types. He published this in *Management Teams: Why They Succeed or Fail*, rated among the top 50 management books of all time.

> **The professor:** "When teams have a balance of thinkers, doers and supporters, they achieve more than what they are doing alone."

Belbin sorts **nine roles** into **three families**:

| Family | What it contributes | The three roles |
|---|---|---|
| **Thought-oriented** | Generate ideas, solve problems — creativity, analysis, technical expertise | **Plant · Monitor Evaluator · Specialist** |
| **Action-oriented** | Get things done, drive progress — execution, discipline, results | **Shaper · Implementer · Complete Finisher** |
| **People-oriented** | Build relationships, foster collaboration — cohesion, communication, conflict resolution | **Coordinator · Teamworker · Resource Investigator** |

You need not memorise all nine. Remember the **three families** and that each holds three roles. A person may play **one, two or many** roles, roles can **shift over time**, and a team does **not** need nine separate people.

**Class example — spot the role.** The professor ran a quick quiz:

- A student who **divides tasks and ensures on-time submission** → **action-oriented** (execution, results).
- A student who **ensures quieter classmates contribute** → **people-oriented** (collaboration).
- A student who **brings a new framework or analytical insight** → **thought-oriented** (ideas).

**Why it matters.** Belbin maps to the **Capabilities** C — the fit between a role and a person.

- **For the manager:** put the **right person in the right job** and build a balanced team.
- **For the employee:** learn your strengths and weaknesses, and develop the families you score low on.

A live example surfaced when a student, **Chaitanya**, scored high on **thought + action** roles but works in **HR** (a people-heavy function). The professor named it a **misalignment** between his preferred roles and his current role — and such misalignment "can hamper team effectiveness."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Belbin = balance, not clones.** Nine roles in three families — **thought-oriented, action-oriented, people-oriented**. A high-performing team blends all three. In a case, if a team is "all doers and no thinkers" (or all ideas and no finishers), name the **missing Belbin family** and the **role–capability misalignment** as the problem.
:::

## Instrument 2 — FIRO: The Submarine That Cracked Compatibility

**The origin story (worth telling in an answer).** In **1952**, the **US Navy** staffed a critical project with its *most competent* officers — and the team underperformed. They called in Professor **William Schutz** (a Harvard psychologist) to answer one question: how can a team of competent people actually work together? After **six to seven years** of study, he concluded that **competence alone is not enough**.

> **The professor:** "Competence alone cannot promote productivity. Productivity requires both competence — technical knowledge — as well as compatibility, so people can work together."

His formula and finding:

- **Team productivity = Competence + Compatibility.** Balancing the two can lift team performance by **50% to 75%**.
- The **CP effect:** **Compatibility is directly related to Productivity**, and this effect **grows under pressure.** (On the Navy submarine — cramped, high-stress, life-and-death — the FIRO tool improved combat-team effectiveness by about **50%**.)

He published it in 1958 as **FIRO** — **Fundamental Interpersonal Relations Orientation**.

**Concept.** FIRO says that in any team we carry three **interpersonal needs**, and each need has **two sides** — what we **show** others and what we **want** from others. That makes a **six-cell model**:

| Need | What it is | **Expressed** (you → others) | **Wanted** (others → you) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inclusion** | Belonging, membership — *surface level* | You include others in the team | You want others to include you |
| **Control** | Influence, decision authority, structure — *power* | You want to influence / lead | You want others to take charge |
| **Affection** | Closeness, emotional bond, trust — *deep level* | You show warmth to teammates | You want warmth shown to you |

**How compatibility actually happens.** It comes from the **match (or mismatch) between expressed and wanted** across teammates:

- Member A **high Expressed Control** + Member B **high Wanted Control** → **perfect fit** (one wants to lead, the other wants to be led).
- A **high Expressed Control** + B **high Expressed Control** → **conflict** (both fight to lead).
- Both **high Wanted Control** → nobody will take charge → also breaks compatibility.

**Reading the scores.** Each cell scores **0–9**:

| Score (0–9) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **0–2** | Low — you rarely show this |
| **3–6** | Medium — some of the time |
| **7–9** | High — your **typical** behaviour |

Crucially, there is **no "good" or "bad" score**. A student asked whether a high control need was a bad trait. The professor pushed back:

> **The professor:** "There's nothing good or bad — it simply states that control is your most dominant need when you work in teams."

:::tip[Important — Give People a Language, Not a Label]
FIRO's real payoff is a shared vocabulary for behaviour. Without it, teammates jump to conclusions — *"He's a micromanager," "She's not a team player."* Once you can say "I'm high **Expressed Control**, you're high **Wanted Control**," the friction has a name and a fix. That's why FIRO is used for **team building, conflict resolution, leadership development and performance improvement.**
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "I-C-A, Express or Want"]
FIRO = **I**nclusion, **C**ontrol, **A**ffection — each either **Express**ed (you give it) or **Want**ed (you crave it). Inclusion is *membership*, Control is *power*, Affection is *closeness*. Compatibility = when my **Wanted** matches your **Expressed**.
:::

## Instrument 3 — The Five Dysfunctions: It All Starts With Trust

**Concept.** **Patrick Lencioni's** *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* stacks five failures into a pyramid. The genius of the model: they are a **chain** — knock out the **base (trust)** and the whole stack collapses in your favour.

| Level | Dysfunction | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **1 (base)** | **Absence of trust** | Members **hide mistakes and weaknesses**, won't ask for help |
| **2** | **Fear of conflict** | **Artificial harmony** — no open, constructive debate |
| **3** | **Lack of commitment** | Ambiguity and no clarity → no buy-in to decisions |
| **4** | **Avoidance of accountability** | Members don't hold each other to standards |
| **5 (top)** | **Inattention to results** | Personal goals beat collective goals → performance drops |

The read-up the chain: no trust → no honest conflict → only fake agreement → no real commitment → no accountability → everyone chases personal goals and **team performance declines**.

**The key distinction — vulnerability-based trust.** This is *not* "I trust you to deliver." It is trust that it is **safe to be weak**.

> **The professor:** "Vulnerability-based trust is not 'I trust you to do this job.' It's a leader saying 'I trust you enough to admit your mistakes, your weaknesses, and ask for help.'"

**How a leader builds it** (exam-ready list):

1. **Be the role model** — the leader shows vulnerability **first**: openly admits mistakes and limitations, asks for input.
2. **Promote psychological safety** — a culture where people can speak up and admit mistakes **without being judged**, penalised, or having it used against them later.
3. **Shared ownership** — treat success and failure as **collective**, with equity and fairness.
4. **Anonymous feedback mechanisms** — so concerns surface safely.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The Five Dysfunctions is a chain built on trust.** Absence of **trust** → fear of **conflict** → lack of **commitment** → avoidance of **accountability** → inattention to **results**. Fix the **base (vulnerability-based trust)** and everything above it heals. In a case about a dysfunctional team, start your diagnosis at **trust**, and prescribe **psychological safety** plus leaders modelling vulnerability.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Trust → Conflict → Commit → Account → Results"]
Five dysfunctions = five words, bottom to top: **Trust, Conflict, Commitment, Accountability, Results.** Lose trust at the base and each one topples the next.
:::

## The Desert Survival Activity — Is the Team Smarter Than Its Best Member?

To close, the professor interpreted the **desert-survival** exercise, where each person ranks survival items, the team re-ranks them together, and both are compared to an **expert key**. The gap from the expert key is the **error score** — **lower is better.**

From four numbers (team error, average individual error, worst performer, best performer) you get three verdicts:

| Metric | Team compared against… | Positive result means |
|---|---|---|
| **Team functioning** | the **weakest** member | Team beats its weakest member |
| **Team effectiveness** | the **average** member | Team beats the average individual |
| **Team synergy** | the **best** member | Team beats even its **best** individual |

**Why it matters — the whole point of teams:**

> **The professor:** "Collective intelligence is better than individual intelligence. Individuals perform well when they work in teams, compared to when they work alone."

This holds **especially in uncertain environments** — time pressure, resource constraints, ambiguity.

**But teams sometimes lose — and why.** A student's team actually did *worse* than its best member. The professor diagnosed the classic culprit: **groupthink.** The best performer "initially tried to convince others but ended up getting convinced" — the team **pulled back its strongest member** just to keep the peace.

> **The professor:** "Most of the time, to maintain harmony, we just agree with others — that's groupthink, and groupthink often results in faulty decisions."

(Other suspects she named for weak team scores: poor communication, weak conflict-resolution skills, and failure to influence teammates — plausibly worsened by working in **virtual teams** under a **time crunch**.)

:::tip[Important — Synergy Is the Prize, Groupthink Is the Thief]
A truly effective team shows **synergy** — it outperforms even its best individual. The enemy of synergy is **groupthink**: caving to harmony so the smartest voice goes quiet. The fix is the ability to **influence** your group and the courage to hold **constructive conflict**.
:::

## From the Student Presentation: Making Team Decisions Sharp

A student group presented on fast, high-quality team decisions — useful, exam-friendly add-ons that reinforce the day's themes:

- **Set decision rules early** — consensus, vote, or leader's call — and **write it down before the debate**. "Process beats panic."
- **Fight the common-knowledge effect** — teams keep repeating what *everyone already knows*; use a **round-robin** so each person adds **one unique insight**.
- **Create psychological safety** — make it safe to say "I disagree" or "I'm unsure"; leaders **go first** with their own uncertainty.
- **Prevent groupthink** — rotate a **devil's advocate**, invite outsiders for fresh eyes, and run a **pre-mortem** ("it's six months later and the decision failed — why?").
- **Tie decisions to action** — one **owner per action**, because "shared ownership is usually no ownership," and "a decision without follow-through is just a polite conversation."
- **Diversity makes teams smarter** — it "turns blind spots into insights," but only when people feel **safe** to share; for multicultural friction, use **cultural intelligence** (head to understand, body to adapt, heart to stay motivated).

:::note[Good to Know — These Are Student-Presented, Not the Professor's Core Model]
The decision-making checklist, the common-knowledge effect, pre-mortem and cultural-intelligence points came from a **student group presentation**, not the professor's framework slides. They're excellent supporting material — just attribute them as the group's work, and keep **Belbin, FIRO and the Five Dysfunctions** as the session's examinable spine.
:::
