# Session 1: Same Office, Two Very Different People

*Why do two people with the same desk, the same boss and the same targets end up so differently — one thriving, one quietly checking out? This course says the answer is invisible. It lives in personality, values, biases, motivation and emotions — and learning to read them is the real job of a manager.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The thesis — **no two people are the same**, so **one-size-fits-all doesn't work with humans**; the **P-factors** (personal factors) that make us unique; the course's **three-fold purpose** (understand **Self → Others → Organization**); **intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation**; **IQ vs EQ** (the 20/80 idea); **perception is not reality** (unconscious bias); what a **theory** does (goal-setting, Herzberg two-factor); the **case-solving steps**.

**Important (supporting):** Big Five/OCEAN and MBTI as personality frameworks; nature vs nurture; the limits of carrot-and-stick; EQ as a trainable, lifelong skill; leadership styles (transformational, authentic, ethical, servant, autocratic) and abusive supervision; political skill vs organizational politics; referent and expert power; competence vs compatibility in teams; functional vs dysfunctional conflict.

**Context (colour for answers):** Nadella/Gates/Pichai/Musk MBTI codes; Ratan Tata, Naval Ravikant, Anand Mahindra, Mary Barra, Steve Jobs; the 1979 written-goals study; quiet quitting; the unconscious-bias office video; the Michael Peterson case coming next.

## Setting the Stage: What IGD Is Really About

Your instructor for **Individual and Group Dynamics (IGD)** is **the professor** — an organizational-behaviour faculty member. This first class is purely introductory, delivered over Zoom to a cohort of working professionals (an EMBA batch of around 92 students in 19 study groups). Nothing is graded today; the aim is to show *why* a "soft" subject like IGD sits at the centre of management education.

She opens not with theory but with a question:

> **The professor:** "Have you ever wondered why two individuals behave differently in the same situation? Or why some individuals thrive whereas others struggle in the same work environment?"

The entire course is her answer. People differ because each of us is **unique**, and that uniqueness comes from our personal factors — what she calls **P-factors**. Everything in IGD is built to help you read those factors, starting with yourself.

### The Course in One Shape

The professor walked through the full syllabus. It splits cleanly into two halves:

| Phase | What the sessions explore | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-midterm — understanding SELF (your P-factors)** | The manager's job; human behaviour (Michael Peterson case); values & beliefs (VABs); attitudes; **personality ×2** (Big Five/OCEAN + MBTI); **unconscious biases ×2** (self-perception & perception of others); **motivation ×2** | *Who am I? What is my personality, what are my biases, what drives me?* |
| **Post-midterm — understanding OTHERS & the ORGANIZATION** | Emotions & emotional intelligence; **group & team dynamics ×2**; conflict management & negotiation; power, politics & political skill; role transition (manager → leader); leadership & human differences | *How do I work with, motivate and lead other people inside a real organization?* |

There are **20 sessions** in all — 18 teaching sessions (with roughly 8–9 case discussions) and the last two kept for project presentations.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Me → We → The Company"]
The whole course is a three-step zoom-out: **Self → Others → Organization.** First understand yourself (personality, biases, motivation, emotions), then your colleagues, then the organization you all sit inside. Get those three right and you become an effective manager or leader.
:::

**On assessment (good to note):** IGD has **no midterm**. The grade comes from **class participation (20%)**, **group presentations** (one presentation per study group, starting Session 3), a **group project on personality (40%)**, and an **end-term (20%)**. The personality project runs in three stages and is explained only after the two personality sessions.

## Big Idea 1: Everybody Is Unique — So One Size Never Fits All

The spine of the whole subject is a single, deceptively simple claim: **no two individuals are the same.** You carry a bundle of personal factors — personality, values and beliefs, attitudes, biases, motivation, emotions — and no one else has the exact same bundle.

That is why the same workplace produces thrivers and strugglers. The desk is identical; the person is not.

> **The professor:** "When it comes to human beings, a one-size-fits-all approach is not applicable — because everybody is unique, and that uniqueness comes from your personal factors: your personality, your motivation, your emotional states, your biases."

**Why it matters:** a manager who treats all employees the same is managing a fiction. The practical skill IGD teaches is the opposite — read the individual first, then choose how to lead, motivate or give feedback.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**People are not interchangeable, so one-size-fits-all fails with humans.** The reason is the **P-factors** (personal factors) — personality, VABs, attitudes, biases, motivation and emotions — that make each person unique. If a question asks why two employees react differently to the same policy, boss or task, your first move is: *because their personal factors differ.*
:::

## Big Idea 2: Personality — 30 Traits and 16 Types

**Personality** is the professor's shorthand for "the set of characteristics that make you a unique person." Where does it come from? From **nature and nurture together** — some of it inherited from your parents (genes), some learned through life experience.

> **The professor:** "Are you an exact replica of your parents? No. You carry some of their genes, but certain things you learn through your experiences — and nature and nurture combine to shape who you are."

IGD will teach two widely used frameworks to actually *measure* personality:

| Framework | Structure | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| **Big Five / OCEAN** (Five-Factor Model) | 5 traits, each with 6 sub-traits = **30 traits** | A profile across five dimensions |
| **MBTI** (Myers–Briggs Type Indicator) | 4 pairs of psychological preferences → **16 types** | A **four-letter code** |

A striking point: these types show up **across ages and cultures**. All 7 billion people share the same 16 MBTI types — just **in varying degrees**. That paradox (same types, still unique) is the point.

**The class example — leaders by MBTI:** the professor put up codes for famous CEOs — **Satya Nadella (ENFJ)**, **Bill Gates (INTJ)**, **Sundar Pichai (INTJ)** and **Elon Musk (INTJ)**. The common thread students spotted was **N + J** — **Intuition** (seeing the bigger picture, reading between the lines) plus **Judging** (a conscientious, organized worker).

One trait she illustrated directly was **conscientiousness**: high-conscientiousness people are hardworking, duty-oriented, punctual and dependable; low-conscientiousness people are easily distracted and loose with rules. You later use this to decide *how* to motivate someone.

> **The professor:** "Seven billion people share these 16 personality types, but in varying degrees — and that's why everybody is unique."

:::note[Good to Know — The Introvert Myth]
A student assumed the INTJ leaders must be shy. The professor pushed back hard: **introversion is not shyness or weak communication.** Introverts simply prefer smaller friend circles, prefer solitude, and prefer to observe rather than be the centre of attention. Bill Gates and Sundar Pichai are considered introverts and run giant firms. *"It's a myth that introverts can't be good leaders."*
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "N + J at the top"]
The business leaders on the slide shared **iN**tuition + **J**udging — **big-picture thinkers who are also conscientious finishers.** When you take your own MBTI test in the personality sessions, you'll compare your four-letter code against theirs.
:::

## Big Idea 3: Motivation — Why Carrot-and-Stick Isn't Enough

**Motivation** is the drive — "a state of positive energy that keeps you going." It comes in two flavours: **intrinsic** (from within — the work itself feels meaningful and engaging) and **extrinsic** (from outside — rewards to chase or punishments to avoid).

Most managers reach for the **carrot and stick**: work hard, get rewarded; slack off, get punished. The professor's warning is that this usually *under*-delivers, because people don't all want the same thing.

> **The professor:** "Money, no doubt, is the biggest motivator — but money alone cannot motivate you. You'll be motivated for a short duration, but money alone cannot motivate you."

People also want **meaningful work, autonomy, a sense of ownership, recognition, growth, belongingness, fairness** — and the **absence of micromanagement**. Pay someone well but micromanage them or steal credit for their work, and the motivation collapses.

**Why it matters:** motivation is the **essence of leadership**. A leader is simply someone who can motivate and influence others toward a goal — so you can't lead until you understand what actually drives each person.

:::tip[Important — Extrinsic Is Easy, Intrinsic Is the Skill]
Extrinsically motivating people (bonus, threat) is cheap and quick. **Intrinsically** motivating them — making the work itself engaging — is harder but produces "greater engagement and long-lasting impact." A good manager **balances both**. IGD's two motivation sessions hand you the theories to do the hard half.
:::

## Big Idea 4: IQ vs EQ — The 20/80 Rule

Everyone knows **IQ** (your cognitive, intelligence quotient). IGD's interest is **EQ**, the **emotional quotient** — and the professor quotes a striking split.

> **The professor:** "If research is to be believed, only 20% of your success is because of intelligence quotient; 80% of your success is because of emotional quotient — and that's a key leadership skill."

**What EQ contains** has a *self* half and an *others* half: understand and **regulate your own emotions** (behave reliably across situations) **and** read others' emotions, **show empathy**, build good workplace relationships — which then makes it easy to influence and motivate people.

The best part: EQ is a **skill**, so it can be developed. The catch:

> **The professor:** "Like physical fitness, it's a lifelong practice. You cannot be emotionally intelligent in a week or a month's time."

The external authority she cited here is **Professor Daniel Goleman**, who has written extensively on emotional intelligence. (He is a scholar referenced in class, not the lecturer.)

:::note[Good to Know — Where Empathy Comes From]
The professor pointed to **Satya Nadella** and **Indra Nooyi** as highly emotionally intelligent leaders. Nadella has spoken openly about developing deep empathy through raising his son, who was born with cerebral palsy. It's a reminder that EQ is often forged by lived experience, not taught in a classroom.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "20 gets you in, 80 gets you up"]
Roughly **20% IQ, 80% EQ.** Intelligence gets you the job; emotional intelligence is what carries you up into leadership. You'll take an EQ test later to see where you stand and how to improve.
:::

## Big Idea 5: The Human Side of Leadership

IGD looks at leadership **only as a human act** — not its strategic, operational, financial or political angles. The working definition:

> **The professor:** "Leadership is the process of influencing or motivating others to achieve the organizational goals."

Good leaders balance two pulls — **performance** (task) and **people** (relationship):

> **The professor:** "Without performance, organizations cannot survive. But at the same time, you have to keep your employees motivated."

The class named leaders they admire, and the professor mapped each to a style — a preview of the leadership session:

| Style | In one line | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformational** | Inspires change and a bigger vision | Anand Mahindra |
| **Authentic** | Leads from a genuine self and values | Satya Nadella, Anand Mahindra |
| **Ethical** | Won't bribe, won't play politics; employee welfare first | The Tata group |
| **Servant** | Serves people first | Gandhi, Mandela, Mother Teresa (and later-stage Bill Gates) |
| **Charismatic** | Pulls people through sheer magnetism | Narendra Modi (an out-of-context example) |
| **Autocratic** | Controls every decision | Steve Jobs — *"Apple is my brainchild, so I'll get involved in everything"* |
| **Abusive supervision** (destructive) | Micromanage, overload, steal credit | Drives most resignations |

Two ideas worth banking. First, **leadership is situation-specific** — "a good leader keeps changing his style to meet the situation." Bill Gates travelled from **autocratic → transformational → servant** over his career. Second, the three classic **theories**: **trait** (leaders are born with it), **behavioural** (leaders are trained — right place, right time, right skills), and **contingency** (it depends on the situation).

:::tip[Important — The Cost of a Bad Boss]
> **The professor:** "Research suggests that around 80% of employees quit or change their jobs because of abusive supervision."

So when a case asks why good people are leaving, don't jump to salary — look at the **supervisor** and the work climate first.
:::

## Big Idea 6: Power, Politics and Influence Without Authority

Office politics — favouritism, lobbying against someone, hoarding resources — is, in the professor's words, **inevitable**. The survival skill is **political skill** (political astuteness), and she is careful to separate it from the dark version:

> **The professor:** "Political skill is not a negative term. It's not about manipulation. It's about understanding others and using that knowledge to influence others to benefit the organization — whereas organizational politics is self-serving behaviour at the expense of the organization."

She then separated **formal** from **informal** power. Your boss has **formal** power over you. But you can influence people you have no authority over through **informal power** — mainly **referent power** (people admire you / your charisma) and **expert power** (you know something they don't).

> **The professor:** "Why do we get influenced by celebrities? They have no formal power over us — but there's something in them we admire. That's referent power."

**Why it matters:** in flat teams you rarely have formal authority over peers. Relationships, credibility and expertise become your real levers.

## Big Idea 7: Teams and Conflict — Competence Isn't the Whole Story

We work in **teams**, and the professor set a trap question: if every team member is individually brilliant but they can't work together, will the team hit its goals?

> **The professor:** "Everybody is competent — but if they are not compatible, they won't be able to work together and achieve organizational goals."

So teams need **competence *and* compatibility.** Two sessions (and a diagnostic test) are devoted to building that compatibility.

**Conflict** gets a similar myth-bust: it is **not always bad**. Split it in two:

- **Task conflict** — disagreement about the work. Often **functional**; it can raise quality, so a manager may even *encourage* it.
- **Relationship conflict** — friction between people. Usually **dysfunctional**; the manager's job is to reduce it.

Everyone has a default conflict style, which you'll discover through an in-class activity, alongside a session on **negotiation**.

## Big Idea 8: Perception Is Not Reality — Unconscious Bias

The professor played a short office video and let it do the teaching. In it, colleagues trip over hidden assumptions — a woman is assumed to have dressed up only to win a good appraisal; a jokey "regional connect" jibe is used to explain a colleague's leave; young employees are assumed to slack and take coffee breaks; a differently-abled colleague is quietly judged. One character finally names it: everyone is guilty of **unconscious bias** — "judging people based on stereotypes you're not even aware of."

That sets up one of the most quotable lines of the session:

> **The professor:** "Our perceptions are our reality. But our perceptions are different from reality — because our perceptions are full of unconscious biases."

Biases are **mental shortcuts** — simply how the brain works. You can't switch them off, but you can catch them.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**We act on perception, and perception is distorted by unconscious bias.** You cannot control your brain, but **with awareness you can manage your biases.** If a case shows a manager misreading an employee (by gender, age, region or ability), name it as unconscious bias and prescribe **self-awareness**, not a rulebook.
:::

## Why a "Soft" Subject Is Full of Theories

Students often ask why an OB course leans on so much theory. The professor's definition is disarmingly simple:

> **The professor:** "A theory is a set of ideas that explain human behaviour — and it is applicable to every human being on this planet."

She proved it with two theories you'll meet again:

**1. Goal-setting theory.** She described a **1979 study** that asked MBA graduates whether they'd written down clear goals. Reportedly ~**83%** had no goals, **14%** had unwritten goals, and only **3%** had written goals with plans. Twenty years on, the written-goal group had pulled dramatically ahead (the professor cited roughly **30×**, and the unwritten-goal group about **14×**, the success of those with no goals). The lesson: **specific, challenging, written goals** direct your energy, create motivation, and motivation becomes performance — especially with timely, constructive feedback. Hence **SMART** goals: specific, measurable, achievable, time-bound. Compare *"I want to be rich"* with *"I want to save ₹10 lakh in the next 5 years."*

**2. Herzberg's two-factor theory** — her explanation for **quiet quitting.** Every job has **hygiene factors** (salary, bonuses, culture, supervisor, HR policies) and **motivators** (meaningful work, achievement, recognition, growth). Hygiene factors only *prevent dissatisfaction*; they can't energize you. In quiet quitting, the hygiene factors are fine — so you don't leave — but the work itself feels empty, so you do the bare minimum.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Hygiene stops the pain; motivators lift you"]
**Hygiene factors keep you from quitting. Motivators make you care.** Good pay with meaningless work = a quiet quitter. That's Herzberg in one line.
:::

## How to Crack an IGD Case (Starts Next Class)

You'll solve 8–9 cases. A **case** is a real or imagined business scenario that hands you a **problem** plus some **incomplete information** — you must decide under uncertainty. The professor's step-by-step method:

1. **Read carefully** and state the **problem**.
2. Identify the **protagonist** (the person facing it) and their strengths/weaknesses — **put yourself in their shoes**.
3. Identify the **other key players** and the **organization's characteristics**.
4. List **4–5 action alternatives**.
5. Write the **pros and cons** of each.
6. Pick the **most pragmatic** alternative and **justify** it.

She flagged a recurring twist unique to IGD cases:

> **The professor:** "The simplest option is to leave the organization — but that's not an option for you, because the organizations are undergoing downsizing and economic downturn. You have to find a way to manage."

So "I'd quit" is never the answer here. The first case, **Michael Peterson** (human behaviour), lands in Session 2.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Problem → Person → Players → Plans → Pick"]
Five Ps for case-cracking: nail the **Problem**, inhabit the **Person** (protagonist), map the other **Players** and context, list the **Plans** (alternatives with pros/cons), then **Pick** and defend one. And remember: **you can't just quit.**
:::

## The Recipe Chain (Where This Session Sits)

**Session 1 builds the "WHY" and the map.**

> **Session 1 → People aren't interchangeable; read the P-factors. Zoom out in three steps: Self → Others → Organization.**
> Session 2 → Apply the lens to a real person: the **Michael Peterson** case (human behaviour), using the six-step case method.
> Then → VABs, attitudes, personality, biases and motivation build out your picture of **Self**, before the course turns to teams, power, conflict and leadership — the **Organization**.

If Session 1 convinced you that managing people is really the work of *understanding* them, Session 2 asks the obvious next thing: **"Fine — so take one real person stuck in a real organization, and tell me what they should actually do."**
