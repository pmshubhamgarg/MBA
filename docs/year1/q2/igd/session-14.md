# Session 14: My Way, Your Way, or a Better Way

*A top performer gets crushed between two feuding bosses while the class learns the real lesson: conflict itself isn't the villain. There are five ways to handle a clash and two ways to strike a deal — and the only mistake is picking the wrong one for the moment.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** What **conflict** is; **functional vs dysfunctional** conflict; the three types — **task, relationship, process** (and the rule that **relationship conflict is always dysfunctional**); the **Thomas–Kilmann five styles** (Competing, Collaborating, Compromising, Avoiding, Accommodating) on the **assertiveness × cooperativeness** grid; **distributive vs integrative** negotiation; the three **third-party roles** (mediator, arbitrator, conciliator).

**Important (supporting):** How the **Big Five (OCEAN)** traits map to conflict styles; the **nine conflict-resolution techniques**; **triangulation / 360-degree feedback** to read your own style; collaborating vs compromising (the most-confused pair); "no style is good or bad — it's context dependent."

**Context (colour for case answers):** The **TKC Consulting** case — Ralo vs Nayak, with Aman trapped in dual reporting and chairman Mukharji as the decision-maker; everyday examples (TV remote, ₹18 lakh salary settlement, 22°C AC truce, haggling for apples).
:::

## Setting the Stage: A Class Built Around One Fight

Session 14 is **Conflict Management and Negotiation** — and the professor runs it as a sandwich. Two study groups (10 and 11) present the same case, **"Conflict Management at TKC Consulting,"** and in between she teaches the concepts the case needs. When Group 10 ran 30 minutes over, she set a hard rule for the future: **15–20 minutes per presentation, no more.**

Her opening move is to tie the two words of the title together:

> **The professor:** "Conflict represents a disagreement, and negotiation is the process of resolving that conflict."

That one line is the spine of the whole evening. First understand the disagreement (conflict), then learn the ways to settle it (styles, negotiation, third parties).

---

## What Conflict Actually Is — and Why It Isn't Always Bad

**Concept.** In plain language, **conflict is a disagreement between two parties** — or one party's *perception* that the other is about to hurt its interests. It can sit anywhere on a scale "from a subtle form of disagreement to violent acts." It can even happen inside one person who holds two clashing beliefs.

The first myth to kill: conflict is not automatically a problem. Split it by its **effect**:

- **Functional (constructive) conflict** — the disagreement sparks creativity and clarity and ends in a *better* outcome.
- **Dysfunctional (destructive) conflict** — it drains energy, erodes trust, and ends in a *worse* outcome.

> **The professor:** "Conflict is not always bad. If a disagreement promotes creativity and clarity and results in a better outcome, that is functional conflict."

**Why it matters.** A student pushed back — in his experience, conflict in a company is *usually* bad for results. The professor agreed there's no neat percentage, but held the line: whether a clash helps or harms "depends on the people and how the conflict is handled." A manager's job isn't to ban conflict; it's to keep the useful kind and defuse the toxic kind. (As Group 10 noted, studies suggest roughly **20% of top and middle managers' time** goes into resolving conflicts — so handling it well is a core leadership skill, not a soft extra.)

---

## Three Flavours of Conflict: Task, Relationship, Process

Beyond "good or bad," classify conflict by **what the disagreement is about**:

| Type | What it's about | Example | Usually… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task conflict** | The **content of the work** — different approaches or strategy | One member wants a qualitative method, another insists on quantitative; marketing debates digital vs traditional | Can be **functional** |
| **Relationship conflict** | **Personality clashes**, power differences, dislike or distrust | Two colleagues trade sarcastic remarks because their styles grate | **Always dysfunctional** |
| **Process conflict** | **How the work gets done** — roles, responsibilities, timelines, resource allocation | One person feels overloaded while others coast; two teams fight over who got more resources | Can be **functional** |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Relationship conflict always results in negative outcomes.** Task and process conflict *can* be functional (they can raise quality), but relationship conflict — rooted in personalities and egos — is always dysfunctional, so a manager's job is to reduce it. In a case, naming the **type** of conflict correctly is half the answer: content of work = **task**; personalities = **relationship**; roles/resources/"how" = **process**.
:::

There's a third cut too — the **locus** of the conflict:

- Between two individuals → **interpersonal / direct (dyadic)**
- Within a team → **intragroup**
- Between teams → **intergroup** (and, wider still, inter-organizational)

## The Five Ways People Fight — The Thomas–Kilmann Model

This is the heart of the session and a near-certain exam target. The **Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument**, built in the early 1970s by external scholars **Kenneth Thomas** and **Ralph Kilmann**, says every conflict style is a mix of two dimensions:

- **Assertiveness** — how hard you push for **your own** concerns (focus on self).
- **Cooperativeness** — how much you try to satisfy the **other** party's concerns (focus on others).

Blend them and you get five styles:

| Style | Assertiveness (self) | Cooperativeness (others) | Outcome | Everyday line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Competing** | High | Low | I win, you lose (zero-sum) | "My way or the highway" |
| **Collaborating** | High | High | I win, you win (full satisfaction) | "Let's find a solution that works for both" |
| **Compromising** | Medium | Medium | I bend, you bend (partial satisfaction) | "Let's split the difference" |
| **Avoiding** | Low | Low | No winner, no loser | "Can we drop it? It's not worth it" |
| **Accommodating** | Low | High | I lose, you win (keep the peace) | "Fine, I'll do it if it keeps the peace" |

The professor drilled each style with quick examples, which are gold for MCQs:

| Style | Class examples |
|---|---|
| **Competing** | Two siblings grab the TV remote; a sales manager forces his pricing model past Finance because "targets matter more" |
| **Accommodating** | A junior accepts a senior's plan to preserve harmony; you sit through a movie you dislike because the group wants it |
| **Avoiding** | A manager keeps postponing a poor-performance talk because he dislikes confrontation; "forget it, let's not go out today" |
| **Compromising** | A candidate asks ₹20 lakh, HR offers ₹16 lakh, they settle at **₹18 lakh + a bonus**; roommates split 24°C vs 20°C and set the AC at **22°C** |
| **Collaborating** | HR and Operations co-design a flexible-work policy that protects *both* productivity and well-being; friends plan a 10-day trip that includes *both* destinations |

:::tip[Memory Hook — "The 5 C's and who wins"]
**Compete** (I win, you lose) · **Collaborate** (I win, you win) · **Compromise** (I bend, you bend) · **Avoid** (no one wins) · **Accommodate** (I lose, you win). Read the grid as **self vs others**: push hard for yourself *and* care about them = collaborate; push for yourself *only* = compete; care about them *only* = accommodate.
:::

### Collaborating vs Compromising — the pair everyone mixes up

A student asked the sharpest question of the night: "I win–you win, and I bend–you bend — what's the actual difference?"

> **The professor:** "In collaboration you come up with a creative solution that satisfies both parties fully. In compromise, both parties give up something to find a middle ground — so each is only partially satisfied."

So the flexible-work policy (a *new* design that serves both) is **collaboration**; settling a salary at ₹18 lakh (both gave ground) is **compromise**.

---

## Which Style Is Best? None — It's the Situation

The class instinctively answered "collaboration." The professor pushed: what about an **emergency**? Then **competing** is right. What if you only care about the **relationship**? Then **accommodating**. What if you need **both the task and the relationship**? Then **collaborating**.

> **The professor:** "No style is good or bad. It's context dependent."

Her rapid-fire revision line is worth memorising: *"It's my way or the highway"* = competing · *"Let's find a solution for both of us"* = collaborating · *"Let's split the difference"* = compromising · *"Can we drop it, it's not worth it"* = avoiding · *"Fine, I'll do it if it keeps the peace"* = accommodating.

:::tip[Important]
Don't write "collaboration is always best" in an exam. Full marks come from **matching the style to the context**: time-critical or safety issue → **competing**; trivial issue → **avoiding**; relationship matters most → **accommodating**; you need buy-in and a durable fix → **collaborating**; you need a quick, fair deal → **compromising**.
:::

## Your Personality Predicts How You Fight

The professor then linked the five styles back to the **Big Five (OCEAN)** traits from the earlier personality sessions. The pattern isn't always the "obvious" one — which is exactly why it's exam-worthy:

| Big Five trait | Natural conflict style | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraversion** | Competing | Assertive and dominating |
| **Agreeableness** | Accommodating | Empathetic, altruistic, harmony-seeking |
| **Conscientiousness** | Compromising | Practical and dutiful — seeks a quick, workable middle ground so conflict doesn't disrupt the workflow |
| **Openness** | Collaborating | Imaginative and curious — explores many perspectives for a win-win |
| **Neuroticism** | Avoiding | Anxious and withdrawing, so they dodge the clash |

The conscientiousness case was the debated one. Students argued conscientious people "should" compete or collaborate (they're driven and want the *right* answer). The professor accepted the logic but pointed to the data: because they're **practical and time-conscious**, they usually **compromise** — a fast, fair middle ground beats a slow, perfect one.

> **The professor:** "These conflict management styles are closely related to your personality traits."

:::note[Good to Know]
One student noted that an *agreeable* person compromises out of genuine empathy, while a merely practical person may "agree now but keep a count and give it back later as feedback." The professor's read: a truly **conscientious and ethical** person won't nurse grudges — but someone blending conscientiousness with **dark traits** might. A neat reminder that traits combine, and a self-rating is only a starting point.
:::

### Reading your own style honestly — triangulation

Students took a psychometric test (an emailed Excel sheet, scored into animal types — e.g., the **owl** = collaborating) to find their natural style. But a self-test can be distorted by **social-desirability bias** (we rate ourselves as we'd like to be). The fix a student asked for — "how do I interpret these results accurately?":

> **The professor:** "When you collect data from different sources to validate your findings, that is triangulation."

So pair the self-test with **360-degree feedback** — ask peers and group members to rate you too. That is exactly the three-stage method of the IGD personality project (group interview → self-test → compare), and the same approach used in the emotional-intelligence session.

## From Fighting to Dealing: Negotiation

If conflict is the disagreement, **negotiation is the process of resolving it**. It comes in two forms:

| | **Distributive bargaining** | **Integrative bargaining** |
|---|---|---|
| **The pie** | Fixed | Can be expanded |
| **Logic** | One party's gain = the other's loss (zero-sum) | Both parties gain; no loss for the other |
| **Style used** | Competing (win–lose) | Collaborating (win–win) |
| **Mindset** | "How do we **split** what we have?" | "How do we make this **bigger** for both before we split?" |
| **Examples** | Salary negotiation; haggling for 1 kg of apples down from ₹200 to ₹180 | Mergers and acquisitions; a partnership where one firm brings the marketing channel and the other brings product development |

The haggling example sparked a good debate. A student argued the apple sale is "win–win" because the vendor still profits. The professor refocused the lens: *from your side of this single deal*, every rupee you save is a rupee he loses — the pie is fixed, so it's **distributive**. Contrast that with M&A, where combining two companies can **create new value**, so the pie grows — **integrative**.

> **The professor:** "You ask for 15 lakhs, they offer 13, and then you both agree on 14 — your loss is there. That's distributive bargaining."

A later question tested the edge: if a resource moves from one program to another, which is it? The answer depends on the outcome — if one program **loses** so the other **gains**, it's **distributive**; if an *idle* resource is shared and both programs (and revenue) **gain**, it's **integrative**.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Split the pie vs grow the pie"]
**Distributive = split a fixed pie** (competing, win–lose, like a salary or a sabzi-mandi haggle). **Integrative = grow the pie** (collaborating, win–win, like a merger or partnership). If someone must lose for you to win, it's distributive; if you can both end up better off, it's integrative.
:::

## When Two Parties Can't Settle It: Third-Party Roles

When the two sides are stuck, a **third party** steps in. Learn all three — the difference is *how much power to decide* they hold:

| Role | What they do | Decision power | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mediator** | Helps both sides communicate, explore interests, and move toward a mutually acceptable solution | **None** — only facilitates | A bank sitting between two feuding firms; a family elder in a dispute |
| **Arbitrator** | Hears both sides, weighs the evidence, and issues a decision | **Yes** — binding *or* non-binding | A **judge in court** (binding) |
| **Conciliator** | Works **separately** with each side to lower hostility and rebuild trust and communication | **None** — repairs the relationship | A trusted go-between calming both camps |

The class tested the boundary with a judge: a court ruling is an **arbitrator's** binding decision — though students noted a ruling can be overturned on appeal, which is why arbitration can be either binding or non-binding.

## Nine Tools to Cool a Conflict (Resolution Techniques)

Separately from the five *styles*, the professor listed nine **conflict-resolution techniques** a manager can deploy when dysfunctional conflict is too high:

1. **Problem solving** — open, face-to-face discussion between the parties.
2. **Superordinate goals** — a shared goal both must cooperate to reach.
3. **Expansion of resources** — add money, promotions, or opportunities so there's enough to go around.
4. **Avoidance** — withdraw from or suppress the conflict (a negative, short-term option).
5. **Smoothing** — stress common interests and the organization's welfare; play down the differences.
6. **Compromise** — each side gives up something.
7. **Authoritative command** — someone with authority simply decides (e.g., Mukharji over both leaders).
8. **Altering the human variable** — training to shift attitudes and behaviour.
9. **Altering the structural variable** — job redesign, transfers, or new positions.

:::tip[Important]
Don't confuse the two toolkits. **Styles** (Competing, Collaborating, Compromising, Avoiding, Accommodating) are *how an individual personally handles* a clash — the Thomas–Kilmann five. **Techniques** (the nine above) are *organizational levers a manager pulls* to resolve a conflict between others. An exam answer that mixes "styles" and "techniques" loses marks.
:::

## The Case: Conflict Management at TKC Consulting

**The setting.** TKC Consulting is a 25-year-old financial-brokerage firm — results-obsessed, high-pressure, and built on a **dual-reporting structure** meant to be flexible but which breeds confusion. The cast:

- **Ralo** — a 15-year veteran, Tamil Nadu regional head; strong network and expertise, but **conservative, authoritarian, and insecure**.
- **Nayak** — rose from operations executive to **Country Head of Distribution**; **charming and relationship-driven**, but starts to undermine Ralo after his promotion.
- **Aman** — 35, a top-performing regional product head (insurance); calm and respected, but he **avoids challenging authority** and gets anxious under conflict. He is caught in the dual reporting — **the victim**.
- **Mukharji** — the wise, diplomatic chairman who must step in.

**How it escalates.** A transfer request Ralo raises for an employee is ignored by Nayak → Nayak is promoted over Ralo, who then badmouths him in his circle → Nayak **bypasses Ralo**, routing invites and even a cancelled-lunch message *through Aman*, so Ralo feels sidelined and disrespected → in retaliation, Ralo **transfers Aman out of his insurance expertise**, pins "poor performance" on him, and makes him work from his office to tighten control. Aman's performance drops and his morale collapses; he wants to leave but the recession blocks any exit.

**The diagnosis the professor wanted.** Type: **relationship conflict** (clashing styles and egos) laced with **process conflict** (dual reporting, unclear roles); **dysfunctional**; **direct/dyadic** between two leaders. Causes: **communication, structural, and personal**.

**The action plan (as Mukharji):** (1) **damage control** to stop the bleeding; (2) a **combination of conflict-management techniques**; (3) **third-party negotiation** — a mediator, arbitrator, or conciliator; (4) **long-term** attitudinal and behavioural change through training — plus restoring Aman's role, enforcing a formal communication channel, and fixing the dual-reporting structure so no one can abuse authority.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The protagonist is the person who must make the decision — not the person suffering.** Students kept naming **Aman** (the victim). The professor was firm:

> **The professor:** "Aman is the victim here. The protagonist is Mukharji, because he has to make the decision to resolve the conflict."

And, as in every IGD case, "**I'd quit**" is not an answer — the recession means Aman can't simply leave, so the protagonist must *manage* the situation.
:::

## Key Terms Table (Quick Revision)

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | A disagreement between two parties (or a perception that one will harm the other) |
| **Functional vs dysfunctional** | Conflict that improves outcomes vs conflict that drains trust and energy |
| **Task conflict** | Disagreement over the **content** of the work (approach/strategy) — can be functional |
| **Relationship conflict** | Clash of **personalities/egos** — **always dysfunctional** |
| **Process conflict** | Disagreement over **how** work is done (roles, resources, timelines) — can be functional |
| **Thomas–Kilmann model** | Five styles on **assertiveness × cooperativeness** |
| **Competing** | High assert, low coop → I win, you lose (zero-sum) |
| **Collaborating** | High both → I win, you win (creative, full satisfaction) |
| **Compromising** | Medium both → I bend, you bend (partial satisfaction) |
| **Avoiding** | Low both → no winner, no loser |
| **Accommodating** | Low assert, high coop → I lose, you win (keep the peace) |

| **Distributive bargaining** | Fixed pie, win–lose (e.g., salary, haggling) — uses competing |
| **Integrative bargaining** | Pie can grow, win–win (e.g., M&A, partnership) — uses collaborating |
| **Mediator** | Facilitates a solution; no decision power |
| **Arbitrator** | Neutral decider; binding or non-binding (like a judge) |
| **Conciliator** | Works separately with each side to rebuild trust |

## The Recipe Chain (Where Session 14 Sits)

**Session 14 turns self-knowledge outward — from understanding people to managing the friction *between* them.**

> Earlier sessions → Built your map of **Self** (personality, OCEAN, biases, motivation) and then **Others** (emotions, teams, compatibility).
> **Session 14 → When people clash: name the conflict (task / relationship / process), choose a style from the Thomas–Kilmann five, and resolve it through negotiation (distributive or integrative) or a third party.**
> Next → Carry these tools into **power, politics, and leadership** — because influencing people and settling disputes is what the manager-to-leader transition is really about.

If the personality sessions taught you *why* two people see the world differently, Session 14 is where that difference turns into a fight on the floor — and hands you the five styles, the two bargains, and the three referees to settle it without burning the house down.
