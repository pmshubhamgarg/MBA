# Session 15: Politics Is the Water, Political Skill Is Your Swimming

*Power isn't your job title, and office politics isn't optional — it's the water everyone swims in. This session hands you the one skill that decides whether that water drowns your career or carries it: political skill. Then it shows you a brilliant engineer who sank because she never learned to swim.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** **Power = your capacity to influence others** (A influences B so B acts as A wishes); **French and Raven's five bases of power** — **formal** (legitimate/positional, reward, coercive) vs **personal** (expert, referent); the **effectiveness ranking** (referent is most effective, coercive least); **organizational politics** = self-serving behaviour at the expense of others; **political skill** (positive, not manipulation) and its **four dimensions** — social astuteness, interpersonal influence, networking ability, apparent sincerity; the **water-and-swimming** metaphor.

**Important (supporting):** Why employees engage in politics; how **Big Five** traits relate to political skill (extraversion strongest, neuroticism negative); how **EQ** fuels political skill; the **dark triad** gap (they lack apparent sincerity); the introvert myth-bust; the **Political Skill Inventory**; how to develop each of the four dimensions.

**Context (colour for answers):** The **Caroline Reges / Excel–Gemini merger** case; the **Ria** positive example; referent power in celebrity marketing; the **Bhishma** analogy; Max Weber and Etzioni (from the student presentation); power distance and LMX.
:::

## Setting the Stage

The agenda the professor laid out (through a very laggy network) was tight and connected: **what power is, the different bases of power, formal vs informal power, then organizational politics, then political skill** — and finally how all of it links back to the **Big Five personality traits** and the **dark triad**. A small **class activity** (the Political Skill Inventory) and a **case discussion** (Caroline Reges at Excel Systems) closed the session.

One framing note she repeated at the end: this is an **introductory** OB course, so it only *introduces* power and politics. The area offers full **electives on power and politics** in the second year.

## Big Idea 1: What Power Actually Is

**Power** is not a chair, a cabin or a designation. It is a capacity.

> **The professor:** "Power is your capacity to influence others — the capacity of A to influence B so that B acts in accordance with A's wishes. It's a form of social influence."

**Why it matters:** if power is the *ability to influence*, then you can be powerful without being the boss — and your boss can hold a title yet fail to move anyone. That gap is the whole session.

---

## Big Idea 2: The Five Bases of Power (French and Raven)

In 1959, **French and Raven** (external scholars) outlined **five sources of power**, grouped into two families.

| Family | Base of power | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal power** | **Legitimate / positional** | Your position — "because I am the boss" |
| **Formal power** | **Reward** | Your ability to give benefits — "do this, you'll be rewarded" |
| **Formal power** | **Coercive** | Your ability to punish — the flip side of reward, "do this or face consequences" |
| **Personal power** | **Expert** | Your knowledge, skills or expertise others lack — "do it because I know best" |
| **Personal power** | **Referent** | Your charm, charisma or interpersonal qualities — people admire you and follow willingly |

**The class example — referent power:** Why do we get influenced by **celebrities** who have no formal authority over us? Because we admire them, we want to follow them. That is referent power, and it is exactly how the **marketing industry** works.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Formal is your Chair, Personal is You"]
**Formal power** (legitimate, reward, coercive) rides on your **position** — take away the chair and it's gone. **Personal power** (expert, referent) rides on **you** — your knowledge and your charisma travel with you everywhere.
:::

---

## Big Idea 3: Which Power Works Best?

The class ranked them, and the professor gave the verdict — ordered by **influence and positive outcomes**:

| Rank | Power | How it works | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Referent** | People follow you willingly because they admire you | Most effective |
| 2 | **Expert** | People follow because they respect and trust your knowledge | High |
| 3 | **Legitimate** | Based on authority/position | Moderate |
| 3 | **Reward** | Compliance through incentives | Moderate |
| 5 | **Coercive** | Threats and punishment; people obey out of fear | Least effective |

**Coercive is the only power with purely negative influence.** The other four are seen as relatively positive by *both* parties — A and B.

> **The professor:** "You don't need formal power to be powerful. Influence doesn't need a corner office — it needs credibility."

**Who is a good leader?** Someone who **balances formal and personal power** — they have authority over you by position, but because you admire, respect and trust them, you follow willingly. The leader as a **role model** is referent power in action.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Personal power beats formal power.** Rank it from most to least effective: **Referent, then Expert, then Legitimate and Reward (roughly tied), then Coercive last.** If a case shows someone influencing others *without* authority, name **referent or expert power**. If someone rules by fear, name **coercive** — and note it's the weakest for long-term outcomes.
:::

## Big Idea 4: Organizational Politics — the Inevitable Water

Ask anyone what "politics at work" means and the words come fast: **favouritism, sabotaging work, not recognising contribution, rewarding yes-men, emotional (not logical) decision-making.** The professor's definition is sharper:

> **The professor:** "Organizational politics is self-serving behaviour by employees to achieve self-interest or advantage at the expense of others — and sometimes contrary to the interest of the entire organization."

**The classic examples** (a strong list to memorise): favouritism, manipulation, power play, **blame game and scapegoating**, **lobbying** against an individual or a decision, exchanging favours for mutual benefit, spreading rumours, backstabbing, and **withholding important information** to harm someone.

**Why do employees do it?** For **survival**, promotion, personal insecurity, to form alliances for mutual benefit, to gain visibility — and especially when **SOPs are unclear** or resources are scarce, creating ambiguity people try to cover for.

A sharp point from a student: **staying silent** when you see politics is itself a form of *indirectly engaging* in it.

> **The professor:** "Politics is inevitable in organizations. Ignore it, and it can quietly derail your career."

:::note[Good to Know — Weber and Etzioni (from the student group)]
The presenting group added two external scholars worth a line in an answer. **Max Weber** (German sociologist) defined power as *"the probability that one actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his own will despite resistance,"* and separated **power** (ability to secure compliance), **authority** (the *right* to compliance through legitimacy) and **leadership** (inspiring a voluntary response). **Etzioni** classified organizational power as **coercive, utilitarian** (reward/performance — typical of business) and **normative** (typical of religious bodies).
:::

## Big Idea 5: Political Skill — Your Ability to Swim

If politics is unavoidable, the survival skill is **political skill**. The professor's metaphor is the line to remember from this session:

> **The professor:** "If organizational politics is the water, political skill is your ability to swim. It decides whether you sink or sail."

Like **emotional intelligence**, political skill is a **key leadership skill** — and crucially, it is **positive**, not manipulation.

> **The professor:** "Political skill is your ability to understand others and use that knowledge to influence them to achieve your goals and the organization's goals — but *not* at the expense of others. There's no manipulation involved."

**That is the whole difference from organizational politics.** In politics you win *by harming others*; with political skill you win *by understanding, connecting with and earning the trust of* others. Same goal reached — opposite method.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Politics is the water, political skill is the swim"]
**Organizational politics = the water** (inevitable, can drown you). **Political skill = your swimming** (it decides sink or sail). You can't drain the pool — so learn to swim.
:::

## The Four Dimensions of Political Skill (Ferris and colleagues)

Political skill was framed by **Ferris and colleagues** as **four dimensions**. Learn them as a set — this is the heart of the session.

| Dimension | Textbook meaning | In plain English |
|---|---|---|
| **Social astuteness** | Read people and situations; sense motives and hidden agendas | "You see the person behind the words." Walk into a room and you know who's a friend and who's a foe. |
| **Interpersonal influence** | Adapt your style to persuade effectively | You **persuade with a style, not with force** — tailoring a pitch differently for an executive vs frontline staff. |
| **Networking ability** | Build and leverage beneficial relationships across levels | Your connections are a **web of opportunity**; you're a **360-degree player** who knows bosses, peers and juniors. |
| **Apparent sincerity** | Come across as genuine, authentic, trustworthy | You **walk the talk** and win trust through genuine action. |

> **The professor:** "A politically savvy person can read the room, persuade with charm, connect with everybody, and earn trust with every genuine gesture."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Political skill needs all four dimensions together: social astuteness + interpersonal influence + networking + apparent sincerity.** Drop any one — especially **apparent sincerity** — and you stop being politically savvy and start looking manipulative. This four-part model is the most testable idea in the session.
:::

### The Political Skill Inventory (class activity)

Students took a **Political Skill Inventory** — an **18-item self-assessment** on a **1-to-7 scale** (developed by Ferris and colleagues) — to gauge their **political astuteness**. Most scored on the higher side (around 5 and above).

The professor used that to teach a bias lesson: self-assessment scores are inflated by **social desirability bias** and **illusory superiority bias**. "This is how our brain reacts — it's not in our control." And because political skill is a **skill**, people can be **trained** to become politically savvy.

---

## Big Idea 6: How It All Links — Power, Politics, Political Skill

Put the three together and the picture is clean:

- **Power** = your capacity to influence (from a formal position *or* from personal means — expertise, charisma, network).
- **Politics** = how that power gets *used* to pursue goals amid competition and scarce resources.
- **Political skill** = your **navigation ability** — effectively steering through politics by understanding people, influencing them, building connections and earning trust.

A student pressed the obvious tension: why does "politics" always sound negative while "political skill" sounds fine? The professor's answer is the exam distinction:

> **The professor:** "Organizational politics is negative — self-serving behaviour at the expense of others. Political skill is positive — navigating that politics without manipulation. In political skill you still achieve your goals, but by influencing and earning trust, not by harming anyone."

## Big Idea 7: Personality, the Dark Triad, and EQ

### Big Five traits don't *predict* political skill — they make it easier

Your **Big Five (OCEAN)** traits don't directly determine political skill, but some traits make it **easier to develop**:

| Big Five trait | Most related political-skill dimension |
|---|---|
| **Extraversion** | **Networking + interpersonal influence** — the **strongest, most consistent** link |
| **Agreeableness** | **Apparent sincerity** (trustworthy, altruistic, empathetic) |
| **Conscientiousness** | **Apparent sincerity + interpersonal influence** (reliable, deliberate) |
| **Openness** | **Social astuteness + interpersonal influence** (observant, adaptable) |
| **Neuroticism** | **Negatively** related to all four (anxious, hostile, vulnerable) |

:::tip[Important — Introverts Are Not Bad at Politics]
A student revived the old question: aren't introverts weak at networking and influence? **No — it's a myth.** Introverts simply play **depth over breadth**: as keen observers they are often **high on social astuteness**; being thoughtful, they influence well; they build **smaller but deeper** networks (quality over quantity); and being reflective, they read as very **sincere**. Smaller circle ≠ weaker political skill.
:::

### The dark triad: good at three, missing the fourth

The **dark triad** — **Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy** — are manipulative, and political skill is *not* manipulation. So where's the line?

> **The professor:** "Machiavellian individuals are actually good at social astuteness, interpersonal influence and networking — but they **lack apparent sincerity**. That's why they are *not* politically savvy."

That single missing piece — genuine, authentic sincerity — is what separates the **politically savvy "good guy"** from the **Machiavellian "bad guy."**

### Emotional intelligence fuels political skill

The professor leaned on a famous external line to connect EQ and political skill:

> **Dale Carnegie, quoted by the professor:** "When dealing with people, remember you are not dealing with creatures of logic; you are dealing with creatures of emotion."

So EQ maps almost one-to-one onto political skill:

| Emotional intelligence | Feeds this political-skill dimension |
|---|---|
| **Self-awareness** (know your strengths, biases) | **Apparent sincerity** |
| **Self-regulation** (control yourself) | **Interpersonal influence** |
| **Empathy** (read others' emotions) | **Social astuteness** |
| **Relationship management** | **Networking ability** |

**Bottom line:** if you are emotionally intelligent, you can develop political astuteness far more easily.

## How to Build Political Skill (Practical)

Since it's a skill, you can train each of the four dimensions:

- **Practise situational awareness:** active listening, read between the lines, learn the culture and the "wiggle room," identify key decision-makers, notice non-verbal cues, and **ask questions instead of assuming** — know who are friends and who are foes.
- **Develop interpersonal influence:** focus on **relationships, not only the task**; treat everyone with respect; don't reach out only when you need something; use empathy to understand the other person's point of view before you persuade.
- **Build a dynamic network:** be a genuine listener, help others before you need them, and cultivate relationships **across the hierarchy** so you become a true **360-degree player** who can get information from anyone.
- **Develop apparent sincerity:** **walk the talk**, be forthright and honest, and (a conscientiousness habit) **think of the implications before you respond.**

## The Case: Caroline Reges at Excel Systems

**The setup.** Excel Systems (a fast-growing tech firm in wearable/variable devices, founded by **Ron Whitney**) merges with rival **Gemini Systems**. Post-merger, Gemini's CEO **Roger Drennan** becomes CEO of the combined company and Ron Whitney moves up to **chairman**. Drennan — backed by **Margaret Ogilvie** (VP Supply Chain, a long-time ally and outsourcing champion) — pushes to **outsource all manufacturing**. That directly threatens **Caroline Reges, VP of Manufacturing**: if manufacturing is outsourced, **her department and role vanish**.

**The hidden story.** For the first seven pages it looks like a strategy fight — in-house quality and engineering integration vs outsourcing's cost savings. But **page 8** reveals the real game: **board-level lobbying between the chairman and the CEO**, with the outsourcing push entangled in that power struggle. Caroline never saw it.

> **The professor:** "She mainly suffered because she was unaware of what was going on. Had she developed a good network, she would not have faced this problem."

**Why she sank — mapped to the four dimensions:**
- **Social astuteness — weak.** The CFO **Earl Warner** warned her, "Drennan sees you as someone he needs, but you haven't given him much to work with." She missed the signal.
- **Networking — weak.** Well respected for her technical skill, yet she **never reached out** to key players to understand the politics or build allies.
- **Interpersonal influence — weak.** Direct and fact-based — effective on the shop floor, not in a boardroom run on narratives.
- **Apparent sincerity — strong.** Her technical integrity made her genuine and trustworthy. But one dimension alone can't keep you afloat.

**The lesson:** Caroline had **sound technical skill but lacked political skill — a people skill.** A good manager needs **technical + people + decision-making** skills together.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Being right is not enough"]
Caroline was *right* about engineering integration — and still lost. **Technical arguments must be matched with political skill.** In a merger, uncertainty is high and **politics intensifies**; the **silence of leaders** (Whitney not backing her) quietly sinks good people.
:::

### The contrast: Ria, who swam

The professor read out a mirror example. **Ria**, a new marketing manager, faces the same storm — teams competing for limited resources, colleagues lobbying and forming coalitions, decisions driven by alliances over merit. But Ria has **strong political skill**: she **reads** team dynamics (social astuteness), **persuades** stakeholders without creating conflict (interpersonal influence), **builds** cross-team allies who feed her information (networking), and stays **reliable, not manipulative** (apparent sincerity). Outcome: she **wins resources and reputation**. Same water as Caroline — opposite result.

:::note[Good to Know — The Bhishma analogy]
One group likened Caroline to **Bhishma** from the Mahabharata: loyal, competent and courageous, yet a victim of the process because he was bound to a **flawed throne**. Competence without the political awareness to navigate the power structure around you is not enough.
:::

## Closing: Politics Can Help, Not Only Harm

A student asked the sharp question: we always hear about politics' *victims* — has anyone ever *gained*? The honest answer: **if someone suffers, someone benefits.** Several students admitted they'd gained through strong, trust-based manager relationships — which the professor named precisely:

> **The professor:** "When you share a trust-based relationship with your supervisor, we call it **LMX — leader-member exchange**."

She flagged the fine line: that same closeness can shade into **favouritism**, and tactics like **ingratiation** and **impression management** sit right beside genuine political skill. And in high **power-distance** settings, when the board has decided, a junior manager often simply has to follow — there's a real **power gap**.

Her most reassuring point: political skill is **learned over time, through experience.**

> **The professor:** "You develop political skill over years, through experience — just like you develop resilience and psychological hardiness after facing abusive supervision for the first time."
