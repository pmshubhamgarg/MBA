# Session 8: If You Have a Brain, You Have Biases

*Your perceptions quietly become your reality — but they are stuffed with mental shortcuts. Session 8 names the biases that run your judgement without asking permission, and shows how two colleagues at Jensen Shoes lived the exact same events as two completely different stories.*

## The Big Idea: "You Are Not as Rational as You Think"

The professor opened with a claim that stings a room full of MBAs:

> **The professor:** "You are not as rational as you think. Do you agree with this?"

Here is the logic. We all act on our **perceptions** — how we see the world. Because we act on them, our perceptions *become* our reality. But perception and reality are not the same thing, because our perceptions are **full of biases**. So the decisions we are sure are "logical" are often just our mental shortcuts talking.

Two quick warm-ups made the point:

- **The office video (unconscious bias).** Colleagues judged a woman as dressing up only to impress the boss, teased another for using "Madrasi bonding" to get leave, pitied a "differently-abled" colleague, and assumed a young achiever takes too many breaks. Everyone was guilty of **unconscious bias** — judging people through stereotypes they were not even aware of.
- **The image activity.** The same picture produced wildly different first impressions ("Jungle Raj" for some, "jolly, great sense of humour" for others; a fairness-cream ad read as "beauty" by some and "racist, discrimination, Photoshop" by others). Same image, different perceptions — because of different **preconceived notions**.

## The Chain: Memes → VABs → Preconceived Notions → Biases

In OB, everything is in the terminology, and biases sit at the end of a chain the class had been building for weeks:

- **Memes** — ideas and beliefs passed from one generation to another (the "mental analogues of biological genes").
- **VABs** — the **V**alues, **A**ssumptions, **B**eliefs and **E**xpectations those memes harden into.
- **Preconceived notions** — beliefs, assumptions or expectations we form *without full information*. These are our **mental shortcuts (heuristics)**.
- **Biases** — the distortions those shortcuts create in our judgement, decisions and behaviour.

> **The professor:** "You don't carry your preconceived notions in your genes. You learn them through conditioning."

**Example:** believe "IIT/IIM graduates are always smarter" (a preconceived notion) and you will overrate a candidate for the badge while overlooking someone equally talented from a lesser-known college. Believe "women are more nurturing" and you will nudge women toward HR or teaching roles even when a man has the right skills.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Memes feed VABs, VABs breed biases"]
Trace any bias back up the chain: a **meme** you inherited became a **VAB**, which set a **preconceived notion**, which fires as a **bias**. Conditioning, not genes.
:::

## Attribution Theory: Why Did They Do That?

**Attribution theory** explains **how we attribute the causes of behaviour**. Two buckets:

- **Internal (dispositional) attribution** — the cause is inside the person ("he's lazy and irresponsible").
- **Situational (external) attribution** — the cause is the situation or environment ("the client kept changing requirements").

**Example:** an employee misses a deadline. Blame his character and it is internal; blame the shifting brief and it is external. The *same* missed deadline, two different stories — and which story you pick is where bias sneaks in.

While attributing causes, we commit two signature errors.

## Fundamental Attribution Error vs Self-Serving Bias

| Bias | Whose behaviour? | What we do | One-line example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental Attribution Error** | **Others'** | Over-weight their **internal** factors, under-weight their **external** ones | "He failed because he doesn't work hard" (ignoring a bad teacher or heavy workload) |
| **Self-Serving Bias** | **Our own** | Credit success to **internal**, blame failure on **external** | "I succeeded through hard work; I failed because of my boss" |

The professor's neat test: *"I am successful because of my hard work, but Yoga is successful because of her luck."* The first half is **self-serving bias** (about me); the second half is **fundamental attribution error** (about her).

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Fundamental Attribution Error = judging *others* (internal over external). Self-Serving Bias = judging *yourself* (success internal, failure external).** If a case character explains their *own* flop by blaming circumstances while explaining a *colleague's* flop by blaming character, name both biases by name — that is the full-marks move.
:::

## Overconfidence Bias: The 80–90% Problem

Self-serving bias quietly inflates into **overconfidence bias** — we overestimate our abilities and knowledge and build a false picture of being better than we are.

> **The professor:** "Research suggests that 80 to 90% of people suffer from overconfidence bias — and it often leads to poor decision making."

A student pushed back ("overconfidence can help when you feel insecure"). The professor drew the line: useful self-belief is fine, but **over**confidence means *overestimating* your ability so badly that you make wrong calls. In the case, Jane Kravitz dismissed Chuck's warning about Brooks because "I knew myself to be a good manager" — and later paid for it.

## The Jensen Shoe Company Case: One Office, Two Realities

The session's spine is a two-sided case. The class presented **Jane's side** and **Lyndon's side** separately, and the gap between them *is* the lesson.

**The players:**

| Person | Role | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| **Jane Kravitz** | New strategic product manager, casual wear | Ambitious, people-oriented; coaches and nurtures, struggles to enforce accountability |
| **Lyndon Brooks** | Her staff member (moved from a staff role to a line role) | MBA + PhD, charming, strong in consumer trends; low motivation, feels stereotyped |
| **Chuck Taylor** | Director, strategic marketing (Jane's boss) | Result-driven, skeptical of Brooks, dismisses his wins |
| **Mitch Lawson** | VP operations, Brooks' mentor | Supportive sounding board, but only informal influence |
| **Sally Brooks** | VP marketing | Sets tight, pressure-creating deadlines |
| **Patty Russell** | HR | Jane's route to options and documentation |

**Same facts, opposite stories.** Both agree Brooks' project is stalling. Jane reads it as *"he isn't trying hard enough"*; Lyndon reads it as *"she's micromanaging me, and I was relegated to a stereotype."* Each one's perception felt like reality — and each was full of bias.

**Map the biases (classic exam task):**

- **Jane → Fundamental Attribution Error:** she blamed Brooks' *character* (reluctant, evasive) and ignored the *situation* — a demotion to a line role, Chuck's hostility, thin resources and time.
- **Jane → Overconfidence bias:** "Chuck could be a little quick to judge; besides, I knew myself to be a good manager." She overrode his warning and suffered later.
- **Jane → Halo effect, then racial stereotyping:** first a "charming African-American businessman… a star player if given the right situation," then slotted into African-American and Latino markets *because of his race*.
- **Jane → Gender stereotyping / selective perception:** "I cannot afford my boss to think I could not manage — as a woman I must be seen as a strong leader."
- **Lyndon → Self-serving bias and overconfidence:** "I am good, diligent, competent; I couldn't finish because I was given an inexperienced staff, inadequate resources and time, and my concerns weren't taken seriously." He even called an unfinished objective "one of my most significant achievements."

The result: Jane's distorted perception made her manage Brooks badly (ending in a trust-destroying demand for a formal **contract**), and Brooks' distorted perception made him disengage. Perceptual distortion, not a lack of talent, broke the relationship.

:::note[Good to Know — it is the real "Jensen Shoe Company" case]
Jensen Shoe Company (founded 1953 by Kenneth Jensen) prided itself on an open-door, employee-first culture, but was turning performance-driven and political. Casual wear (about 45% of the mix, with athletic about 30% and children's about 25%) had flat sales, so pressure ran high — exactly the soil where biased, stressed judgement grows.
:::

## The Cognitive-Bias Catalogue

Beyond attribution, the professor ran through the biases that show up daily at work. Learn one crisp example each — these are prime MCQ and short-note targets.

| Bias | What it is | Sticky example |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective perception** | See only what fits your beliefs; overlook the rest | Backing a political party regardless of what it actually delivers |
| **Confirmation bias** | A sub-type of selective perception — gather proof for an existing belief | A manager who "knows" you're lazy notices every late entry, ignores your hard days |
| **Halo effect** | One *positive* trait leads you to assume a whole set of positives | "Attractive, so clearly intelligent and competent" — why we dress up for interviews |
| **Horn effect** | One *negative* trait leads you to assume a whole set of negatives | "Overweight, so lazy and incompetent" |
| **Contrast effect** | Judge people against each other, not against a standard | An average candidate shines right after a weak one, flops right after a star (*"Sharma ka beta"*) |
| **Stereotyping** | Over-generalised belief about a group, ignoring individuals | "Girls are bad at maths," "boys don't cry," racial and gender typecasting |
| **Anchoring bias** | Fixate on the first number you hear | HR floats ₹12 LPA and you haggle around it; an MRP of ₹5,000 "on sale" for ₹250 |
| **Availability bias** | Decide on whatever information is top-of-mind | After the Ahmedabad plane crash, feeling air travel is unsafe — though data says it's the safest mode |
| **Hindsight bias** | "I knew it all along," after the fact | Once the cricket match ends: "I knew that team would win" |
| **Escalation of commitment** | Stick with a decision you *know* is wrong | Staying in a job you despise; marrying despite knowing it isn't working |
| **Randomness bias** | Invent meaning in random events | "I never decide on the 13th"; "I wear my lucky blue shirt to big meetings" |

> **The professor:** "While negotiating your salary, be the first one to make an offer — otherwise you'll fall into their anchoring trap."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Halo lifts, Horn gores"]
**Halo** = one good trait haloes the whole person bright; **Horn** = one bad trait gores the whole person dark. Both are first-impression traps — which is why interviews reward the well-dressed.
:::

## Spot-the-Bias Drill: Ravi's Appraisal

The professor tested the catalogue with **Ravi**, a newly promoted manager rating his six-member team. Each line maps to one bias — a perfect revision quiz:

| What Ravi does | Bias |
|---|---|
| Believes "young people are careless" | Stereotyping |
| Rates Ana highly because she's well-dressed and confident | Halo effect |
| Judges Rohan's average talk as poor, straight after Ana's excellent one | Contrast effect |
| Notices only Priya's mistakes, ignores her wins | Selective perception / confirmation bias |
| Is sure his ratings are final despite past errors | Overconfidence bias |
| Keeps the increment close to HR's recommended number | Anchoring bias |
| Seeks only colleagues who agree Priya underperforms | Confirmation bias |
| Reads about fraud elsewhere, suspects his own team without evidence | Availability bias |
| After a project fails, claims he "always knew" | Hindsight bias |

## Also in the Case — But Not Biases

The professor deliberately flagged two concepts in the case that students mistake for biases:

- **Power distance** — believing in an *unequal* distribution of power, so you follow senior management without questioning ("You don't disagree with senior management"). A cultural value, not a bias.
- **Positive and negative reinforcement** — Jane took Brooks to lunch to reward a win. **Positive** reinforcement adds something pleasant; **negative** reinforcement removes something unpleasant. Both *strengthen* a behaviour; neither is a bias.

:::tip[Important]
In a case answer, separate the **biases** (mental shortcuts that *distort* judgement) from **values and tools** like power distance and reinforcement (which *shape* behaviour but aren't errors of perception). Mislabelling reinforcement or power distance as a "bias" is a common, avoidable slip.
:::

## Managing Biases: You Can't Delete Them, You Can Manage Them

The punchline of the session:

> **The professor:** "If you have a brain, you have biases — it's as simple as that. You cannot control your brain, but with proper awareness you can identify your biases and manage them."

The four-step toolkit:

1. **Self-awareness** — accept that everyone has biases; use introspection and reflection.
2. **Educate yourself** continuously about the different biases and how they bite.
3. **Seek feedback** — 360-degree feedback from people with different perspectives, to break your confirmation and selective-perception loops.
4. **Sensitivity training** — what organisations run to surface prejudices and stereotypes, build empathy, improve communication, and advance **DEI (Diversity, Equity, Inclusivity)**.

## A Few Sharp Clarifications (From Q&A)

- **Biases vs personality / Dark Triad.** Both are "personal factors," but they are *not* linked. Personality is **who you are** (stable, slow to change — think the *12th Fail* arc); biases are **how your brain shortcuts**. Any personality type can carry any bias, so you can't claim "conscientious people get *this* bias."
- **Why we all shortcut.** Buying a cereal, do you compare every vitamin and mineral? No — you decide on limited, available information. Mental shortcuts exist because they make decisions fast.
- **Escalation of commitment vs sunk-cost fallacy.** A student raised the sunk-cost angle (pouring more into a losing stock hoping to recover); the professor noted the overlap and said she would confirm the exact term — a fair flag that the two ideas are cousins.
