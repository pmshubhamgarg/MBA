# Session 9: The Man Who Said No to a Raise

*The session opens with a clip of a man rejecting more money for a job he hates — "I need something I really care about." That is the whole puzzle of motivation in one scene. Money is the biggest motivator, yet money alone never moves anyone for long. Session 9 opens the motivation toolbox: intrinsic vs extrinsic drive, and the **content theories** that explain what people actually want from work.*

## Setting the Stage

The professor frames the evening simply: leadership is, in plain terms, **the process of influencing or motivating others to achieve organizational goals**. So motivation is not a soft topic — it is the *core* of leadership. If you cannot read what drives a person, you cannot lead them.

> **The professor:** "If as a leader you have to motivate your employees and you don't know what motivates them, will you be able to motivate them? No."

The plan: define motivation, run a quick self-assessment, then walk through roughly eight to nine **theories of motivation** — the drivers behind human behaviour — and finally turn them into a practical toolkit.

---

## What Motivation Actually Is

**Concept.** Motivation is the **drive that keeps you going to achieve your goals.** That drive has two sources:

- **Extrinsic motivation** — you work because of *external* factors: pay, bonuses, rewards, or because the boss told you to.
- **Intrinsic motivation** — the drive comes *from within*: you find the work itself meaningful, engaging and satisfying.
- **Amotivation** — the third state: no intrinsic *and* no extrinsic drive. You are simply not motivated at all.

**Why it matters.** Extrinsic motivation is **easy to switch on but short-lived** — remove the reward and the motivation is gone. Intrinsic motivation is **hard to build but long-lasting**, and it is what lifts long-term performance.

> **The professor:** "It's very easy to extrinsically motivate employees — give them rewards and bonuses. But it is very difficult to intrinsically motivate employees. If you can, it results in long-term positive benefits."

The signature line of the whole session — say it in any motivation answer:

> **The professor:** "Money, no doubt, is the biggest motivator. But money alone cannot motivate employees, because employees have different expectations from their work."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Extrinsic fades, Intrinsic stays"]
Rewards are a sugar rush; meaning is a slow fire. A smart manager lights the fire *before* the sugar wears off.
:::

---

## Why Carrot-and-Stick (Transactional Leadership) Fails

Most managers run on **carrot-and-stick**: work hard and you get incentives (carrot); slack off and you get punished — salary cuts, no bonus, demotion (stick). This is **transactional leadership** — an *exchange-based* relationship.

The class listed exactly why it breaks down:

- It only ever produces **extrinsic** motivation, so it creates **short-term compliance**.
- There is **no real connection** with the work itself.
- Extrinsic push has a **ceiling** — you can only reward so far.
- It can trigger **dissatisfaction, attrition, stress and zero-sum rivalry**, and it **kills creativity and innovation**.
- Above all, **one size does not fit all** — the same carrot does not appeal to everyone and the same stick is not equally feared.

> **The professor:** "One-size-fits-all does not apply when we talk about human beings. People are driven by different motivational drivers."

Because you cannot sit one-on-one with 250 people, good leaders don't chase individuals — they **build an inclusive culture** where most people feel valued, heard, and have a sense of autonomy and competence.

---

## The Making Progress Principle (and the Self-Test)

A **Harvard professor** proposed the **Making Progress Principle**: the recipe for motivation is meaningful work, clarity of goals, a supportive environment (mutual respect and empathy), fairness in rewards, a sense of autonomy–competence–relatedness, and *no* micromanagement. Its heart: people feel motivated when they are **both learning (competence) and growing up the hierarchy** at the same time.

The class then took the **Work Preference Inventory (WPI)** — a 30-item self-test. Fifteen items measure **intrinsic** orientation (*enjoyment + challenge*), the rest measure **extrinsic** orientation (*outward recognition + compensation*). Low scores on both signal amotivation.

A sharp student spotted that almost everyone scored "intrinsic." The professor named the culprit — **social desirability bias**: we rate ourselves on what sounds good, not on how we behave.

> **The professor:** "If you have a brain, you have biases. The statement looks positive, so you relate it with yourself; it looks negative, so why would I disclose something negative about myself? That's how our brain works."

:::note[Good to Know]
Your WPI result is an **orientation**, not a fixed personality trait. It shifts with **life stage, environment, stress and burnout**. Early career often skews extrinsic (you need the money); once "roti, kapra, makan" — food, clothing, shelter — is handled, many drift toward intrinsic, quality-of-work motivation. But you **cannot generalise** it; everyone is different.
:::

## Content vs Process Theories — the Map

The professor splits the field into two families. Learn this split first; it organises everything else.

| Family | Question it answers | Theories (this course) |
|---|---|---|
| **Content theories** | **What** motivates people — the *drivers* | Maslow's Hierarchy, Herzberg's Two-Factor, Theory of Needs, Self-Determination |
| **Process theories** | **How** motivation happens — the *mechanism* | Goal-Setting, Self-Efficacy, Reinforcement, Equity, Expectancy |

> **The professor:** "A theory is a set of ideas that explain human behaviour — and because we have all evolved the same way, these theories apply to everybody."

This session dives deep into the **content** theories; the **process** theories are previewed today and taught next session.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Content = WHAT drives, Process = HOW it flows"]
Content theories hand you the *ingredients* of motivation. Process theories explain the *cooking* — how effort turns into performance and reward.
:::

## Content Theory 1 — Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

**Concept.** Every job has two separate sets of factors:

- **Motivators** — *intrinsic* to the work: the content itself, achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, growth. These genuinely **motivate**.
- **Hygiene factors** — *extrinsic* to the work: salary, security, working conditions, relationship with the supervisor and colleagues, company policies. These only **prevent dissatisfaction**.

The twist that examiners love: **satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of one line.** Fixing hygiene removes *dis*satisfaction but never creates *satisfaction*.

> **The professor:** "Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction, but they don't motivate you. A good salary stops complaints — it doesn't create motivation."

**Class example — quiet quitting.** Why do people stay but do the bare minimum? Because **hygiene is good** (salary, boss, policies all fine, so they don't leave) but **motivators are missing** (the work itself is dull), so they are neither dissatisfied nor satisfied.

**The Retika case.** Retika, a new marketing analyst, has a decent salary, a modern office and clear instructions (**hygiene present**) — but her work is repetitive, recognition is scarce, and there is no path to grow (**motivators absent**). Diagnosis: classic two-factor gap. Fix: enrich the *work* — responsibility, a role change, growth — not the perks.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Herzberg = two separate scales.** Hygiene factors (salary, conditions, policies, supervision) can only move you from *dissatisfied* to *not dissatisfied*. Motivators (meaningful content, achievement, recognition, growth) move you from *not dissatisfied* to *motivated*. **Quiet quitting** = hygiene present + motivators absent. A good manager secures hygiene **and** adds motivators.
:::

## Content Theory 2 — Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

**Concept.** Proposed by **Abraham Maslow in 1943**, the most basic theory of human motivation: needs stack in a pyramid, and we satisfy lower needs before chasing higher ones.

| Level | Need | At work |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Self-actualization** | Achieving your full potential; passion-driven work |
| 4 | **Esteem** | Recognition, prestige, promotion, respect |
| 3 | **Belongingness** | Friendship, inclusion, collaborative projects |
| 2 | **Safety** | Job security, health benefits, a safe environment |
| 1 | **Physiological** | "Roti, kapra, makan" — pay for food, warmth, rest |

**Why it matters.** A fresh MBA hire first chases **financial stability and security**, then **belonging**, then **esteem (growth and recognition)**, and only later **self-actualization**. If someone is struggling with money or family, they cannot jump to the top of the pyramid.

> **The professor:** "We all are not sadhu. If you are struggling with money, can you go straight to self-actualization? That's why it's the top of the pyramid — you've achieved everything, now you want your full potential."

:::tip[Important]
Maslow is the **lens for reading life stage**. When a student asked why a team's "intent" keeps shifting — money now, status later, competition after a new hire — the professor's answer was: place them on Maslow. Their *position in the hierarchy* explains the change in motivation.
:::

## Content Theory 3 — The Theory of Needs (Acquired Needs)

**Concept.** We are all driven by three **acquired (learned)** needs; everyone has all three, but **one dominates**:

- **Need for achievement** — wants challenging goals, milestones, measurable success.
- **Need for affiliation** — wants good workplace relationships and belonging.
- **Need for power** — wants influence, status and control.

**Why it matters.** Match the work to the dominant need or motivation collapses. The professor's test line:

> **The professor:** "A high achiever wants challenging goals, not just a friendly team. Put them in a friendly team with no challenge — will they be motivated? No."

**The Vistora Pvt Ltd case.** Three managers promoted, three different styles:

| Manager | Behaviour | Dominant need |
|---|---|---|
| **Aditya** | Set ambitious targets, measured success, loved outperforming | **Achievement** |
| **Mira** | Built team harmony, inclusive spaces where everyone felt heard | **Affiliation** |
| **Karan** | Aligned with senior leadership, shaped company-wide strategy | **Power** |

Your dominant need even shapes your **leadership style**: need for **power** leans **directive/autocratic**; **achievement** leans **participative/democratic**; **affiliation** leans **empathy-based**.

**A detour into power.** Power is simply your **ability to influence others**, and it has two bases: **positional (formal)** power — "do it because I'm your boss" — and **personal (informal)** power — expertise ("do it because I know better") or charisma/trust ("do it because you admire me"). The best managers blend both; a full session on power and politics comes later.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "A-A-P: Achieve, Affiliate, Power"]
Three acquired needs — **A**chievement, **A**ffiliation, **P**ower. Everyone carries all three; find the one that *dominates* and design the work around it.
:::

## Content Theory 4 — Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

**Concept.** The most widely used theory of **intrinsic** motivation. We are **born** with three **innate** needs, and when all three are met, people act in self-determined, intrinsically motivated ways:

- **Autonomy** — freedom, ownership and responsibility over the work.
- **Competence** — learning and growth opportunities.
- **Relatedness** — a sense of belonging and good relationships.

**Why it matters.** Starve any one of the three and motivation dies, *even at high pay*. The professor hammered it: great salary but no growth for 15 years — not motivated; great salary but banned from talking to your team — not motivated.

**The Kavita case.** Kavita thrived as a product manager because all three needs were met — she had **autonomy** to lead, **recognition** (competence), and **strong team bonds** (relatedness). Under a new supervisor her independence was restricted, her skills underused, her team scattered. Despite the role's prestige, she disengaged and started job-hunting — proof that **unmet psychological needs break motivation even in high-status roles**.

**The crowding-out effect.** A crucial warning:

> **The professor:** "Too much focus on extrinsic motivation kills intrinsic motivation. If employees are paid for every single thing they do, they lose the internal drive to work hard."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**SDT = Autonomy + Competence + Relatedness (ACR), all three, or no intrinsic motivation.** Extrinsic rewards work only up to a point; beyond it, long-term motivation needs these three innate needs met. And beware the **crowding-out effect** — over-paying for everything *displaces* the intrinsic drive.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "ACR is the intrinsic core"]
**A**utonomy, **C**ompetence, **R**elatedness. If a case shows a motivated person suddenly going flat, check which of the three a new boss quietly took away.
:::

## The Process Theories (Previewed)

Through a rapid round of yes/no questions, the professor planted the five **process** theories to be taught next session — each one a lens on *how* effort becomes motivation:

| Process theory | Core idea (from the class questions) |
|---|---|
| **Goal-Setting** | **Specific + challenging** goals beat vague, easy ones; positive feedback boosts the drive |
| **Self-Efficacy** | If you don't believe in your ability, you won't take on the task — confidence feeds motivation |
| **Reinforcement** | Immediate praise and positive feedback reinforce the behaviour |
| **Equity** | **Fairness** in reward distribution; unfair rewards instantly demotivate |
| **Expectancy** | Motivation holds only if people believe **effort leads to reward** (and credit isn't stolen) |

## Supporting Thread — Big Five × Motivation

The professor linked personality to drive: **conscientiousness is the strongest predictor of motivation** (these people are self-driven and intrinsically motivated — you don't need to push them). **Openness** craves creative, novel work; low-openness people are motivated by conventional, non-creative tasks. **Extraverts** are motivated by networking opportunities; **neurotic** individuals need stable, reassuring environments.

> **The professor:** "If a person high on conscientiousness is demotivated in your team, it means there's a problem in your team."

---

## The Toolkit Takeaway

The practical close: you cannot change the *content* of everyone's work to please them. What you **can** do is cheap and repeatable — **remind people why their work matters**, give a little **autonomy and responsibility**, show how it feeds **their growth**, and build **genuine relationships**. A five-minute Monday conversation can shift motivation more than a bonus.

> **The professor:** "Nobody suffers because of the work. We suffer because of people relations or hygiene factors."

Awareness is the first win; the real skill is **experimenting** — one approach never fits everyone.

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 9 reframes a manager's job: stop asking "what reward will make them work?" and start asking "**what do they actually need?**" Hygiene keeps the peace; motivators light the fire. Place a person on Maslow to read their life stage, spot their dominant acquired need, and protect their autonomy, competence and relatedness. Do that, and you motivate without overspending — because, as the clip at the start showed, sometimes a person will say no to a raise and yes to meaning.
