# Session 4: The Phone Call That Exposed a People Problem

*A trivial phone call to a big client blows up into a career problem. The real lesson of Session 4 is that technical brilliance is worthless if you cannot influence the people you have no power over.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **case-study analysis method** (problem statement → protagonist → key players → organization → action alternatives → pros/cons → pragmatic plan); the session thesis — **technical skill without people skill makes you an ineffective manager**; **influence without formal authority** (influence tactics — consultation, inspirational appeal); the pragmatic plan — **Burns first, then the client, then keep the boss informed**.

**Important (supporting):** **Uncertainty** as the defining feature of a case; **micromanagement** as a negative leadership style; the **SWOT** of the protagonist; pros and cons of **involving the boss**; **emotional intelligence (empathy)** and **political astuteness** as the two missing people skills; same-hierarchy means you **cannot directly confront**.

**Context (colour for case answers):** The phone-call incident; Rogers as "the hub of the wheel"; Burns argumentative when cornered; Jim Paglia being relationship-focused; advisors Robert Sparksman and Mary Stewart; the **Michael Peterson** parallel; OB as a **micro-level** (individual), not macro-level, discipline.
:::

## Quick Recap

This session was a **case class**, not a lecture. The professor opened by making the class recall the fundamentals: what a case is, and the steps of a good case-study analysis. The case for the evening was **Amelia Rogers at Tassani Communications (TC)** — and it quietly delivers the central message of the whole Organizational Behaviour course.

> **The professor:** "A case is a real or imagined business scenario which presents a problem that needs to be addressed. It involves uncertainty — there is some incomplete information, and you have to make a decision keeping that uncertainty in mind."

---

## What Is a Case? And Why Uncertainty Is the Point

A **case** is a real or imagined business situation with a problem to solve. Its one peculiar feature: **uncertainty**. You never have complete information, yet you must still decide. In this case, Rogers knows the client is upset — but she does **not** know what was actually said on the call. That missing piece *is* the uncertainty.

The professor then drilled the **steps of a good case-study analysis**. This is the skeleton you reuse for every OB case and almost certainly in the exam.

| Step | What you do | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem statement | Name the real problem and who faces it (the **protagonist**) | Rogers must handle Burns's unauthorised call to the client |
| 2. Protagonist profile | Strengths, weaknesses, characteristics | Rogers: strong technical skill + client rapport; weak colleague rapport; micromanager |
| 3. Other key players | Who is directly or indirectly involved + their traits | Burns, Johnson (client), Paglia (boss) |
| 4. Organization | The context where the case unfolds | An ad agency where accounts and creative teams must collaborate |
| 5. Chronology (optional) | Sequence of events for background | The phone-call incident and what led to it |
| 6. Action alternatives | Several options — **never just one** | Talk to Burns / call the client / involve the boss |
| 7. Pros, cons and pick | Weigh each, then justify the **most pragmatic plan** | Burns → client → keep Paglia informed |

> **The professor:** "We never solve a case by proposing a single action alternative. We write down different action alternatives, and from them we select the most pragmatic plan of action."

A subtle but testable point: **you are always the protagonist.** You analyse the case as the person who has to act.

> **The professor:** "In every case, you are the one who is solving the case — you are the protagonist."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Memorise the **analysis spine**: **Problem statement → Protagonist → Key players → Organization → Action alternatives → Pros/cons → Pragmatic plan.** You cannot judge the pros and cons of any option until you understand the **characteristics** of the protagonist, the other players, and the organization. That is why character analysis comes *before* the action plan.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Problem, People, Place, Plans, Pick"]
Five beats of any case answer: **Problem** (statement) → **People** (protagonist + players) → **Place** (the organization) → **Plans** (several alternatives) → **Pick** (the most pragmatic, justified).
:::

## The Case in Sixty Seconds: Rogers vs Burns

**David Burns**, the **Creative Director**, phoned the client **Johnson** (at the highly visible **Mufflerman** account) **directly** — bypassing **Amelia Rogers**, the **Account Director** who owns that client relationship. Johnson then called Rogers to complain. In this agency, the rule is that creatives should route client communication **through** the account director. The client is now upset.

**The problem statement:** Rogers must decide three things —
1. **What to do about David Burns** (her colleague).
2. **What to do about the client** Johnson.
3. **Whether the matter is serious enough to involve her boss**, Jim Paglia.

The professor split the challenge in two:
- **Immediate challenge** — fix the phone-call fallout before a key client walks.
- **Long-term challenge** — introspection: *why* did Burns go over her head? So she never faces this again.

:::tip[Important]
A strong case answer always separates the **immediate** fire-fight from the **long-term** root cause. Here the immediate issue is client damage control; the long-term issue is Rogers's own people skills. Examiners reward students who address **both** layers, not just the crisis.
:::

---

## What's at Stake, and Where the Uncertainty Sits

Why is "just a phone call" serious? Because of what it threatens:

- **Reputation and revenue** — Mufflerman is a highly visible client; good work brings more work.
- **Rogers's internal credibility** — Paglia and the firm trusted her with this account; delivering proves her and opens career doors; failing damages her standing.
- **Cross-functional collaboration** — a healthy relationship between the **accounts** team and the **creative** team is essential to deliver on time.
- **Client satisfaction** — the client is already unhappy.

And the **uncertainty**: Rogers knows the client is upset, but she does **not** know what transpired between Burns and Johnson. That gap must be closed before she acts.

---

## Know Your Characters: Work Styles That Collide

Most OB cases turn on a **clash of work styles**. Map the cast before you plan anything.

| Person | Role | Key traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Amelia Rogers** | Account Director (protagonist) | Technically strong, hardworking, excellent rapport with **clients** — but buttoned-up, rigid, demanding, a **micromanager** with poor rapport with **colleagues** |
| **David Burns** | Creative Director | Wants **liberty and autonomy**; can be argumentative and irritable when cornered; believes clients "play it safe," so it is his job to push bold creative ideas |
| **Johnson** | Client contact at Mufflerman | A highly visible client; upset that a creative contacted him directly |
| **Jim Paglia** | Rogers's boss | **Relationship-focused**; wants teams to collaborate; encourages healthy confrontation to get the best output |
| **Sal Tassani** | Agency founder | Not directly involved in this incident |
| **Robert Sparksman / Mary Stewart** | Potential advisors | Had worked with Johnson / with Burns before; can reveal if this is a one-off |

The structural trap: Rogers and Burns are at the **same hierarchy**. Neither has **formal authority** over the other, and their relationship is already strained. So Rogers **cannot simply confront or reprimand** Burns — that would worsen the relationship and hurt the quality of the work they must produce together.

Rogers saw herself as **"the hub of the wheel"** — everything routed through her — which is exactly why she micromanaged.

> **The professor:** "Micromanagement is a negative leadership style — you give directives, you don't involve others in decision-making, you don't let them do their own work. And it negatively impacts other employees."

:::note[Good to Know]
Jim Paglia being **relationship-focused** changes the whole calculus. He is not interested in Rogers's rigid work style; he cares whether the teams get along. That is why dragging him in can backfire — if he learns two departments cannot cooperate, **both** Rogers and Burns may be marked down, not just Burns.
:::

## SWOT for the Protagonist

The professor ran a quick **SWOT** (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) on Rogers — a clean, exam-ready tool for any protagonist.

| SWOT | For Amelia Rogers |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | Strong technical skill; hardworking and diligent; excellent client rapport |
| **Weaknesses** | Buttoned-up, tough, rigid, demanding; micromanages; poor relationships with colleagues |
| **Opportunities** | Deliver the project → prove herself to the boss, win more Mufflerman work, earn career growth |
| **Threats** | Lose a key client; lose the boss's trust; ruin the working relationship with Burns; break accounts–creative collaboration |

Notice how the weaknesses (micromanagement, poor colleague rapport) are the **likely reason** Burns bypassed her in the first place. The SWOT quietly points straight at the root cause.

## The Pragmatic Plan: Burns → Client → Boss

With characters mapped, the class built and weighed **action alternatives**. The agreed sequence:

**Step 1 — Talk to Burns first.** Close the uncertainty: understand *his* side before judging. Crucially, **do not "confront"** him — same hierarchy, strained relationship. Use a curious, collaborative, non-accusatory tone and clarify the communication norm going forward.

> **The professor:** "Her tone should be very curious, collaborative and non-accusatory."

A sample opener the professor dictated: *"Hey Burns, I heard you spoke with Johnson directly — I just wanted to understand the context. Normally I'd expect us to sync first so we present a united front to the client. Can we talk through the best way to coordinate going forward?"*

**Step 2 — Damage control with the client.** Call Johnson, reassure him the situation is under control, keep it to a calm, non-complaining tone, and **do not blame** Burns. If the firm ignores the client, it signals the organization is not serious — and a highly visible account could walk.

**Step 3 — Decide on the boss.** This is the real judgement call. Should Rogers involve **Jim Paglia**?

| Involve Jim Paglia? | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Yes** | He has **formal authority** over Burns; being proactive protects her if the client tells him first; he can intervene if Burns repeats | Relationship-focused Paglia may be upset the teams don't collaborate; Burns may feel **cornered** and the relationship worsens; Paglia may discover the root cause is Rogers's own micromanagement — hurting **her** career |
| **No** | Keeps the matter contained; avoids needless escalation; preserves the Burns relationship | If it escalates and is left unmanaged, they may lose an important client |

**The balanced verdict:** If Burns's explanation is reasonable and the call was a genuine one-off, **don't** escalate — it would only strain things. If it is likely to repeat, **proactively keep Paglia in the loop** (because the client might reach him anyway) and seek his advice — but framed as information, not a complaint.

> **The professor:** "She's not complaining about her colleague — she's just keeping him in the loop and seeking his advice."

She can even reassure Paglia that **his intervention is not required** yet; only if Burns contacts the client *again* should she request Paglia to use his formal authority.

**Other advisors:** Rogers can quietly consult **Robert Sparksman** (who knows the client — is Johnson an over-reactor?) and **Mary Stewart** (who knows Burns — is this a habit or a one-off?). Gathering this reduces the uncertainty before she commits.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Burns → Buyer → Boss"]
The pragmatic plan in three beats: calm the **colleague** (Burns), reassure the **buyer** (client Johnson), then keep the **boss** (Paglia) informed — escalating only if it repeats.
:::

---

## The Real Lesson: Influence Without Authority

Here is where the case stops being about one phone call. Rogers has **no formal authority** over Burns, yet she must get him to change behaviour. The professor's answer: use **influence tactics**.

| Influence tactic | What Rogers can do |
|---|---|
| **Consultation** | Involve Burns in decisions and seek his inputs — he craves liberty, so stop micromanaging him |
| **Inspirational appeal** | Emphasise shared goals — show how on-time delivery benefits *both* teams and the whole agency |

> **The professor:** "Can you confront somebody who is on the same hierarchy as you?"

The point of the rhetorical question: you can't. When you lack positional power, **persuasion replaces command**. The professor also laid out the mindset for working in cross-functional teams — worth full marks on a teamwork question.

:::note[Good to Know — Cross-Functional Teamwork Rules]
When you work with partners you don't control: **believe in your partner's competence and best motives**, trust them, try to understand their perspective, **bring problems to their attention promptly**, and be honest about what you are doing. And when the organization's welfare is at stake, employees should **let go of their conflicts** and work together.
:::

---

## Technical Skill Is Not Enough: The People-Skills Gap

The closing message — and the thesis of the session — is blunt:

> **The professor:** "Technical competence without people skill can make you an ineffective leader or manager."

Rogers's failure was **not** about capability. She is technically sound and great with clients. She fell short because she could not **understand Burns's expectations**, could not **build a good working relationship**, and therefore could not **influence** him or build cross-functional support. That is why he went around her.

The professor named the **two missing people skills**, both flagged for later sessions:

- **Emotional intelligence (especially empathy)** — she could not sense Burns's frustration with her management style, which killed her ability to influence him. *(Detailed in Session 11.)*
- **Political astuteness (social astuteness)** — she could not read organizational cues like Burns's need for autonomy; she had weak interpersonal influence and poor rapport. *(Detailed in Session 15.)*

This echoes the earlier **Michael Peterson** case: Peterson too was conscientious and technically strong, but landed in trouble because of weak people skills (his overly open, direct communication). **Same disease, different patient.**

The professor also drew a boundary for the whole course:

> **The professor:** "The focus of IGD is to understand employee behaviour in organizations — these cases are at the micro level, not the macro level."

So don't try to fix a case like this by changing **organizational norms**. The root cause is **individual** — a mismatch of characteristics and management styles — and the fix is Rogers improving her people skills.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The one-line thesis to carry into the exam: **an effective manager needs a perfect blend of technical skill *and* people skill.** Both Michael Peterson and Amelia Rogers were technically excellent and still failed — purely on people skills. If a case shows a capable person who is nonetheless "in trouble," the answer is almost always a **people-skills / influence / relationship** gap, not a competence gap.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Competence gets you in; chemistry keeps you effective"]
Technical skill gets you hired and respected. People skills — empathy, influence, relationships — are what actually let you get work done through others. Lose the second half and the first half stops mattering.
:::

## Key Terms Table (Quick Revision)

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Case** | A real or imagined business scenario with a problem to solve, always under **uncertainty** |
| **Problem statement** | The core decision the protagonist must make |
| **Protagonist** | The person facing (and solving) the problem — in analysis, that's you |
| **Action alternatives** | Several candidate solutions — never settle on just one |
| **Pragmatic plan of action** | The best, most workable option, chosen by weighing pros and cons |
| **SWOT** | Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats of the protagonist |
| **Micromanagement** | A negative leadership style — giving directives, excluding others from decisions |
| **Formal authority** | Positional power; absent between same-hierarchy peers like Rogers and Burns |
| **Influence tactics** | Ways to persuade without authority — e.g., consultation, inspirational appeal |
| **Emotional intelligence** | Understanding and managing emotions, especially empathy for others |
| **Political astuteness** | Reading organizational cues and building influence and rapport |
| **People skill** | The ability to understand, relate to, and influence colleagues |
| **Micro vs macro (OB)** | IGD studies the **individual** in the organization, not organization-wide structure |
