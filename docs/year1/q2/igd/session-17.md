# Session 17: Born or Made? The Leader Who Changes Gears

*Are leaders born or trained? Mostly trained — because leadership is a skill you can build. And the sharpest leaders never lock into one style; they carry a whole toolkit and switch to fit the situation, the task and the people in front of them.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The definition — **leadership is a skill** to influence/inspire/motivate others toward organizational goals (so it can be **trained**); the **three theories** (Trait → Behavioural → Contingency); the **Managerial Grid** (Team style wins); **Big Five link** — **conscientiousness = effectiveness, extraversion = emergence**; the **Full Range Model** (Laissez-faire → Transactional → Transformational) and the **Four I's** of transformational leadership; **warmth + competence**; **Lewin's three styles** (autocratic / democratic / laissez-faire) and **"no single best style"**; **Responsible leadership** (Authentic, Ethical, Servant); **abusive supervision**.

**Important (supporting):** Leader vs Manager; the **Self + Others** halves of a leader; **Situational Leadership** (directing / coaching / supporting / delegating); transactional sub-styles (contingent reward, management by exception); charismatic & authoritative styles; **leadership emergence vs effectiveness**; demographic **boundary conditions** (generation, gender).

**Context (colour for answers):** Ratan Tata / Tata group (ethical), Satya Nadella (transformational; Microsoft "know-it-all → learn-it-all"), Steve Jobs (autocratic), Warren Buffett (laissez-faire), Bill Gates (autocratic → transformational → servant), APJ Abdul Kalam & Narayana Murthy (authentic), Gandhi / Mandela / Mother Teresa (servant), PM Modi (charismatic); the in-class **style-spotting drill**.
:::

## Setting the Stage

After the previous session's case — the technically brilliant star performer **denied promotion for weak people skills**, who had to be *coached* over a year into balancing task with people — this class zooms out to leadership itself. The professor's plan for the evening: define leadership, list the qualities of a good leader, walk the **three theories**, tour every major **style** (with a business leader for each), connect leadership to the **Big Five**, and finish with two activities — one to spot styles in scenarios, one to find *your* default style.

The spine of the whole session is a single idea: there is **no one-size-fits-all** leader. Great leaders read the moment and change gears.

---

## What Leadership Really Is — and Why It Can Be Learned

The professor keeps the definition deliberately human.

> **The professor:** "In simple human language, leadership is the art — or the process, or a set of skills — to influence, inspire or motivate others to achieve the organizational goals. And since leadership is a skill, people can be trained to be leaders."

That last line is the hinge of the session. If leadership were a fixed trait, training would be pointless. Because it is a **skill**, anyone can build it.

**Leader vs Manager.** Organizations need both. A **manager** manages tasks; a **leader** creates and articulates a **compelling vision and direction**.

> **The professor:** "All leaders are managers, but not all managers are leaders. A leader can lead *plus* manage — but not every manager can provide direction to the organization; that needs a different skill set."

A student sharpened this: some people inspire brilliantly yet manage poorly, because the skills differ. The professor held the framing — leading includes managing, but managing doesn't automatically make you a leader.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Managers mind the task; leaders mind the direction"]
A manager keeps the train running on today's track. A leader decides **which track** the train should be on. Every leader can mind the task; not every task-minder can set the direction.
:::

## The Two Halves of a Leader: Self + Others

Before styles and theories, the professor splits leadership into **Self** and **Others**.

> **The professor:** "If you are not self-motivated and self-aware, can you motivate others? No. So the first quality of any good leader is that they are self-aware and self-motivated."

- **Leading Self** = knowing your own P-factors — strengths, weaknesses, personality, values, motivations, biases, emotions and blind spots (a direct callback to Session 1).
- **Leading Others** = **authenticity** (honesty, empathy, fairness), creating vision, inspiring and motivating, **leading by example** as a role model, adaptability under uncertainty, and strong **decision-making** and **communication**.

**Why it matters:** you cannot pour from an empty cup. Self-awareness is the licence to lead anyone else.

## Three Theories: Born, Made, or "It Depends"

This is a near-certain exam question. Learn all three as a set.

| Theory | Core idea | One line | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trait theory** | Leaders are **born** with it (the "Great Man" idea) | Leadership is **innate** | **Outdated** |
| **Behavioural theory** | Leaders are **made** — trained to balance **task + people** | Leadership is a **behaviour** you learn | Current |
| **Contingency theory** | **Effectiveness depends on the situation** + followers | **No single best style** | Current (the big one) |

> **The professor:** "Trait theory says leaders are born, not made — but these are the outdated theories. Behavioural theory says leaders are *made*: anyone can be trained to be good by focusing on both the task and the people doing the task. Contingency says we cannot pick one style and call it best — effectiveness depends on the situation and the characteristics of the followers."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Trait = born. Behavioural = made. Contingency = it depends.** If a case asks "is this person a 'natural' leader?", the modern answer leans **behavioural + contingency**: leadership is a trainable skill, and the *right* style depends on the situation and the followers. Trait theory is the one you name as **outdated**.
:::

## The Managerial Grid: Balancing Task and People

The behavioural camp's signature tool is the **Managerial Grid** (Blake & Mouton, early 1960s — external scholars referenced in class). Plot a leader on two axes: **concern for people** and **concern for production (task)**.

| Style | Concern for people | Concern for task | What you get |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Impoverished** | Low | Low | Bare minimum; just enough to get by |
| **Country Club** | High | Low | Friendly, happy office — but goals neglected |
| **Authority–Compliance (Task)** | Low | High | Work gets done, but employees are unhappy |
| **Middle of the Road** | Moderate | Moderate | Mediocre people *and* mediocre results |
| **Team** | High | High | **Best** — people are happy *and* goals are hit |

> **The professor:** "Who is a good leader? Somebody who can create a balance — who can focus both on the task and on the people doing the task."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "High-High wins"]
On the grid, the **Team** style (high people + high task) is the champion. Low-low is "impoverished," and anything that maxes one axis while starving the other eventually breaks. Aim for the **top-right corner**.
:::

## Big Five and Leadership: Emergence vs Effectiveness

The professor links the OCEAN traits to leadership — and makes one distinction that students miss.

- **Conscientiousness** (efficient, dependable, achievement-oriented, persistent, organized) = the **strongest predictor of leadership *effectiveness***.
- **Extraversion** (sociable, assertive, strong communication) = the **best predictor of leadership *emergence***.
- Then **openness**, **agreeableness**, and **emotional stability** (low neuroticism).

> **The professor:** "Extraversion is related to leadership *emergence* — extraverted people are more likely to emerge as leaders. But conscientiousness is related to leadership *effectiveness*. Emergence does not guarantee effectiveness."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Extraversion gets you *noticed* as a leader (emergence); conscientiousness makes you a *good* leader (effectiveness).** The loud, confident person often rises first — but the dependable, achievement-oriented person actually delivers. In an exam, this pair is worth full marks: *emergence ≠ effectiveness.*
:::

## The Full Range Leadership Model: Passive to Active

Now the style tour. The **Full Range Leadership Model** lays styles on a spectrum from **passive** to **active**:

**Laissez-faire → Transactional → Transformational** (least to most active/effective).

### Transformational (the most effective)

The leader's focus is to **bring positive change in the followers** — not just extract work, but *transform the people*. It runs on the **Four I's**:

| The Four I's | What the leader does |
|---|---|
| **Idealized Influence** | Acts as a **role model** — charisma, confidence, clarity of thought, competence, leads by example |
| **Inspirational Motivation** | Creates and articulates a **compelling vision**, giving work meaning and focus |
| **Intellectual Stimulation** | **Encourages creativity and innovation**; supplies resources, information, knowledge |
| **Individualized Consideration** | Shows **genuine care** for each follower's well-being; coaches and mentors |

Transformational leadership is prized because it combines **warmth + competence** — the **Stereotype Content Model** the professor flagged.

> **The professor:** "When leaders are both warm *and* competent, they earn their followers' trust, respect and love. That's why transformational leadership is one of the most effective styles."

**The class example — Satya Nadella:** empathetic *and* competent, he shifted Microsoft's culture from a **"know-it-all" to a "learn-it-all."** Contrast **Steve Jobs** — hugely competent, but often low on warmth.

### Transactional (exchange-based)

Classic **carrot-and-stick**: work hard, get rewarded; slack, get punished. Its sub-styles:

- **Contingent reward** — reward or penalty tied to performance.
- **Management by exception (active)** — monitors performance, corrects mistakes early.
- **Management by exception (passive)** — steps in only after a problem appears.

It lifts **productivity** but tends to **ignore follower well-being**. (Class scenario: the project manager who sets milestones, monitors daily, and rewards or penalizes on deadlines.)

### Laissez-faire (hands-off)

The leader hands over **freedom and authority** and stays out of the way.

> **The professor:** "Warren Buffett is considered a successful laissez-faire leader — 'I hire people who can perform their task adequately without my help.'"

It suits highly skilled, self-driven teams and creative work. But watch the motive: a student noted it can signal **high trust** — or, less flatteringly, a leader who simply **isn't interested**.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Let-them-be, Deal, Transform"]
Full Range, left to right: **L**aissez-faire (let them be) → **T**ransactional (make a deal) → **T**ransformational (change them). Passive to active, weakest to strongest.
:::

## Lewin's Three Styles: Who Makes the Call?

A second, older map (Kurt Lewin's framework, 1940s — external) sorts leaders by **who decides**:

| Style | Mantra | How decisions are made |
|---|---|---|
| **Autocratic** | "Do as I say" | Leader decides alone, minimal input |
| **Democratic / Participative** | "Let's decide together" | Seeks and weighs team input |
| **Laissez-faire** | "Do whatever you want" | Team is free to decide |

**Autocratic example — Steve Jobs:** *"Apple is my brainchild, so I'm responsible for making all the decisions."* When the class voted democratic as "best," the professor pushed back:

> **The professor:** "There is no best style — it depends on the situation and the characteristics of the followers."

- **Highly skilled, experienced team / structured task** → laissez-faire or delegate.
- **Crisis, emergency, or a very complex call** → autocratic.
- **Need innovation and diverse perspectives** → democratic.

**Situational Leadership** (directing / coaching / supporting / delegating) refines this by **follower competence + commitment**: low competence but willing → **directing**; some competence, low motivation → **coaching**; high competence, low motivation → **supporting/participative**; high competence and committed → **delegating**.

## Responsible Leadership: Authentic, Ethical, Servant

A third family puts **ethics, values and service** at the centre — the professor's umbrella of **responsible leadership**.

| Style | Focus | Remember by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | **Authenticity** — self-aware, transparent, "same in and out" | Doesn't hide mistakes or weaknesses | APJ Abdul Kalam, Narayana Murthy |
| **Ethical** | **Doing what is right** — honesty, fairness, integrity, respect | Won't bend the rules for gain | Tata group |
| **Servant** | **Well-being and growth of people above profit** (selfless) | "How can I help my people?" | Mother Teresa, Gandhi, Mandela |

> **The professor:** "Authentic leaders are the same inside and out — they don't hide their mistakes or their weaknesses. Servant leadership is the selfless approach: the focus is on the growth and well-being of the employees, not on profit."

Authentic leadership's four parts: **self-awareness, balanced processing** (gather inputs before deciding), **relational transparency** (openness), and an **internalized moral perspective** (do what's right, not what's easy).

:::note[Good to Know — Bill Gates Changed Styles Over a Career]
The professor used **Bill Gates** as the textbook "style traveller": **autocratic** early on, then **transformational**, and now — through philanthropy — **servant**. One person, three styles across one life. Proof that style is a choice, not a cage.
:::

**Two quick extras** she mapped:
- **Charismatic** — pulls people through confidence, energy, clarity and communication (class example: **PM Modi**).
- **Authoritative / Visionary** — sets a clear vision and guides the team, allows some flexibility, but keeps the final call.

## The Dark Side: Abusive Supervision & Workplace Bullying

Not all leadership is constructive. **Abusive supervision** is **sustained hostile** behaviour, verbal and non-verbal: taking credit for your work, demeaning or shouting, **micromanagement**, excessive workload with punishing deadlines, and **intrusion into personal space and time** (expecting weekend and after-hours work).

> **The professor:** "Around 80 to 90% of supervisors engage in some abusive supervision, and 70 to 80% of employees leave their organizations because of it."

Which trait sits behind it? **Neuroticism** — anger, hostility, vulnerability, insecurity. Insecure bosses abuse to protect themselves.

**A sharp student question:** where is the line between a caring boss and an intrusive one — if the *same* behaviour feels like empathy to three team members and intrusion to two? The professor's answer tied back to Session 1's **person × situation** idea:

> **The professor:** "It's about sustained *hostile* behaviour. Impact is different from hostility. A leader asking you for extra time because you share a good relationship is not the same as a supervisor misusing power to show hostility. And how you perceive it depends on *your* personality — a conscientious employee may read the same push very differently."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Abusive supervision = sustained hostile behaviour that negatively harms the follower over time** — and it is a leading cause of attrition. The tell is **hostility + sustained + harmful**, driven by a supervisor's **insecurity/neuroticism**. Don't confuse a demanding-but-respectful boss with an abusive one; the difference is **hostility and misuse of power**, not merely high expectations.
:::

## The Big Lesson: Carry a Toolkit, Switch by Situation

Pull it all together and the session lands on one message.

> **The professor:** "Great leaders don't pick one style. They have a leadership toolkit — they keep switching their style depending on the situation. That's contingency."

- **Crisis** → autocratic. **Routine task** → transactional. **Need deep change** → transformational. **Want a supportive, ethical culture** → responsible (authentic / ethical / servant). **Want creativity** → laissez-faire / transformational.

And the right style also shifts with **boundary conditions** — demographic factors like **generation, gender and educational background**. The professor's live example: the fear-driven abusive style that "worked" in the 1990s or early 2000s **does not land with Gen Z** today.

:::tip[Important — "Default style" is a starting point, not a destiny]
The in-class test (Lewin's three styles) reveals your **default** leaning — most people come out a **combination**. But a framework that captures only three styles is just a mirror, not the whole map. The real skill is **awareness of many styles** plus the judgement to **switch**. That is exactly what contingency theory demands.
:::

## Spotting Styles in the Wild (Exam Drill)

The class matched scenarios to styles. These make excellent one-line exam illustrations:

| Scenario | Style |
|---|---|
| Asks all members for ideas, encourages debate, then decides on the input | **Democratic / Participative** |
| Rarely instructs; lets an expert team decide how to work | **Laissez-faire** |
| Shares a vision, mentors, pushes the team to exceed targets | **Transformational** |
| Sets milestones, monitors daily, rewards/penalizes on deadlines | **Transactional** |
| Listens to concerns, ensures tools and support; morale rises | **Servant** |
| Reports inflated revenue honestly, even if it delays bonuses | **Ethical** |
| Shares successes *and* failures, admits she lacks all the answers | **Authentic** |
| Inspires with energy, confidence and a compelling vision | **Charismatic / Transformational** |
| Sets a clear vision and guides, but keeps the final decision | **Authoritative / Visionary** |
| Criticizes harshly and publicly, blames people; team is fearful | **Abusive supervision** |
