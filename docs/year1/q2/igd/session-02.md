## What Makes You *You*? The Nature vs Nurture Debate

**Concept.** Ask "what makes you who you are — your genes, or your upbringing?" and you get three answers:

| View | Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Your **genes** decide your personality and behaviour | Too narrow |
| **Nurture** | Born a **blank slate**; experience, parenting, culture shape everything | Too narrow |
| **Interactionist** | A **complex interplay** of genes **and** conditioning | The accepted view |

**The proof — identical twins.** Separate identical twins (same genetic material) at birth; twenty years later they have very **different** personalities. So some of you is genes, some is environment — and the interplay is so tangled you can't cleanly separate them.

**The business examples.** The professor reads two CEOs through this lens:
- **Satya Nadella** — intelligence and curiosity look like **nature** (IAS-officer father, scholar mother); global exposure, mentoring by Bill Gates and Steve Ballmer, and life experience were the **nurture** that turned potential into leadership.
- **Sundar Pichai** — sharp memory and humility look like **nature** (a humble middle-class background he often credits); IIT Kharagpur, Stanford, and big opportunities at Google were the **nurture**.

**Why it matters.** Nature sets your **potential**; nurture decides whether that potential ever **unfolds**. A child born musical who never gets training may never become a musician.

> **The professor:** "Leadership potential may be born, but leadership capability is made."

:::note[Good to Know]
The class activity drove this home: write your **3–4 core strengths** and tag each as **nature**, **nurture**, or **both**. Students found most of their strengths (humility, curiosity, interpersonal skills, decisiveness) were a **mix** — inherited seeds watered by experience. That "both" answer *is* the interactionist view in action.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Nature deals the cards, nurture plays them"]
Genes deal your hand; your environment decides how well that hand gets played. Neither alone wins the game.
:::

## The Master Equation: Behaviour = f(Person, Environment)

**Concept.** This is **Kurt Lewin's equation** and the spine of the course: **Behaviour = f(Person × Environment)**. How you act at work is a function of **who you are** (your personality, your "P factor") interacting with the **characteristics of your organisation** — its culture, climate, leadership style, politics, justice, and HR practices.

**The example that makes it click.** Put **two employees under the same empowering leader** (a leader who hands out autonomy, responsibility, and decision-making power). The environment is **identical**. Yet:
- The one **high on conscientiousness** (hardworking, dependable, rule-following) seizes the autonomy and *raises* task performance and innovative work behaviour.
- The one **low on conscientiousness** (disorganised, easily distracted, dislikes responsibility) is pushed toward **deviant behaviour** and time theft.

Same environment, opposite behaviour — because the **P factor** differs.

**Why it matters.** This is the exam-ready answer to "why do people in the same office behave so differently?" and the reason **one-size-fits-all people management fails**: everyone is unique, and that uniqueness lives in personality.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Behaviour = f(Person, Environment)** — Kurt Lewin. Hold the environment constant (same boss, same culture) and people **still** behave differently because of their personalities. If a question asks why one person thrives and another fails under the *same* manager, name the equation and point to the **P factor**.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "B = PE"]
**B**ehaviour equals **P**erson meeting **E**nvironment. Change either side and the behaviour changes.
:::

## Behaviour *in Organisations*: The OB Vocabulary

**Concept.** OB has its own set of named behaviours. The test for "is it a behaviour?" is simple: **can others observe it?** If yes, it's behaviour.

| Behaviour | What it means | Why it counts as behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Task performance** | Meeting your job's performance expectations | Your output is visible |
| **Innovative work behaviour (IWB)** | **Creating AND implementing** new ideas | Implementation is visible |
| **Employee voice** | Speaking up with suggestions to fix problems | Others hear the suggestion |
| **Organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB)** | Helping colleagues beyond your job description | The helping act is visible |
| **Deviant / counterproductive behaviour (CWB)** | Time theft, long breaks, lateness — acts that hurt productivity | Observable actions |
| **Absenteeism** | Not turning up regularly | Visible absence |
| **Turnover** | Actually leaving the organisation | A visible exit |

**Why it matters.** These are the raw materials of every OB exam answer. **OCB** is the clean favourite: helping that is **not** in your job description but you do it **willingly**. **Time theft** (long breaks, coming late, leaving early) is the everyday **counterproductive** behaviour almost everyone has brushed against.

## The Thin Line: Attitude vs Behaviour

**Concept.** If you can **see** it, it's a **behaviour**. If it lives **inside the head**, it's an **attitude** (your mind's predisposition toward something). Next session goes deeper on attitudes — but the dividing line is tonight's takeaway.

| Attitude (inside the head) | Behaviour (visible to others) |
|---|---|
| **Job satisfaction** | Task performance |
| **Work engagement / commitment** | Employee voice |
| **Creativity** (idea in your head) | **Innovation** (idea implemented) |
| **Turnover intention** (thinking of leaving) | **Turnover** (actually leaving) |

**The examples.** You **cannot** tell by looking whether someone is satisfied with their job — satisfaction is an **evaluative statement** in their mind, an attitude. But you **can** see whether they hit targets, speak up, help others, or quit.

> **The professor:** "Creativity is creating new ideas; innovation is implementing those ideas to improve the work process."

**Why it matters.** Two exam-perfect pairs fall straight out of this line: **creativity (attitude) to innovation (behaviour)**, and **turnover intention (attitude) to turnover (behaviour)**. The moment a thought becomes *visible action*, it crosses from attitude to behaviour.

:::tip[Important]
Keep the **creativity vs innovation** and **turnover intention vs turnover** pairs ready. Each pair is the *same idea* on both sides of the visibility line — the invisible, in-the-head version is the **attitude**; the observable, acted-out version is the **behaviour**. Examiners love testing exactly this boundary.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Seen = Behaviour, Hidden = Attitude"]
If a colleague could point at it, it's behaviour. If it's only in your head, it's an attitude.
:::

## How to Crack an OB Case (The Method)

Before the first case, the professor lays out the **toolkit for every case** in this course. You step into the shoes of the protagonist — and solve it with *their* traits, not yours.

> **The professor:** "You cannot solve the case as yourself. For solving that case, you are Michael Peterson — and he has certain characteristics you must respect."

**The steps:**
1. **Problem** — state the core problem/decision the case turns on.
2. **Protagonist** — who faces it, and their personality, strengths, and weaknesses.
3. **Players** — other people **directly** or **indirectly** linked to the problem, and their traits.
4. **Place** — the **organisation's** characteristics (and the broader economy). Never overlook these.
5. **Progression** — the chronology of events: what happened, when.
6. **Plans** — generate **three or four** action alternatives (not one).
7. **Pros/cons to Pick** — weigh each alternative, then choose and **justify** the best, most pragmatic one.

A **SWOT** (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) on the protagonist is a handy add-on tool.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Crack a case with P's"]
**Problem → Protagonist → Players → Place → Progression → Plans → Pick.** Seven P's and your analysis is built.
:::

## Case Study: Michael Peterson — The Star Engineer Who Couldn't Read the Room

**The setup.** **Michael Peterson** works at **Confederation Kitchen**, a company downsizing amid declining profits and structural change. A rumour says he may soon report to **John MacTier**, a supervisor he clashed with in 2002. Tomorrow he meets his likely new manager, **William Peak**. **The decision: should Peterson voice his concerns about the rumour to William Peak — or stay quiet?**

### The players

| Player | Relation to the problem | Key traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Michael Peterson** (protagonist) | Faces the dilemma | Quiet, **conscientious**, strong technical skills, exceeds expectations — but **blunt, tactless**, avoids politics |
| **William Peak** | **Direct** — the meeting | New engineering/manufacturing manager; performance-driven; **dislikes non-technical / people issues** |
| **John MacTier** | **Direct** — the rumour | Past supervisor; 2002 conflict; gave a poor rating after a disagreement (**misuse of power**) |
| **Erica Meldon** | **Indirect** — if it escalates | Senior, profit-driven, also dislikes non-technical issues |

### The organisation's changing culture (why "Place" matters)

| Era / Leader | Leadership style | Effect on employees |
|---|---|---|
| **Jim Allen** (founder, 1982) | **Performance + People** — hands-on, "big family," camaraderie | Loyal, motivated, **minimal turnover** |
| **Aaron Rouse** (restructuring, ~2 yrs) | Wishy-washy / laissez-faire | Demotivated; sales fell to **20% staff cut** |
| **Erica Meldon** (2008, hired outside) | **Performance-oriented**, profit-driven, cost-cutter | Less concerned how staff feel |
| **William Peak** (2008, hired outside) | **Performance-oriented**, strong technical | Avoids non-technical/people matters |

**The chronology.** Founded 1982 → Peterson joins 1988 → 2002 conflict with MacTier → 2005 succession planning and restructuring begin → 2008 Meldon and Peak hired from outside to restore profits → the case unfolds, with an **economic downturn** making it risky to lose a job.

**Why the 2002 wound still hurts.** Peterson once voiced an opinion **contrary to MacTier** — logically and unemotionally — in a public meeting. MacTier said nothing but handed him a **poor performance rating** (a textbook **misuse of power**). From that one incident, Peterson built a **perception** that MacTier is a weak manager under whom his growth would stall.

### The decision: Peterson's SWOT

| | |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | Conscientious; strong technical expertise; consistently exceeded expectations; logical |
| **Weaknesses** | Blunt, direct communication; lacks tact and people skills |
| **Opportunities** | If the rumour is false, he works under Peak — shared traits could make a strong fit |
| **Threats** | A new boss may read him as a complainer; being forced under MacTier again; downsizing + downturn make job loss costly |

### The two real options

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Say nothing** (wait; let Peak reveal the structure) | No risk of offending a new, performance-driven boss on a people issue in a first meeting; safe during downsizing; fine if the rumour is false | If the rumour is true he's stuck under MacTier → demotivation, weak reviews, stalled growth |
| **Voice concerns** (thoughtfully) | He and Peak share a performance focus, so Peak may understand and help; the concern gets addressed | Peak dislikes non-technical issues; he may look like a complainer; it could strain ties or **escalate to Meldon**; job risk |

A third route — "**rephrase it tactfully**" — isn't realistic for Peterson, because **lacking tact is exactly his weakness**.

### The recommendation

If Peterson speaks, he must do it like a **prudent professional, not a blunt engineer**. A student framed the winning approach and the professor endorsed it:
- **Lead with shared ground** — both are technically strong and performance-driven.
- **Build trust first** — propose KPIs, show he's action-oriented and eager to deliver.
- **Only then, gently**, confirm the reporting line; never badmouth a previous boss in a first meeting.
- Simplest first step of all: **confirm whether the rumour is even true.**
- **Long-term fix:** work on **people skills** and mend the MacTier relationship — because the root problem is behavioural, not technical.

> **The professor:** "Is being open and direct always desirable in an organisation, especially with your supervisors? No. You need to be tactful."

:::note[Good to Know — Part B of the case]
What actually happened: Peterson **did** raise it with Peak; Peak (who dislikes non-technical issues) responded with an **official reprimand**; Peterson had to keep working with MacTier. Their relationship was **strained at first but eventually improved** — a reminder that outcomes are rarely as clean as either option predicts.
:::

### What the case teaches

- **Leadership styles shape outcomes.** Under Jim Allen (people + performance) staff were loyal and productive; under profit-only leaders, motivation sagged. The context matters: Meldon and Peak were **deliberately hired from outside** to cut costs, so a performance-only style was their mandate.
- **Perception is not reality.** Peterson's view of MacTier rests on one 2002 incident; six years on, MacTier may be a changed man. We have **no direct information** about him — so we must not over-assume (a point students pressed hard, and the professor held the line: *stick to the facts in the case*).
- **Cases carry uncertainty.** Here it's literally a **rumour**. You decide under incomplete information — explore alternatives, but justify every one with **case facts**.
- **The big lesson — the same equation again:** whether to voice depends on **Peterson's attributes** (blunt, tactless) × **Peak's attributes** (performance-driven, dislikes people issues) × **the organisation's attributes** (performance culture, downsizing, downturn). Behaviour = Person × Environment, playing out live.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The headline takeaway the professor repeated: **technical skills get you hired; people skills (and decision-making skills) keep you growing.** A conscientious star performer *still* suffered because of poor people skills. In most IGD cases the protagonist is technically brilliant but struggles for the **same** reason — so a strong answer always pairs the technical read with a **people-skills** recommendation.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Hired for hard skills, promoted for people skills"]
Peterson's whole problem in one line. Competence opens the door; tact and relationships decide how far you walk through it.
:::
