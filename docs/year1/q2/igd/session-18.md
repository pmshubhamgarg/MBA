# Session 18: The Trolley, the Fat Man, and Three Ways to Judge Right from Wrong

*The same person who happily diverts a runaway trolley to save five people refuses to push one man off a bridge to save the same five. Nothing in the maths changed — yet the answer flipped. Inside that contradiction live the three great tests of right and wrong: consequences, actions, and character.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** **Ethics vs Morality** (external rules vs your own principles); what an **ethical dilemma** is ("no good choices"); the **three ethical decision-making frameworks** — **Utilitarianism** (focus on consequences), **Deontology** (focus on the action/duty), **Virtue Ethics** (focus on the doer's character); the utilitarian line — *greatest good for the greatest number* — and its **blind spot** (individual rights and justice); the **Deontology vs Virtue Ethics** distinction (external duty vs internal character).

**Important (supporting):** The **trolley problem** (both versions) and the **doctor / transplant-surgeon** cases as the teaching vehicle; the **"killing vs letting die"** (act vs omission) insight; the end-term format revealed in class; the **four sub-dimensions of political skill** (exam-prep recall).

**Context (colour for answers):** euthanasia; cheating in exams; a lawyer defending a criminal; the romantic-partner twist; **The Dark Knight** — should Batman kill the Joker?
:::

## Setting the Stage: How Do We Decide What Is Right?

This is the **last teaching session** of IGD (Sessions 19 and 20 are reserved for group project presentations). The professor opens not with teams or leadership but with a question every manager eventually faces: when two choices are **both wrong**, how do you pick?

She begins by separating two words we throw around as if they mean the same thing.

> **The professor:** "Ethics refers to rules provided by an external source — a code of conduct for students, or for employees in a workplace. Morality refers to an individual's own principles regarding what is right or wrong. Morality is subjective, because what is right for you may be different from what is right for others."

So **ethics is external** (the rulebook handed to you) and **morality is internal** (your personal compass). The two often disagree.

- A rule says **students must not cheat** — but a student's own morality may say cheating is fine "sometimes, depending on the situation."
- A **lawyer defends a criminal** (professional duty) while personally believing the crime is wrong.
- **Euthanasia:** in most countries a doctor may **not** euthanise a patient even on request (medical ethics), yet the same doctor may **personally** believe a patient has "the right to die" (morality).

:::tip[Important — Ethics vs Morality]
**Ethics = the external rulebook. Morality = your own inner principles.** They frequently clash: your morals may be *at odds* with the ethics imposed on you. If a case shows someone torn between "the policy/code says X" and "but I personally believe Y," you are looking at an **ethics-vs-morality** conflict — name it directly.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "E is External, M is Me"]
**E**thics comes from an **E**xternal source. **M**orality comes from **M**e. Two different compasses — and they don't always point the same way.
:::

## Ethical Dilemmas: Choosing When There Are No Good Choices

> **The professor:** "Have you ever encountered a situation where you had to choose between two alternatives, and both the alternatives were wrong?"

A student described it perfectly — in daily life we often compare **which option is "less wrong"**, knowing both will cause harm. That, the professor says, is an **ethical dilemma**: a forced choice where every path is bad. To feel how our judgement actually works under pressure, she runs the class through a famous thought experiment.

---

## The Trolley Problem, Part 1: You're the Driver

You are driving a trolley car speeding down the track. Ahead, **five workers** are on the line. Your brakes fail. If you do nothing, the five die. But a **side track** has just **one worker** on it, and your steering still works. Turn the wheel and you kill one to save five.

**The class verdict:** the **majority would turn** the trolley. The reasoning was blunt and quick — "saving five lives is better than one life."

Then the professor twists the knife to test whether the principle is really a principle:

- What if the lone person on the side track is **your romantic partner**? (The room hesitates.)
- What if it's your partner **who cheated on you and just broke up with you**? ("Let the trolley follow the track.")

The twists show our "principle" is wobblier than we admit — but **most still back saving the five**. Hold that thought.

One student also raised the sharpest objection of the night: the lone worker is **on the correct, safe track** — *"why should he be penalised?"* Another added that **diverting means you personally decide a man's fate**, whereas doing nothing is "an automatic accident for which no one takes the blame." Bank those two objections; they become the whole point later.

---

## The Trolley Problem, Part 2: Now You Push the Fat Man

Same runaway trolley, same five workers about to die. But now **you are an onlooker on a bridge**, not the driver. Beside you stands a **large man** leaning over the railing. If you **push him onto the track**, his body stops the trolley: he dies, the five live.

Same maths — **one life to save five.** So, by your own earlier logic, you should push.

> **The professor:** "What happened to our principle that it is better to save five lives by sacrificing one? Earlier we were ready to sacrifice one life to save five. The same applies here."

**But almost nobody will push.** The students scramble for reasons: he's *innocent* and not even on the track; pushing him is a *deliberate* crime; the first case felt *accidental*, this one feels *intentional*.

One student crystallised it:

> *"Deliberately killing somebody is different from allowing somebody to die as collateral damage."*

That single sentence is the hinge of the whole session: **our focus silently shifted** — from the **outcome** (five saved) to the **act itself** (I used my own hands to kill an innocent person).

:::note[Good to Know — The Doctor Version]
The professor reruns the exact same flip with a medical case. **Version 1:** you're an ER doctor; five patients have moderate injuries, one is critical. Spend time on the one and the five die, or treat the five and the one dies. The class says **save the five**. **Version 2:** you're a transplant surgeon with five patients each needing a different organ, and a **healthy person** walks in for a check-up. Harvest his five organs and you save five patients. Now the class refuses — *"No!"* Identical arithmetic, opposite answer. Same lesson: when the act becomes **actively killing an innocent**, consequences stop driving us.
:::

---

## The Big Reveal: Three Ways to Make an Ethical Decision

The professor names what the class was doing all along. There are **three criteria** for making ethical decisions, depending on where you put your focus:

| Framework | Focus is on... | Core question | What it says about the "fat man" |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** | The **consequences / outcomes** | What produces the **greatest good for the greatest number**? | Push — five lives beat one |
| **Deontology** | The **action itself** (duty, rules) | Is the **act** right or wrong, whatever the result? | Don't push — killing an innocent is wrong |
| **Virtue Ethics** | The **moral character** of the doer | What **kind of person** do I become by doing this? | Don't push — a good person doesn't kill |

### 1. Utilitarianism — Judge by the Outcome

> **The professor:** "Utilitarianism suggests that the morally correct decision is the one that maximises well-being for the greatest number of people — the greatest good for the greatest number."

This drove **Version 1** of both cases ("save five"). But the professor flags its weakness clearly:

> **The professor:** "It does not consider individual differences — especially individual rights and justice."

That is exactly the student's earlier protest: the lone worker on the safe track had a **right** not to be sacrificed, and utilitarian maths simply overruns him.

### 2. Deontology — Judge by the Action

**Deontology** means **duty, obligation, or rule-ethics.** You do what is right because the **act itself** must be right, *irrespective of consequences.* Its commandments sound familiar: **do not kill, do not steal, do not cheat, do not lie.**

> **The professor:** "Telling the truth is considered morally right even if lying might lead to better consequences — because here your focus is on the action itself. You feel it is your duty to do what is right."

This is **Version 2** of both cases: pushing the man or harvesting the organs is *killing an innocent*, and killing is wrong — full stop.

### 3. Virtue Ethics — Judge by the Person

The third lens ignores both the outcome **and** the rule. It asks about the **character of the person doing the act.**

> **The professor:** "Instead of asking 'What is my duty?' (deontology) or 'What produces the best result?' (utilitarianism), virtue ethics asks: **what kind of person am I becoming through my action?** A virtuous person develops and practises good traits — honesty, courage, integrity, fairness, compassion."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The three ethical decision-making frameworks are the spine of this session:
- **Utilitarianism → focus on CONSEQUENCES** ("greatest good for the greatest number"; blind to individual rights/justice).
- **Deontology → focus on the ACTION** (duty and rules: do not kill/steal/cheat/lie — regardless of outcome).
- **Virtue Ethics → focus on the CHARACTER** of the doer (what kind of person am I becoming?).

Map any dilemma to all three. In the trolley/doctor cases, **Version 1 = utilitarian** (save five), **Version 2 = deontology + virtue** (don't kill an innocent). Being able to label a case on these three axes is a high-value exam move.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Maths, Rulebook, Mirror"]
**Utilitarianism = the Maths** (count the outcomes). **Deontology = the Rulebook** (follow the duty). **Virtue ethics = the Mirror** (what kind of person do I see?). Three questions for every hard choice: *What's the result? What's the rule? Who am I becoming?*
:::

## The Subtle One: Deontology vs Virtue Ethics

Students kept blurring these two — one even asked whether virtue ethics is just a **sub-set of deontology**. The professor said flatly: **no, they are different.** Both make you "do the right thing," but for **opposite reasons.**

> **The professor:** "In deontology you act because you are **obliged by rules** — there is an external pressure on you to follow duties and principles. In virtue ethics you do the right thing not because of external pressure, but because **it expresses who you are as a person** — your virtuous character."

So the **action can look identical**; the **moral driver** is different:

- **Deontology** = right act from **external** obligation (the rule pushes you).
- **Virtue ethics** = right act from **internal** character (you *are* that kind of person).

### The Dark Knight Test

The professor applies all three to one question — **should Batman kill the Joker?**

| Lens | Verdict on Batman killing the Joker |
|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** | **Endorses** it — taking one life (Joker) saves many future lives |
| **Deontology** | **Rejects** it — the act of killing is itself wrong |
| **Virtue Ethics** | **Rejects** it — *does Batman want to be the kind of person who kills his enemies?* |

> **The professor:** "There are some lines that good people never cross, and killing somebody falls on the wrong side of that line. Batman does not kill his enemies — not because of consequences, not because of the rule, but because of his **character**. That is virtue ethics."

:::note[Good to Know — Why the "Flip" Matters for Managers]
This session is secretly about **self-awareness under pressure** (the whole pre-midterm theme). Most of us *think* we are consistent utilitarians — until we are asked to do the killing with our own hands. Real ethical leadership means noticing **which lens you are actually using**, and whether the outcome is quietly letting you trample someone's rights.
:::

## Exam Watch — The IGD End-Term (shared in this class)

Because this is the final teaching session, the professor walks through the **end-term paper** itself. Treat this as revision gold:

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Weightage** | 20% of the course |
| **Format** | Closed book, **2 hours**, likely pen-and-paper |
| **Structure** | **4 questions** — 2 from pre-midterm sessions, 2 from post-midterm sessions |
| **Question type** | **Short case scenarios** to be analysed using the relevant IGD concept |
| **Space** | About **two pages per answer** (half an hour each) |
| **Possible mix** | Some MCQs / one-word answers plus **two of three** case studies |

The sample question she shared asked students to **evaluate a protagonist's political skill** across its four sub-dimensions. Her coaching line is the real tip:

> **The professor:** "Everything is in the question itself. If you know what social astuteness or apparent sincerity means, you just identify the instances from the case and write your answer. If you don't know the concept, you can't answer."

So the four sub-dimensions of **political skill** are worth re-memorising cold:

| Sub-dimension | In one line |
|---|---|
| **Social astuteness** | Reading people and situations accurately |
| **Interpersonal influence** | Adapting your style to persuade effectively |
| **Networking ability** | Building and leveraging a web of connections |
| **Apparent sincerity** | Coming across as genuine, authentic, trustworthy (you "walk the talk") |

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Know the term, mine the case"]
The IGD end-term rewards **concept recall, not essay-writing.** Learn each key term in one crisp line, then hunt the case for a matching instance. "Read the room," as the professor noted during a presentation, *is literally* **social astuteness.**
:::

## The Recipe Chain (Where Session 18 Sits)

**Session 18 is the course's last concept class — and it circles back to where IGD began: knowing yourself.**

> Earlier sessions → **Self → Others → Organization**: personality, biases, motivation, emotions, teams, conflict, power, politics and leadership.
> **Session 18 → How do you decide what is *right*? Ethics vs morality, and the three lenses — Utilitarianism (outcome), Deontology (action), Virtue ethics (character).**
> Sessions 19–20 → **Group project presentations** (no new theory) — plus the closed-book end-term that rewards clean concept recall applied to short cases.

IGD opened by asking *why two people behave differently in the same situation.* It closes by asking the harder version: *when the situation is a genuine ethical dilemma, which version of yourself shows up — the one who counts the outcomes, the one who obeys the rule, or the one who refuses to become a certain kind of person?* That self-awareness is the whole course in one question.
