# Session 12: Everyone's Competent — So Why Does the Team Still Fail?

*A team of brilliant, competitive individuals can still lose — sometimes even to its own members. Session 12 says competence is only the first of four C's, and that trust, not talent, sits at the dead centre of every team that actually works.*

---

## Setting the Stage: Two Classes on What Makes a Team Work

This is the **first of two sessions on team dynamics**. Today is mostly the map: what a team is, the different types of teams, and the real reasons teams fail. The professor keeps it conceptual and then runs a live in-class exercise; the psychometric tests and deep dives come next class.

She warms up with the basics. **A team is a group of people working towards a common goal.** Types include **functional, cross-functional, self-managed, virtual** (like this Zoom cohort) and **project teams**. Then she drops the question the whole evening circles around:

> **The professor:** "Why do teams comprising competitive individuals often fail or struggle to succeed? Everyone is competent — yet the team still can't give its 100%. What would be the reasons?"

---

## The Opening Puzzle: Competent, Competitive — and Still Sinking

The class fires back a long list: different opinions, poor communication, lack of collaboration, misalignment with the common goal, poor planning, unclear roles, intra-team competition, **lack of trust**, office politics, lack of motivation, poor leadership, and "the wrong mix of people, personalities and skills." The professor nudges them to tie it to the **P-factors** from the earlier sessions — different personalities, motivations and emotional states.

Then she sharpens it into four "would this team still hit its goals?" scenarios:

1. **Everyone is competent but they can't work together** (compatibility missing). → points to **Compatibility**.
2. **Roles are misaligned with people's actual capabilities.** → points to **Capabilities**.
3. **Members don't trust each other**, so they don't feel connected or committed. → points to **Cohesion**.
4. **Why do people chase personal goals over collective goals?** → again, **absence of trust**.

The through-line is one blunt correction to how most of us think about hiring stars:

> **The professor:** "Competence, no doubt, is essential to make a team functional — but competence alone cannot make your team functional. Teams need compatibility."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Competence is necessary but not sufficient.** A team of individually brilliant people can still fail. If a case shows talented members who don't deliver as a unit, don't blame skill — look for the missing C's: **Capabilities alignment, Compatibility, or Cohesion**. The exam-ready line is *"competence alone cannot make a team functional."*
:::

## The Spine of the Session: The Four C's of an Effective Team

Everything today hangs on one framework. As a manager or team lead, you build an effective team by aligning **four C's**:

| C | What it means | The framework behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Members have the skills to do the work | (assumed — the baseline) |
| **Capabilities** | Roles match people; the team balances **thinkers, doers, supporters** | **Belbin Team Roles (BTR)** |
| **Compatibility** | Competent people are comfortable working **together** | **FIRO-B** |
| **Cohesion** | Members **trust** each other, feel connected and committed | **Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions** |

When the four C's line up, the professor's phrase is that teams *"don't just function — they flourish."*

:::tip[Memory Hook — "The 4 C's: Competent, Capable, Compatible, Cohesive"]
Four words, in order of build: hire for **Competence**, assign for **Capabilities**, bond for **Compatibility**, glue with **Cohesion**. Miss any one and the competent team still underperforms.
:::

To unpack the last three C's, she introduces **three frameworks of team dynamics** — one per C.

---

## C2 — Capabilities: A Team Needs Thinkers, Doers and Supporters (Belbin)

**Concept.** Capability is not "more skill" — it is **role balance**. From **Meredith Belbin's Team Role Inventory (BTR, 1981)**, the idea is that a good team mixes people who play different natural roles:

| Role | What they bring | One line |
|---|---|---|
| **Thinkers** | Generate ideas and options | The brains |
| **Doers** | Drive action and deliver results | The engine |
| **Supporters** | Build harmony, trust and collaboration | The glue |

**Why it matters.** A team of five "thinkers" argues forever and ships nothing; five "doers" sprint in five directions. You need all three present.

> **The professor:** "A good team needs a balance of thinkers, doers and supporters. When all three functions are present, the team gains both brains and bandwidth."

Next class, students take the **Belbin inventory** to discover whether they naturally act as a thinker, a doer or a supporter in a team.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Brains and Bandwidth"]
**Thinkers = brains, Doers = bandwidth, Supporters = the glue that holds both.** A team missing any one of the three is lopsided — lots of ideas with no delivery, or lots of effort with no direction.
:::

---

## C3 — Compatibility: Matching Interpersonal Needs (FIRO-B)

**Concept.** Compatibility is explained through **FIRO-B — Fundamental Interpersonal Relations Orientation (Behaviour)**, from **William Schutz**. The core claim: when we work in teams, our behaviour is shaped by **three interpersonal needs**, and compatibility depends on how well these are met.

| Need | What we want in a team |
|---|---|
| **Inclusion** | To belong and to participate |
| **Control** | To have influence, authority and responsibility |
| **Affection** | To be trusted, respected and supported |

**The clever part — expressed vs wanted.** Each need runs on two dimensions: how much you **express** it, and how much you **want** it from others. Take control: one person scores high on **expressed control** (wants to steer the team); another scores high on **wanted control** (prefers to be guided). Put those two together and they *fit*.

> **The professor:** "Expressed means how much control I express; wanted means how much control I want from others. When one person wants to influence and the other wants to be influenced, that alignment promotes compatibility."

**Why it matters.** This is the **CP effect — Compatibility drives Productivity.** Two equally competent teams can diverge sharply in output purely because one has its interpersonal needs met and the other doesn't.

:::note[Good to Know — Compatibility Is Complementarity, Not Similarity]
A student asked whether compatibility just means focusing on similarities and ignoring differences. The professor said no: it is about matching **complementary needs**. One person wanting to lead and another happy to be led is a *difference* that creates a perfect fit — not a similarity.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "I-C-A: Include me, let me steer, value me"]
The three FIRO needs are **Inclusion, Control, Affection.** Meet all three — "I belong, I have a say, I'm respected" — and competent people become compatible teammates.
:::

## C4 — Cohesion: The Five Dysfunctions of a Team (Lencioni)

**Concept.** Cohesion is **trust-based commitment and connection**, explained through **Patrick Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions of a Team (FDT, 2002)**. Lencioni's model is a pyramid, and each layer topples the next:

| # | Dysfunction | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Absence of trust** | You hide mistakes and won't ask for help (no **vulnerability-based trust**) |
| 2 | **Fear of conflict** | No healthy debate; an **artificial harmony** settles in |
| 3 | **Lack of commitment** | Without open dialogue you lack clarity, so you don't buy in |
| 4 | **Avoidance of accountability** | You don't own your work or hold teammates to theirs |
| 5 | **Inattention to results** | Collective goals get dropped for personal goals |

The chain is the point: no trust → no healthy conflict → no clarity → no commitment → no accountability → personal goals beat team goals.

> **The professor:** "A team fails not because of a lack of talent, but because of a breakdown in trust, commitment and accountability. Everything starts with trust — trust is the centre of everything when you work in a team."

Note the twist on **conflict**: it is not the villain. **Productive conflict** — exchanging ideas to improve the quality of work — is healthy and needed. **Dysfunctional conflict** — clashes of ego and personality that damage the work — is what you fight. Low trust kills the *productive* kind and invites the *destructive* kind.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Lencioni's chain, in order: Absence of Trust → Fear of Conflict → Lack of Commitment → Avoidance of Accountability → Inattention to Results.** Trust is vulnerability-based (showing weakness, asking for help). Fix the base and the whole pyramid stands — the team gains *"both trust and traction."* Being able to walk this chain top to bottom is a high-value answer.
:::

:::tip[Important — The Model Isn't the Only Cause]
The professor was honest that FDT explains *one* path to low commitment. In the real world, commitment can also collapse from **lack of motivation, bad conflict, ego clashes, power politics or poor leadership.** In a case, name the dysfunction chain first, then add these as alternative drivers for full marks.
:::

## Team Synergy and the Desert Survival Exercise

**Concept.** Why bother with teams at all? **Synergy** — collective output beating individual output.

> **The professor:** "Teams are not just about bringing different people together. Teams are about leveraging their differences to create synergy, so that collective intelligence beats individual intelligence."

To test whether the cohort's teams actually *had* synergy, the professor ran the **Desert Survival** exercise — a piece of experiential learning. The setup: you are stranded in a desert with a **survival kit of 15 items**, and you must rank them by importance for survival.

**How it works:**

1. **Rank the 15 items alone** (individual step).
2. **Rank them again as a team** (group step).
3. Reveal the **expert ranking**.
4. **Individual error** = absolute difference between your ranking and the expert's.
5. **Team error** = absolute difference between the team's ranking and the expert's.

From those scores the team computes four readings:

| Metric | What it compares | Positive means |
|---|---|---|
| **Team functioning** | Team vs its **weakest** member | Team beat its worst performer |
| **Team effectiveness** | Team vs the **average** member | Team beat the typical member |
| **Team synergy** | Team vs its **best** member | True synergy — team beat even its star |
| **Overall performance** | The combined picture | The team added value |

**The humbling result.** For the team the professor reviewed, the numbers came out **negative** — the team's error was *larger* than its members' errors. In plain terms: **people did better alone than together.** The team had **negative synergy**; it pulled down both its best and its worst performer. Her candid explanation: the activity normally needs 2–3 hours but was squeezed into about 30 minutes, and a **virtual team** can't discuss as richly as a room can.

The debrief surfaced two classic team traps:

- **Groupthink.** One student genuinely believed the **mirror** was the most important item but gave it up under group pressure to keep harmony — exactly how conformity produces faulty decisions.
- **Biases.** **Anchoring bias** — the first person's suggestion over-shaped the final ranking. **Availability bias** — groups over-valued **water** (we drink it daily) and under-valued the **mirror**, never having used one for signalling. Experts actually rank the mirror highest, precisely because it sends rescue signals.

:::tip[Important — What Actually Lifts Team Decisions]
The exercise's real lesson: **teams that listen, explain their reasoning and clarify their assumptions make better decisions.** Add constructive conflict management — surfacing disagreement and resolving it through open dialogue — and synergy turns positive. Silence and anchoring turn it negative.
:::

> **The professor:** "It's not about deserts. It represents a mirror of organizational life — limited resources, uncertainty, pressure, conflicting opinions, and still you have to collaborate to achieve your goals."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "The desert is the office"]
Swap "survival kit" for "quarterly target" and the exercise is just a normal work week: scarce resources, uncertainty, pressure, clashing opinions — and the job is still to **decide together** and beat what any one of you could do alone.
:::
