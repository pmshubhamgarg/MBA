# Session 7: The Villain in the Mirror — Your Dark Triad, and Why Charisma Isn't Leadership

*Self-perception begins with knowing the self you'd never put on your résumé. Session 7 finishes the personality unit with its shadow side — the Dark Triad — then asks the sharper question: why do manipulators so often rise, yet so rarely last?*

---

## Quick Recap — The Personality Toolkit So Far

The professor opened by stitching this class to the two personality sessions before it. Her logic for the whole unit: **self-perception is shaped by your P factors** — your personality. So before we study *perception*, we finish *personality*.

What you already carry into the room:

- **MBTI** — four pairs of psychological preferences, giving a four-letter code; these are **universal** traits (found across ages and cultures).
- **Big Five / OCEAN / Five-Factor Model** — five broad traits, each with subtraits; everyone has all five **in varying degree**.

She tested recall with five one-liners — can you name the trait?

| "If I say…" | …the trait is |
|---|---|
| "I'm full of ideas" | **O**penness |
| "I'm someone you can rely on" | **C**onscientiousness |
| "I'm the life of the party, very sociable" | **E**xtraversion |
| "You can trust me, I have empathy" | **A**greeableness |
| "I'm always calm and confident" | (low) **N**euroticism — emotional stability |

> **The professor:** "There's nothing good or bad about your personality. OCEAN — that's who you are."

That line matters, because the next topic deliberately breaks the rule: the Dark Triad traits **are** labelled negative.

---

## The Dark Triad — The Personality You Don't Put on LinkedIn

Today's new material is the **three "dark" personality traits**: **Machiavellianism, Narcissism, Psychopathy.** They are called "dark" because they are **malevolent, socially undesirable** traits — very different from the morally-neutral MBTI/Big Five.

Two facts the professor repeated until they stuck:

1. The three are **distinct yet related** — different traits, but with **overlapping subtraits**.
2. **We all carry traces** of them, in varying degree. Most people **don't score high** — but nobody is at zero. Example: you can be genuinely kind and empathetic (high agreeableness) and *still* occasionally manipulate someone for an advantage. That occasional manipulation is a **trace of Machiavellianism.**

A quick history, because origins make great one-mark answers:

- **Machiavellianism** — from Italian diplomat **Niccolò Machiavelli**, book **The Prince (1532)**; philosophy: *the end justifies the means.*
- **Narcissism** — rooted in **Greek mythology** (Narcissus, in love with his own reflection).
- **Psychopathy** — roots in **clinical psychology**, tied to antisocial behaviour.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Mac · Narc · Psycho"]
Three shadows of the self: **Mac** (manipulates you), **Narc** (worships himself), **Psycho** (harms you without remorse). Everyone has a trace; few score high.
:::

### Why the test came *before* the theory

The professor made the class take a **dark-triad self-assessment first**, then taught the concepts. That order is itself exam-worthy, because it defeats two self-report problems:

- **Social desirability bias** — we rate ourselves *more* positive and *less* negative than we are.
- **Priming effect** — once you know what a test is measuring, that knowledge quietly reshapes your answers.

The "healthy" band she gave: a score **under 25** (lower is better — these are negative traits). When a few students reported 60–90%, her verdict was blunt: that is not possible for genuine traces — **something went wrong in how the questions were read and answered.**

---

## Machiavellianism ("Mac") — "The End Justifies the Means"

**Concept:** the calculated manipulator. High scorers lie, cheat, flatter (ingratiation), deceive and exploit — all **selfish and calculated** — because for them the only test of right and wrong is the **outcome.**

- They are **ambitious and dominant**, driven by power, status, control and success.
- They **lack morality** — no fixed right or wrong, so ethics don't bind them.
- The hallmark: they **lack empathy but can fake sympathy.** (Sympathy = *understanding* another's emotion; empathy = *understanding and feeling* it. Mac types read your emotion well enough to work you, without actually feeling it.)
- The twist — their **positive** side: charisma, strong self-confidence, excellent communication, courage, patience, strategic thinking. That cocktail is why **Mac people often emerge as business leaders.**

**Class examples:** **Shakuni Mama** (Mahabharata) — the textbook Mac for his manipulative, deceptive calculation; **Bhallaladeva** (Bahubali). The room debated others, and the professor corrected sharply: **Ravana and Duryodhan are narcissism, not Mac**, and **Krishna is not Mac at all.**

> **The professor:** "Their mantra is simple — the end justifies the means. Do whatever it takes."

---

## Narcissism — "I, Me and Myself"

**Concept:** an **excessive sense of self-importance.** Narcissists crave attention, recognition and dominance because they believe they are **special and superior** — all vanity, grandiosity and **entitlement**, built on an **inflated self-image.**

- They are **aversive to criticism and feedback** — you cannot correct them.
- They **lack empathy**, but (like Mac types) come wrapped in charisma: competent, confident, courageous, **visionary big thinkers.**

**Class examples:** celebrities as a category; **Michael Jackson**; **Donald Trump**; **Ravana** and **Duryodhan**; **Mogambo**; and the sitcom poster child **Sheldon Cooper** (*The Big Bang Theory*) — "I did a bad thing. Does it affect me? No? Then suffer in silence."

**The important sub-category — the humble (productive) narcissist.** Researchers added "humble" for people who think highly of themselves **yet know their weaknesses and admire others' strengths.** The professor's examples: **Elon Musk** and **Steve Jobs.**

> **Elon Musk (quoted in class):** "I'm a narcissist, but at least I'm a useful one."

---

## Psychopathy — Charming on Top, Dangerous Underneath

**Concept:** **impulsive** people who **lack remorse and guilt** and can harm others for the **thrill** of it. They too are charismatic, confident and well-spoken — which is exactly why victims fall into their trap. The professor split this into three terms you must keep separate:

| Type | Where it comes from | Behaviour | Will they kill? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Psychopath** | Born with it — "something in the brain" | Charming cover, dangerous core | **Yes** — serial killers |
| **Corporate psychopath** | Same wiring, inside organisations | Manipulative, cruel, impulsive, no remorse | **No** — non-violent, but will harm you |
| **Sociopath** | Made by **childhood trauma** | Struggles to bond; feels *some* guilt after harming | **Maybe** |

**Class examples:** **Ted Bundy** — the notorious serial killer so charismatic that people fell for him; **Hitler**; and the signature **corporate psychopath, Elizabeth Holmes** — once hailed as the next Steve Jobs, now in prison.

The one-line separator a student earned (using *Dexter*): **psychopath vs sociopath is about guilt** — the sociopath feels a little, the psychopath feels none.

---

## How the Three Fit Together

They overlap but are not identical. What they **share**: a lack of empathy and remorse, plus the charisma that lets them lead. What **differs**: Mac types and corporate psychopaths **manipulate and deceive**; narcissists run on **inflated ego.** And the relationship the professor drilled hardest:

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**All psychopaths are narcissistic and Machiavellian — but not every narcissist or Machiavellian is a psychopath.** Picture it as nesting: psychopathy sits inside the overlap of the other two. In a case, name the trait by its hallmark — *manipulation* (Mac), *self-importance* (narcissism), *remorseless harm* (psychopathy) — and reserve "psychopath" for someone who shows all three.
:::

| Trait | Core idea | Hallmark | Tell / mantra | Class tags |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Psychopathy** | Impulsive harm, no remorse | Lacks empathy **and** remorse | Thrill in harming | Ted Bundy; Elizabeth Holmes |

## Emergence vs Effectiveness — Why the Charismatic Villain Rises, Then Falls

If dark-trait people are so toxic, why do they keep reaching the top? Because leadership has **two separate questions**, and they confuse them.

- **Emergence** — do you *rise* into the leader's chair? Charisma, confidence and boldness win this.
- **Effectiveness** — do you *perform and last* once there? Trust, ethics and results win this.

Dark-trait leaders are brilliant at the first and poor at the second. The case the professor kept returning to: **Elizabeth Holmes** emerged as "the next Steve Jobs," dazzled everyone — and then collapsed into a prison sentence.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Emergence does not guarantee effectiveness.** People high on the Dark Triad tend to **emerge** as leaders but fail to become **effective** ones. If a question asks why a charismatic high-flyer imploded, this is your framework.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "They Emerge, then Submerge"]
Charisma gets them the title; the missing ethics and empathy sink them later. Emergence ≠ effectiveness.
:::

When a student (half-joking) asked how to *raise* their dark scores for a "second career," the professor's answer doubled as the cure:

> **The professor:** "Why would you want to increase your dark traits? If you want to reduce them — follow ethics. Don't run after success, fame and money."

## Can You Trust a Self-Score? Validating What You "Know" About Yourself

Because these are self-assessments, accuracy depends on your attentiveness and how well you understood each item. The professor laid out how personality is **properly** measured:

1. **Self-assessment** — honest responses first, *before* the concept is revealed (to dodge priming).
2. **360-degree feedback** — ask people who know you well; cross-check your self-view against theirs.
3. **Projective tests** — the deeper instruments psychiatrists use.

:::tip[Important]
A suspiciously **high** self-score on a negative trait usually signals a **measurement problem**, not a villain — misread items, priming, or social-desirability distortion. That is why one test is never enough: triangulate self-report with 360-degree feedback before believing a number.
:::

## Four Workplace Traits That Aren't Universal

Beyond MBTI, Big Five and the Dark Triad sit four traits that are **not universal** but show up constantly at work. You don't need to memorise every detail — just recognise them.

| Trait | The question it answers | A high scorer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Core self-evaluation (CSE)** | "Do I see myself positively?" | Feels valuable, handles hard tasks, stays calm under stress |
| **Locus of control** | "Who controls the outcomes in my life?" | *Internal* = "my hard work"; *External* = "luck, fate, others, divine help" |
| **Self-monitoring** | "Do I adapt my behaviour to the room?" | Reads people, manages impressions, networks well |
| **Proactive personality** | "Do I spot and act on opportunities?" | Initiates early, finishes on time, doesn't wait to be told |

The professor's wry real-world note: the **self-monitor** is often the colleague who "doesn't do much work but gets good ratings" — a master of networking and impression management. (Note: *locus of control* is a personality trait about cause-and-effect over your life; the student's question about "locus of identity" is a different idea she flagged to check.)

## When the Wiring Misfires — Personality Disorders

A **personality disorder** is your **default way of relating to the outside world** when that default is harmful. The ones named in class:

- **Antisocial** — insensitive to others, prone to deviant behaviour.
- **Borderline (BPD)** — extreme mood swings, distorted self-image, chronic emptiness, risk of self-harm.
- **Schizotypal** — thought disorder, constant suspicion of others' intentions.
- **Obsessive-compulsive (OCD)** — repeated acts or thoughts (e.g., washing hands every few minutes; fanatical cleanliness).
- **Avoidant** — strong aversion to social contact.

(Keep **bipolar** separate — that's a *mood* disorder, not a personality disorder.)

:::note[Good to Know]
**Nature or nurture?** Both — a complex interplay. The professor's rough estimate: **roughly 60–70% genetic**, with the rest triggered by environment, or by a gene that was recessive in your parents turning dominant in you (so a child can have OCD when the parents don't). And they are **manageable/curable with proper medication** — she pointed to Yo Yo Honey Singh's publicly discussed recovery from borderline personality disorder as an example.
:::

## Person vs Situation — Behaviour = P × E

Human behaviour is a **complex interplay of your P factor (personality) and E (environment).** The classic puzzle: two people in the **same** environment — one thrives, one fails — because of the difference in **P.** But sometimes the situation does the steering:

| Theory | One-line claim | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| **Situational strength** | A **strong** situation overrides personality | Traffic signals / strict offices — rules, not traits, drive behaviour |
| **Trait activation** | A situation can **activate or suppress** a trait | *12th Fail* — someone low on conscientiousness grows high in the right environment |

The uplifting read: put a person in the right, strong environment and you can **condition** better behaviour out of them over time. The professor's own tip — understand your partner's, child's or boss's traits and you can **manage** (not manipulate) the relationship better.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Strong room beats strong you"]
In a **strong situation**, the room writes the script (situational strength). Stay long enough and the room can even **rewrite your traits** (trait activation).
:::

## The Case Bridge — Jensen Shoes & Jane Kravitz

A student group then presented the first of two **companion cases**: *Jensen Shoes — Jane Kravitz's story* (session 8 covers Lyndon Brooks's side). Companion cases = the **same workplace events seen from two sides** (supervisor and subordinate), to expose how each person's perceptions were **full of biases** and **changed after working together.**

The group analysed Jane through four **core self-evaluation** traits — **self-esteem, locus of control, introversion–extroversion, dependability** (the first two = how we see *ourselves*; the last two = how we *interact with others*). The professor's own additions were the exam gold — she named the **biases** hiding in Jane's story:

- Jane **disregarded Chuck's warning** about Lyndon and backed her own coaching ability → **overconfidence bias.**
- Jane pre-judged Lyndon as a "charming man who, given the right chance, could be a star" → **halo effect.**
- Working with him, those distorted perceptions **shifted** — proof that perceptions are built on bias, not fact.

> **The professor (to the group):** "Good presentation — but you missed the most important points: perceptions and biases."

## Perceptions Are Reality — The Pivot to Session 8

The class closed on the sentence that bridges personality into the next unit:

> **The professor:** "Our perceptions are our reality — but reality is different from perceptions."

Why the gap? Because perceptions are **stuffed with biases** — the mind's **shortcuts** (the brain can't store everything, so it guesses, and the guesses skew decisions).

- You **cannot control** your biases — but with **awareness you can manage** them.
- This is why firms run **diversity-and-inclusion trainings**: not to erase bias, but to make people aware enough to manage it.

That is Session 8's whole job: the catalogue of biases, applied back onto Jane and Lyndon.
