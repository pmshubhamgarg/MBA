# The Kabir Story (Sessions 1–20)

*One continuous story to lock the whole course into memory. Follow Kabir — a newly promoted Indian manager — as each class quietly solves a problem sitting on his own desk, turning confusion into clarity, one P-factor at a time.*

---

Kabir was thirty-four, and for eight years he had been the best individual contributor on his floor at a mid-sized firm in Pune. He hit every target, knew the product cold, and assumed that was the whole job. So when the promotion came — **team lead, nine people, one shared number** — he took it as a reward.

It felt more like a trapdoor. His stars bickered. A quiet high-performer stopped trying. A peer went around him to his own client. His ratings sparked sulks. Nothing in his spreadsheets explained any of it, and his own boss, Mrs. Iyer, asked him a question that would follow him for twenty weeks:

> "You can clearly manage the work, Kabir. But can you manage the *people*?"

He didn't have the answer. That evening he logged into his first **Individual and Group Dynamics** class and discovered the honest reply almost always starts the same way: **people are not interchangeable — so read the person first.**

## Chapter 1: The promotion that changed the job (Session 1)

Kabir arrived expecting tips and templates. Instead the professor opened with a question that landed on his exact problem: *why do two people, same desk, same boss, same target, end up so differently — one thriving, one checking out?*

His confusion was personal. *I did the job brilliantly alone. Why is leading nine versions of it so much harder?* The professor's answer reframed everything: because **no two people are the same**, and that uniqueness comes from your **P-factors** — personality, values, biases, motivation, emotions. Manage everyone the same way and you are managing a fiction. The course, she said, is a three-step zoom-out — **Self → Others → Organization** — and it starts with him.

She dropped two ideas he wrote down twice. **Money is the biggest motivator, but money alone never moves anyone for long** — people also want meaning, autonomy, recognition, fairness. And **roughly 20% of your success is IQ; the rest is EQ**, the one intelligence you can actually train. His technical brilliance had gotten him the promotion. It would not, by itself, carry him through it.

> The confusion: *I was the best at the work, so I'll be the best at leading it.* The clarity: managing people is the work of *understanding* them — and that is a different, learnable skill.

## Chapter 2: Same desk, two different people (Session 2)

Two of Kabir's analysts sat under the identical setup — same autonomy, same brief. One flourished and shipped improvements; the other drifted and padded his breaks. Kabir had been blaming the second man's attitude. The second class handed him a cleaner lens.

**Behaviour is a function of the Person and the Environment** — Kurt Lewin's equation, **B = f(P, E)**. Hold the environment constant and people still diverge, because the **P factor** differs: his flourishing analyst was high on **conscientiousness** and seized the freedom; the other, low on it, slid toward time theft. Same empowering boss, opposite behaviour.

Then the professor taught the course's first case — **Michael Peterson**, a conscientious, technically excellent engineer who still landed in trouble because he was blunt and tactless. Kabir felt the jolt of recognition: *that's me in meetings.* The lesson stuck like a slogan: **technical skill gets you hired; people skills decide how far you grow.** Nature had dealt Peterson a strong hand; he kept misplaying the people cards.

> The confusion: *one of them is just lazy.* The clarity: same environment, different person — read the P factor before you judge the behaviour.

## Chapter 3: The ninety percent he couldn't see (Session 3)

Kabir's quietest star, Meera, had gone flat — still polite, still on time, but doing the bare minimum. Nothing on the surface explained it. The third class told him why: he was only ever seeing the surface.

**People are icebergs.** About **10% is visible behaviour**; the other **90%** — VABs, attitudes, motivation, emotions — sits below the waterline. Meera's silence was the tip of something he couldn't see. The professor gave him a map for it: **EVLN**, the four things everyone does when fed up — **Exit, Voice, Loyalty, Neglect**, laid on two axes (active/passive, constructive/destructive). Most people *start* with **Voice**; ignore them and they slide to **Loyalty**, then **Neglect**, then **Exit**.

Kabir realised Meera had **voiced** a concern months ago, been brushed off, and quietly moved to **Neglect**. He also learned why some people never budge: a **core VAB** is set in concrete, a **peripheral** one is putty. If resistance sits on a core value, you change the team; if it's peripheral, you can influence it. Meera's disengagement was peripheral and reachable — if he acted.

> The confusion: *she seems fine, so she is fine.* The clarity: behaviour is the tip; a loyal performer quietly neglecting her work is signalling dissatisfaction you must investigate.

## Chapter 4: The call that went over his head (Session 4)

Then a peer from the creative side phoned Kabir's client directly, bypassing him, and the client called Kabir to complain. His first instinct was to march over and confront the man. The fourth class — a case almost eerily like his week — stopped him.

In **Amelia Rogers at Tassani Communications**, a creative director phones the client over the account director's head. The trap Kabir recognised: the peer was on the **same hierarchy**, so Rogers had **no formal authority** to confront him — and neither did Kabir. The professor's answer was **influence without authority**: use **consultation** (bring him into decisions, stop micromanaging) and **inspirational appeal** (shared goals). The plan ran **colleague first, client next, boss only if it repeats** — curious and non-accusatory, never a complaint.

And again the diagnosis was the familiar disease: Rogers was technically superb and great with clients, but couldn't read a peer's need for autonomy. Kabir saw his own reflex — control, then confront — and chose persuasion instead.

> The confusion: *he broke the rule, so I'll put him in his place.* The clarity: with no authority over a peer, persuasion replaces command — influence beats confrontation.

## Chapter 5: Four letters and five traits (Sessions 5–6)

Halfway in, the professor turned the lens on Kabir himself. He took the **MBTI** and got a four-letter code — one of **16 types** built from four pairs (**E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P**). Then the richer mirror, the **Big Five / OCEAN**: five traits, each scored high, moderate or low, with one **defining trait** that dominates.

His confusion was the one a classmate voiced aloud: *if I can't change my personality, why bother knowing it?* The professor's layered answer reorganised his thinking. First, **self-awareness** — know your strengths, your blind spots, *why* you clash with certain people. Second, **manage others** — you can't lead a team whose traits you can't read. Third, **you can't rewrite personality, but you can place around it and nudge it slowly** (the **maturity principle**: we drift more conscientious and stable with age).

He also learned to distrust his own test scores. **Social desirability, overconfidence, availability and priming** quietly inflate self-ratings; the fix is **360-degree feedback**, because *leadership is about how others see you, not how you see yourself.* And the organizationally ideal profile was worth a slogan: **high on Conscientiousness, low on Neuroticism, moderate on the rest.**

> The confusion: *knowing my type is pointless if it's fixed.* The clarity: you don't rewrite traits — you read them, place people around them, and shrink your blind spots.

## Chapter 6: The charming operator (Session 7)

There was a colleague everyone found magnetic — confident, persuasive, always rising — yet projects he touched left quiet wreckage. Kabir couldn't square the charm with the damage. The seventh class named it.

The **Dark Triad**: **Machiavellianism** ("the end justifies the means"), **Narcissism** ("I, me, myself"), and **Psychopathy** (remorseless harm). Everyone carries a trace; few score high. The professor's nesting rule stuck: **all psychopaths are Machiavellian and narcissistic, but not the reverse.** And the key that explained the charming operator: **emergence is not effectiveness.** Dark-trait people *rise* on charisma and boldness, then *fail* on the trust and ethics that make leadership last — Elizabeth Holmes being the textbook flame-out.

Kabir stopped mistaking confidence for competence. The question wasn't *does this person dazzle?* but *do they deliver, and can they be trusted?*

> The confusion: *he's so impressive — why do things quietly break around him?* The clarity: charisma wins the *emergence*; ethics and empathy win the *effectiveness* — and they are not the same thing.

## Chapter 7: The appraisal mirror (Session 8)

Appraisal season arrived, and Kabir sat down to rate his nine. The eighth class made him put the pen down first, because it opened with a sting: *you are not as rational as you think.*

**If you have a brain, you have biases.** He walked straight into the catalogue. He'd rated his well-dressed, confident analyst highest — **halo effect**. He'd marked an average presenter poorly only because a brilliant one went just before — **contrast effect**. He'd blamed a missed deadline on one man's character while excusing his own slips on circumstances — **fundamental attribution error** against others, **self-serving bias** for himself. In the **Jensen Shoe Company** case, a manager and her subordinate lived the *same events as two different realities* — proof that **perceptual distortion, not lack of talent, breaks relationships.**

The relief was practical: you can't delete biases, but with **awareness, feedback and honest criteria** you can manage them. Kabir redid his ratings against a fixed standard, not his gut.

> The confusion: *my ratings are objective.* The clarity: every brain shortcuts — name the bias, check it against evidence, and judge against a standard, not a mood.

## Chapter 8: The man who said no to a raise (Sessions 9–10)

Meera, the quiet star from Chapter 3, turned down a retention bonus. Money wasn't the lever. The two motivation classes handed Kabir the toolbox he'd been missing.

First the **content** theories — *what* drives people. **Herzberg**: salary and policies are **hygiene** — they stop dissatisfaction but never create motivation; the **motivators** are the work itself, recognition, growth. Meera had good hygiene and empty work — textbook **quiet quitting**. **Maslow** placed people on a ladder of needs; **McClelland** sorted them by a dominant need (achievement, affiliation, power); **Self-Determination Theory** named the three that, if starved, kill motivation *even at high pay* — **Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness.** And the warning: over-rewarding everything triggers the **crowding-out effect**, displacing the inner drive.

Then the **process** theories — *how* motivation flows. **Goal-setting** (specific, challenging, SMART); **equity** (we compare our effort-to-reward ratio with peers'); **expectancy** (**Effort → Performance → Reward**, and if any link is zero, motivation is zero); **self-efficacy** (confidence, from four sources); **reinforcement**. The capstone case, **Crutchfield** — two near-identical teams, one thriving, one withering — proved the hardest truth: **motivation only becomes performance when the environment lets it.** Back to **B = f(P, E).** Meera had the motivation; Kabir's environment had been smothering it.

> The confusion: *pay her more and she'll re-engage.* The clarity: hygiene keeps the peace, motivators light the fire — protect autonomy, competence and relatedness, and the raise becomes beside the point.

## Chapter 9: The snake under the table (Session 11)

Under deadline pressure, Kabir snapped at his team in a review — and watched the room go cold for a week. The emotions class explained both the outburst and the fallout.

Emotions aren't noise to suppress; they're **survival hardware**. We're born with **six universal emotions** (anger, happiness, surprise, disgust, sadness, fear — four of six negative, hence our **negativity bias**), and each does a job — *fear*, the professor said, is the jolt that saves you from the snake under the table. The headline landed on Kabir's exact gap: **EQ beats IQ.** IQ is fixed like your height; **EQ is trainable like a muscle**, and it decides *how far you rise.* **Goleman's five components** gave him the sequence — **know yourself, control yourself, drive yourself, feel others (empathy), connect with them.** Empathy, he learned, is the core — *manipulators can fake sympathy, but empathy can't be faked.*

Two ideas explained the frozen week. **Emotional contagion** — a leader's mood spreads, so his panic had become the team's. And **Affective Events Theory** — repeated emotional hits harden into lasting attitudes. His one bad evening had been an "event" with a long tail.

> The confusion: *I'm brilliant at the work — why can't I hold the room?* The clarity: IQ lands the job; EQ carries you up — and your mood is a broadcast the whole team catches.

## Chapter 10: Competent, and still sinking (Sessions 12–13)

Kabir's team was nine genuinely talented people — and as a unit they underperformed, sometimes losing to decisions any one of them could have made better alone. The team sessions explained the paradox: **competence is necessary but not sufficient.**

A good team aligns **Four C's** — **Competence, Capabilities, Compatibility, Cohesion** — and each has an instrument. **Belbin**: a team needs a balance of **thinkers, doers and supporters**, not nine of the same. **FIRO**: compatibility comes from matching **expressed and wanted** needs for inclusion, control and affection — one person who wants to lead fits one who wants to be led. **Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions**: a pyramid that starts at **absence of trust** — and the trust that matters is **vulnerability-based**, safe enough to admit a mistake and ask for help.

The **Desert Survival** exercise drove it home: teams can beat their best member — or, through **groupthink**, pull their strongest voice into silence and do *worse*. Kabir saw his own team: all doers, little trust, the quiet experts conceding to keep the peace. The fix wasn't more talent. It was **trust, role balance and the courage to disagree well.**

> The confusion: *I hired stars, so the team should shine.* The clarity: talent is the baseline — trust sits at the centre, and a team of clones clashes while a balanced cast clicks.

## Chapter 11: My way, your way, or a better way (Session 14)

Two of Kabir's people stopped speaking — a clash of styles that was poisoning the group. He wanted to stamp it out. The conflict class corrected him: **conflict isn't always bad.**

Split it by type: **task** conflict (about the work) and **process** conflict (about roles and resources) can be **functional**; **relationship** conflict (egos and personalities) is **always dysfunctional** — and that was his pair's problem. The **Thomas–Kilmann** model gave him five styles on **assertiveness × cooperativeness** — **competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating** — and the rule that **no style is best; it depends on the situation.** Emergencies want competing; trivia wants avoiding; durable fixes want collaborating.

He learned to **negotiate**, too: **distributive** bargaining splits a fixed pie (win–lose, like a salary haggle), **integrative** grows it (win–win, like a partnership). And when two parties are stuck, a **third party** steps in — a **mediator** (no power), **arbitrator** (decides), or **conciliator** (rebuilds trust). The **TKC Consulting** case taught the sharpest lesson of all: the **protagonist is the one who must decide**, not the victim — and "I'd quit" is never the answer.

> The confusion: *conflict is failure — kill it.* The clarity: keep the useful kind, defuse the toxic kind, and match your style to the moment rather than fighting every clash the same way.

## Chapter 12: The water he was already swimming in (Session 15)

A reorganisation rippled through Kabir's floor, and suddenly good work wasn't enough — alliances and visibility were deciding outcomes. He found it distasteful and tried to ignore it. The power-and-politics class told him that was itself a choice.

**Power is the capacity to influence** — from a formal position (**legitimate, reward, coercive**) or from personal sources (**expert, referent**) — and **referent and expert power beat the formal kinds; coercion is weakest.** **Organizational politics** is inevitable: *if it's the water, political skill is your swimming.* And political skill, crucially, is **positive, not manipulation** — four dimensions: **social astuteness, interpersonal influence, networking ability, apparent sincerity.**

The cautionary tale was **Caroline Reges**, a VP who was *right* about the engineering and still lost her role, because she never read the board-level power game or built allies — strong only on sincerity. Her mirror image, **Ria**, read the politics, built a trust-based web, and won. The difference between office politics and political skill, the professor said, is the method: you win by **understanding and earning trust**, not by harming anyone. The one piece the Dark Triad always lacks is **apparent sincerity.** Kabir stopped treating politics as beneath him and started building genuine relationships before he needed them.

> The confusion: *if my work is good, politics shouldn't matter.* The clarity: you can't drain the pool — learn to swim, by reading the room, connecting widely, and staying genuinely trustworthy.

## Chapter 13: From manager to leader (Sessions 16–17)

Kabir got his own hard feedback: strong delivery, weak on people — passed over, once, for the same reason as every case protagonist. The role-transition and leadership classes showed him the way through, and that it could be *learned.*

In **Coaching McKenna Lane**, a star with the identical gap is given a year of **executive coaching** — which, the professor stressed, is about **unblocking potential, not fixing problems.** The test for whether change is real became Kabir's own yardstick: **Relational** practices (small outward gestures) can be faked, but **Independent** practices (change from within) and **Self-generation** (sustaining it with no coach) prove **authentic transformation.** *Small changes make a big difference* — listen, check in, let others shine, seek input instead of only giving feedback.

Then the full leadership map. Leadership is a **skill** (so it's trainable); the modern theories are **behavioural** (leaders are made, balancing task and people) and **contingency** (it depends). The **Full Range Model** runs **laissez-faire → transactional → transformational**, the last built on the **Four I's.** The Big Five split he underlined twice: **extraversion gets you noticed (emergence); conscientiousness makes you effective.** And the governing lesson echoed the whole course: **there is no single best style — carry a toolkit and switch by situation**, just as there's no single best way to manage a person.

> The confusion: *either you're a born leader or you're not.* The clarity: leadership is trained from the inside out — and the real skill is carrying many styles and reading which one the moment demands.

## Chapter 14: Two wrong choices (Session 18)

Near the end, Kabir faced a genuine dilemma — a reporting line where every option hurt someone. The ethics class gave him three ways to think it through instead of one gut reaction.

First, a distinction: **ethics is the external rulebook; morality is your own inner compass** — and they often disagree. An **ethical dilemma** is a forced choice where **both options are wrong.** The professor ran the class through the **trolley problem**: almost everyone will *divert* a trolley to save five (counting outcomes) but refuse to *push a man* to save the same five — even though the maths is identical. Our focus silently shifts from the **outcome** to the **act.**

That exposed the three lenses: **Utilitarianism** (judge by consequences — greatest good for the greatest number, but blind to individual rights), **Deontology** (judge by the action and duty — some things you simply don't do), and **Virtue Ethics** (judge by character — *what kind of person am I becoming?*). Kabir learned to run a hard choice past all three — the maths, the rulebook, and the mirror — and to notice when "the greater good" was quietly trampling someone's rights.

> The confusion: *there must be one correct answer.* The clarity: hold a dilemma up to all three lenses — consequences, duty, and character — and choose with your eyes open.

## Chapter 15: The mirror that spoke back (Sessions 19–20)

The course closed not with a lecture but with his own **Big Five project.** Kabir's team interviewed each other, then compared those peer views with their own test scores — and the gap was the whole lesson.

He thought he was organised; his peers barely noticed. He rated himself low on warmth; his team saw it clearly. The gap had a name he'd met in week eight — a **blind spot** — and the only way to close it was **triangulation**: *never trust a single data point*, combine the test, your reflection, and what others see. **Perception is not reality**, even about yourself.

The professor's parting counsel tied the knot. **Traits are enduring, but behaviour is workable**: identify the traits a role rewards, compare your profile, and deliberately close the gap. There is no perfect 10 in a subject about human beings — only well-reasoned, honest work. Kabir finally had an answer for Mrs. Iyer. *Can you manage the people?* **It depends** — on reading each person's P-factors, on protecting what motivates them, on his own emotional intelligence and political skill, and on switching his style to fit the moment. He could read the room at last.

> The confusion: *I know exactly who I am.* The clarity: you can't know yourself from one viewpoint — run the test, ask the peers, sit with the gap, and work on what the room rewards.

## What Kabir kept

Twenty weeks earlier, Kabir thought the promotion was a reward for being the best at the work. By the end he knew the work had been the easy part — the hard part was learning to read the **people** it ran through.

He'd forget the exact frameworks in a decade. What he meant to keep was smaller and sturdier: **no two people are the same, so read the P-factor first; behaviour is a person meeting an environment; most of a person sits below the waterline; you can't delete your biases, only manage them; EQ carries you further than IQ; trust sits at the centre of every team; politics is water you learn to swim in; and no single style — of leading, fighting or deciding — is ever "best."**

The professor's parting habit became his own — a few honest minutes with the mirror, and a standing invitation to his team to hold it up for him. And when Mrs. Iyer next asked whether he could manage the people, Kabir didn't flinch. He gave her a method instead of a boast — and that, he realised, was the whole point of the course.
