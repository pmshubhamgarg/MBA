# Session 11: The Snake Under the Table — and Why EQ Outranks IQ

*Emotions aren't noise to switch off — they're survival hardware we're all born with. And the intelligence that decides how far you rise isn't IQ; it's EQ, the one kind of intelligence you can actually train.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** **Discrete Emotions Theory** and the **six universal emotions** (2 positive, 4 negative → negativity bias); the **functions of emotions**; **Emotional Intelligence (EI/EQ)** and **Goleman's five components**; **IQ vs EQ** (fixed vs trainable; the 20% / 70% stats); **empathy vs sympathy**; **emotional labor**; **emotional contagion**; **Affective Events Theory**.

**Important (supporting):** The **self vs others** split inside EI; **blind spots** and **illusory superiority bias**; "leadership is how others view you, not how you view yourself"; the **Big Five ↔ EI** links (agreeableness strongest, neuroticism negative); the introvert myth; EI shaped by age, gender and personality.

**Context (colour for answers):** Satya Nadella and the Microsoft "know-it-all to learn-it-all" turnaround; Indra Nooyi's letters to employees' parents; Howard Schultz / Starbucks; Ratan Tata, Narayana Murthy, Azim Premji; Travis Kalanick (Uber) and Jack Ma as low-EQ cautionary tales; Sheldon Cooper; the snake-under-the-table and sitcom laugh-track examples.
:::

## Setting the Stage: A Class About Feelings

Tonight the professor parks the frameworks for a moment and asks the class something unusually human: *can you even imagine a life without emotions?* Take away just **love** and **fear** and life stops making sense. That is the whole session in one question — emotions are not a weakness to manage away, they are the machinery that keeps us alive and connected.

She lays out the plan: what emotions **are**, what they **do**, and then the four ideas every manager must carry — **emotional intelligence**, **emotional labor**, **emotional contagion**, and one theory, **Affective Events Theory**.

She warms the room up with a string of "have you ever wondered why…" questions — why we dwell on bad news more than good, why flight attendants stay calm and smiling, why a sad movie makes you cry. Hold those three; each one is a concept in disguise.

---

## Big Idea 1: The Six Emotions You Were Born With

**Concept.** The professor's anchor is **Discrete Emotions Theory**: emotions were **built into us over the course of evolution because they support our survival.** Every human is born with a small, innate set of **basic (core / universal) emotions** that are **recognisable across cultures** — and all our other, more complex feelings are just **combinations** of these.

The **six universal emotions** are **anger, happiness, surprise, disgust, sadness and fear.** We literally **wear them on our face** — you can read them on a stranger without a word.

> **The professor:** "All of us are born with six basic emotions, these emotions have been found across cultures, and they are hardwired in us to support our survival."

Now split the six: **happiness and surprise** are the two **positive** ones; **anger, fear, disgust and sadness** are the four **negative** ones.

**Why it matters.** Four of our six innate emotions are negative — so **we are wired to respond more strongly to negative events than to positive ones.** A student nailed the reason: it is a **survival instinct** — the brain treats bad news as a risk and spends more time on it. When everything is going well, nobody stops to reflect.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Discrete Emotions Theory = six hardwired, cross-cultural emotions (anger, happiness, surprise, disgust, sadness, fear); everything else is a mix of these.** Because **4 of the 6 are negative**, humans have a built-in **negativity bias** — we react harder to negative events. This bias is not a flaw; it is a survival mechanism.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "SADFHS: four storms, two sunrises"]
**S**adness, **A**nger, **D**isgust, **F**ear (the four storms) + **H**appiness, **S**urprise (the two sunrises). Four negatives to two positives — that ratio is exactly why bad news sticks.
:::

## Big Idea 2: Every Emotion Has a Job

Emotions feel messy, but each one is doing **useful work.** The professor's favourite illustration: imagine a **snake under your table.** What happens if you feel **no fear**? You get bitten — you might die. Fear fires instantly, your body flips into **fight-or-flight**, and you escape. The "useless" feeling just saved your life.

Map each emotion to its function:

| Emotion | Its job (function) |
|---|---|
| **Fear** | Protects us from danger — triggers fight-or-flight |
| **Anger** | Helps us fight our problems |
| **Disgust** | Helps us avoid what is not good for us |
| **Sadness** | Helps us connect with those we love; sharpens attention to detail |
| **Happiness** | Helps us grow; motivates us |
| **Surprise** | Helps us focus on a new situation |

Two combination-emotions she added: **guilt** stops us repeating mistakes and nudges us to change behaviour, and **envy / jealousy** — "one of the most common human emotions" — **motivates us to work hard.** Remove envy and we might stop pushing ourselves at all.

**Emotions vs logic.** A sharp exchange: how do emotions differ from logic? The class landed it well — **logic is reflective, emotions are reactive**; **logic is objective, emotions are subjective**; logic has a reason, emotions may not. Crucially, **emotions make us act fast; logic takes time.** Back to the snake: you don't calculate, you run. That speed is exactly why emotions were so vital to survival.

:::note[Good to Know — We Often Feel Two Things at Once]
The professor pointed out something we all recognise: we can feel **good and bad emotions at the same time** — pride mixed with anxiety, relief mixed with sadness. Emotions are rarely tidy, single signals.
:::

## Big Idea 3: EQ Beats IQ — The Leadership Multiplier

Here is the heart of the session. **Emotional Intelligence (EI)** is your ability to **recognise, understand, manage and influence emotions — your own and other people's.** It has two halves: **self** and **others.** The concept was popularised by **Professor Daniel Goleman** (an external scholar) in his bestseller *Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More Than IQ.*

First, separate the two intelligences:

| | **IQ (Intelligence Quotient)** | **EQ (Emotional Quotient)** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Cognitive ability, reasoning, working memory, technical know-how | Interpersonal skill — managing your own emotions and reading others' |
| **Can it change?** | Fixed / stable — like **height** (you grow to a point, then stop) | Trainable — like a **muscle** (the more you train it, the stronger it gets) |
| **Career role** | Can **land** you a job | Decides **how far you rise** up the hierarchy |

The numbers she quoted make the case: **only about 20% of your success as a manager or leader comes from IQ** — the major determinant is **EQ.** And people with a **high EQ and an average IQ outperform those with the highest IQ about 70% of the time.** The class name for the opposite profile? **Sheldon Cooper** — sky-high IQ, very low EQ, hopeless with people.

> **The professor:** "Your IQ can land you a job, but your EQ determines how far you rise up in the hierarchy."

Why does high EQ travel so well into leadership? Because high-EQ people are good at the exact things leaders need: **decision-making, relationship-building and networking, influence, conflict resolution, stress management and impulse control, communication, teamwork, resilience and adaptability.** That is a job description for a leader.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**IQ is relatively fixed; EQ is a trainable skill.** IQ can get you in the door, but **EQ determines how far you rise.** A good manager needs **three** skills — **technical, decision-making and interpersonal** — and the balance of **IQ + EQ**. If a case shows a brilliant-but-abrasive performer who can't lead, the gap is **low emotional intelligence**, and the fix is that EQ **can be developed with deliberate practice.**
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "IQ is your height, EQ is your muscle"]
You stop growing taller; you never stop being able to build muscle. **Use it or lose it** — EQ is the one intelligence you can keep training for life.
:::

## Big Idea 4: Goleman's Five Ingredients of EI

Goleman breaks emotional intelligence into **five components** — the **first three are about the SELF**, the **last two are about OTHERS.**

| # | Component | Aspect | What it means | Key sub-skills |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Self-awareness** | Self | Recognise your emotions, internal states and drives, and how they affect your behaviour and decisions; know your strengths and weaknesses | — |
| 2 | **Self-regulation** | Self | Manage your impulses and reactions; stay controlled and adaptable | Impulse control, adaptability, trustworthiness |
| 3 | **Motivation** | Self | Driven from **within**, beyond external rewards | Achievement orientation, commitment, optimism |
| 4 | **Empathy** | Others | Understand and **feel** others' emotions and perspective | Active listening, developing others |
| 5 | **Social skill** | Others | Build networks and relationships, inspire and persuade | Conflict management, collaboration |

The professor's plain-language chain is worth memorising: **know yourself → control yourself → drive yourself → feel what others feel → connect with and influence others.** And since **leadership is the process of influencing and motivating others**, being emotionally intelligent is, almost by definition, being leadership-ready.

**Empathy vs sympathy** (a student pushed hard on this, and it's a classic trap):

- **Sympathy** = you **understand / know** that someone is sad.
- **Empathy** = you **understand *and* feel** what they feel — and then interact to actually help them.

> **The professor:** "People with a dark personality lack empathy, but they can fake sympathy. You cannot fake empathy."

That line is the tell: **empathy is the core of EI**, and it is the one component manipulators can't counterfeit.

:::tip[Important — The Core Is Empathy]
Of the five components, **empathy is the heart of emotional intelligence.** It is what lets you build the relationships that make influence possible. Sympathy can be performed; empathy is felt. In an answer, treat empathy as the hinge between the "self" skills and the "others" skills.
:::

## Big Idea 5: Myths and Links — Introverts, and the Big Five

**The introvert myth.** A student worried that "reserved" people simply lack social skills — so how can EQ be trainable for them? The professor pushed back firmly: **introversion is not poor social skill.** Introverts communicate well, build strong relationships and make excellent leaders — they just **prefer a smaller, closer, more tightly connected circle.** Her examples: **Bill Gates** and **Sundar Pichai**, both introverts, both run giants. Preferring a small group is a *preference*, not a ceiling on influence.

**How personality feeds EI.** Emotional intelligence is a skill, but your **Big Five** traits tilt the starting line:

| Big Five trait | Link to emotional intelligence |
|---|---|
| **Agreeableness** | **Strongest positive** link — its sub-traits include **empathy**, altruism, trustworthiness |
| **Neuroticism** (low emotional stability) | **Negative** link — anxiety, low confidence, vulnerability work against EI |
| **Extraversion** | **Moderate** — sociability, warmth and energy support social skill |
| **Conscientiousness** | **Moderate** — discipline and reliability help, but tilt toward perfection |
| **Openness** | Linked to reading perspectives and understanding internal states |

:::note[Good to Know — EI Is Shaped by More Than Effort]
Pressed by students, the professor agreed EI is also influenced by **age, gender and personality.** Women are often considered **more empathetic** (empathy is strongly gendered), while **self-awareness** has little to do with gender. None of this cancels the headline: EI is still a **skill you can train** with continuous, deliberate practice.
:::

## Big Idea 6: Leaders Who Prove the Point

The professor stress-tested EI against real leaders, and the class filled in the evidence.

**High EQ:**

- **Satya Nadella (Microsoft).** Pre-2014 Microsoft was competitive but **stagnant** — cut-throat internal rivalry, little collaboration, a declining business. Nadella brought **empathy**: he emphasised **listening**, fostered teamwork, and shifted the culture from **"know-it-all" to "learn-it-all."** The turnaround was massive — happier, more productive employees and a share-price rally. (Students noted his empathy was partly shaped by raising a son with a health condition.)
- **Indra Nooyi (PepsiCo).** She wrote **personal letters to the parents of her top executives**, thanking them for their child's contribution. Loyalty jumped, turnover fell, and a strong leadership pipeline formed. The lesson: **empathy builds loyalty, and loyalty builds performance.**
- **Howard Schultz (Starbucks).** Deep care for staff and heavy training built loyalty and lifted the hospitality that Starbucks sells.
- **Indian high-EQ names** the class raised: **Ratan Tata** (an **ethical** leader), **Narayana Murthy** (an **authentic** leader), **Azim Premji, Shiv Nadar, Anand Mahindra.**

> **The professor:** "Empathy in leadership is not soft. It's powerful."

**Low EQ (cautionary):**

- **Travis Kalanick (Uber)** famously argued with a driver on camera — prompting Goleman's own article arguing *what the Uber CEO needs is emotional intelligence.* IQ alone doesn't make a leader.
- **Jack Ma** (a heated public spat with Elon Musk) and figures like **Steve Jobs / Elon Musk** showed **narcissistic** streaks — "I, me, myself." Narcissism is **not** emotional intelligence.

:::tip[Important — Don't Judge a Leader on One Incident]
A student raised Narayana Murthy's controversial "70-hour week" remark. The professor's measured reply is a good exam instinct: **don't build a negative perception from one or two incidents.** Murthy's defining pattern is **authenticity and transparency**, so that is how you classify him. Judge leaders on their **consistent style**, not a single headline.
:::

## Big Idea 7: The Dangerous Gap — Blind Spots

The class took a quick **15-item EI self-test** (first natural response only — overthinking distorts a psychometric test). Most scored medium-to-high. Then came the twist.

> **The professor:** "Those who scored high — are you superhumans? Are you the next Satya or Indra?"

The honest explanation for inflated scores is **illusory superiority bias** (we hold inflated views of ourselves), reinforced by **social desirability bias** (we rate ourselves as more positive, less negative). This is where **blind spots** appear.

A **blind spot** is the **gap between how you see yourself and how others see you.** They are dangerous for two reasons: first, if you *believe* you're high on empathy, **you stop working on it**; second, that gap **quietly damages your influence** on the people around you. And the gap cuts both ways — **overestimators** (self-rating higher than peers) *and* **underestimators** (self-rating lower) both have blind spots.

> **The professor:** "Leadership is not about how you view yourself. It's about how others view you."

The fix the class practised: get a **partner or peer to rate you**, compare against your self-rating, find the gap, and write a short **action plan** to close it.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "The gap is the danger"]
Self-view **minus** others' view = your **blind spot.** If the gap is wide, you'll coast on a strength you don't actually have — and your influence leaks. Shrink the gap by **asking for feedback**, not by trusting your own test score.
:::

## Big Idea 8: Emotional Labor — The Smile You Don't Feel

Back to the flight attendant. Why are hospitality and airline staff **always calm and smiling**, even while passengers panic? Because their job **requires** that face. When you **suppress your real emotions and display the emotions your organisation wants**, that is **emotional labor.**

It sounds harmless, but the professor flagged the cost: faking emotions all day leads to **emotional exhaustion** and **drags down employee productivity.** It is extremely common in service organisations and a real hidden tax on well-being.

> **The professor:** "When we suppress our real emotions and display organisationally desired emotions, that is known as emotional labor."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Emotional labor = suppressing genuine emotion to display the emotion the job demands** (the ever-cheerful flight attendant). Its downside is **emotional exhaustion** and **lower productivity.** If a case shows front-line staff burning out despite "good service scores," name emotional labor as the mechanism.
:::

---

## Big Idea 9: Emotional Contagion — Moods Are Catching

Why do you cry at a sad film, smile back when someone smiles, or laugh along with a sitcom's **laugh track**? Because of **emotional contagion** — **a process of social influence in which one person's emotions transfer to another.**

This is a direct lever for leaders. **Positive, optimistic leaders spread positivity; abusive leaders spread negativity** — because the team *catches* whatever the leader is radiating. So if you want to lift a room, **manage your own mood first.**

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Smile and they smile back"]
Emotions are contagious. A sitcom adds a laugh track for a reason — and a leader's calm (or panic) spreads the same way. **Your mood is a broadcast.**
:::

## Big Idea 10: Affective Events Theory — Events → Emotions → Behaviour

The session's named theory ties it all together. **Affective Events Theory** says that **workplace events trigger emotions, and those emotions — accumulated over time — turn into workplace attitudes and behaviours.**

- **Positive events** (a supportive boss) → positive emotions → higher **work engagement, job satisfaction and productivity.**
- **Negative events** (an abusive boss) → negative emotions → lower engagement, satisfaction and productivity.

So when the professor asks "why are employees productive under a supportive supervisor and unproductive under an abusive one?", the answer chains two ideas: **emotional contagion** spreads the boss's mood, and **Affective Events Theory** explains how those repeated emotional hits harden into lasting attitudes and behaviour. The biggest **source** of these workplace emotions? **Leadership style.**

:::note[Good to Know — This Loops Back to Motivation]
Session 10 argued the environment decides whether motivation becomes performance. Affective Events Theory is the emotional engine underneath that claim: the manager's behaviour is a stream of "events" that quietly accumulates into how engaged — or how checked-out — a team becomes.
:::
