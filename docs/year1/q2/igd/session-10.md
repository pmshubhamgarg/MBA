# Session 10: Two Teams, One Roof — How Motivation Actually Happens

*The earlier sessions told us **what** motivates people. This one flips to **how** motivation happens — through clear goals, fairness, a visible effort-to-reward chain, confidence, and reinforcement. Then the Crutchfield case drops two near-identical teams under one roof and shows the hard truth: the environment decides whether motivation ever becomes performance.*

## Quick Recap

The professor opened by stitching together the earlier sessions, because today builds directly on them.

- **Motivation has two flavours:** *intrinsic* (from the work itself) and *extrinsic* (money, rewards). Over-feeding extrinsic motivation can **kill** intrinsic motivation, and its effects are short-run — remove the reward and the motivation is gone. Intrinsic motivation is slower to build but **sustainable and long-lasting**.
- **A theory** is simply a set of ideas that helps us understand human behaviour, and these apply to everybody.
- Theories split into **content theories** (they explain *what* motivates us) and **process theories** (they explain *how* motivation occurs).

The content theories already covered:

| Content theory | One-line idea |
|---|---|
| **Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs** | Needs stack in a pyramid: physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualisation |
| **Herzberg — Two-Factor** | Satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposites; **motivators** (intrinsic) truly motivate, **hygiene factors** only prevent dissatisfaction |
| **McClelland — Needs** | Three *learned* needs — achievement, power, affiliation — present in everyone in varying degrees, one dominant |
| **Self-Determination Theory** | Three *innate* needs — competence, autonomy, relatedness; meet them and you get intrinsic motivation |

> **The professor:** "Content theories deal with what motivates employees. Now in this session we'll discuss the process theory, which helps us understand how motivation occurs."

## Process Theory 1: Goal-Setting — Fantasies vs SMART Goals

**Concept.** When goals are **specific, clear and challenging**, all your energy and effort get *channelled* toward them. That focus feels motivating, motivation lifts performance, and **timely, positive, constructive feedback** pushes motivation higher still. Vague, easy goals do the opposite — people drift.

The shorthand is **SMART goals**: **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**ime-bound.

**Class example — the one-line test.** "I want to be rich" versus "I want to save ₹10 lakh in the next five years." The first is a **fantasy**; the second is a **SMART goal** — measurable, time-bound, achievable.

> **The professor:** "First one is our fantasy. Second one is our smart goal — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time bound."

**Why people still don't set goals.** Two reasons the professor pulled out: (1) we confuse **fantasies** for goals, and (2) we were **never taught** to set our own goals — parents set them in childhood, so later we accept a supervisor's goals without objection. The fix: **participative, mutually-decided** goals.

**The study she used.** A famous Harvard goal-setting study (as the professor told it): MBA graduates were asked if they had clear, written goals. **83%** had none, **14%** had goals but not written, **3%** had written goals with an action plan. Twenty years later, the **14%** were earning roughly **10 times** the no-goal group, and the **3% with written goals and plans about 30 times** more. The lesson: **writing goals down and planning them raises the odds you achieve them.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Goal-Setting Theory (Locke and Latham):** specific + challenging + accepted goals, plus feedback, drive motivation and performance. In a case, spot vague or imposed goals as a motivation killer, and prescribe **SMART, participatively-set goals with regular positive feedback.** This is a *process* theory — the focus is the mechanism, not the underlying drive.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Fantasy vs SMART"]
"I want to be rich" is a wish. "₹10 lakh in 5 years" is a plan. If a goal has no number and no deadline, it is a fantasy, not a goal.
:::

## Process Theory 2: Equity — The Brain That Always Compares

**Concept.** People want **fairness in how rewards are distributed**. We instinctively compare our own **input:output ratio** (effort, hours, results vs pay, recognition) *and* we compare that ratio with our **colleagues'**. Feel fairness → satisfied and motivated. Feel inequity → anger, frustration, lower engagement, dropping motivation.

**Class example — Priya and Karan.** Both joined a consulting firm as MBA interns at the same time, same top institutes, equally demanding client projects. Priya discovers **Karan got a higher bonus and more public recognition for similar results.** Her motivation falls, she stops volunteering for stretch work, and she quietly starts job-hunting. Classic equity breakdown.

> **The professor:** "Fairness can motivate you, or unfairness demotivates you — and we have this tendency to compare our outputs with those of our colleagues."

**The manager's move — objective vs subjective.** Some criteria are **objective** (clear to all); some are **subjective**. Because employees *will* compare, a manager should **sit down and explain the reasons** behind a rating, ideally quantified against the quarter's work, so people understand why outcomes differ instead of assuming unfairness.

:::tip[Important]
Equity is about **perception**, not just the actual numbers. Two people may genuinely have contributed differently, but if one *perceives* unfair treatment, the demotivation is real. That is why **communication and transparent, explainable criteria** are the equity lever a manager actually controls.
:::

## Process Theory 3: Expectancy — The Motivation Chain

**Concept (Vroom's Expectancy Theory).** People feel motivated when they believe their **effort will lead to performance, performance will lead to an outcome, and that outcome is something they actually want.** It is a chain:

**Effort → Performance → Reward (and the reward must be desirable)**

Three links:

| Link | The question you ask yourself | Term |
|---|---|---|
| Effort → Performance | "Will my hard work actually produce results?" | **Expectancy** |
| Performance → Reward | "If I perform, will I truly be rewarded?" | **Instrumentality** |
| Value of the reward | "Do I even want this outcome?" | **Valence** |

The killer rule: **if any one link is zero, motivation is zero.** All three must be present and strong.

**Class example — Rahul.** An MBA grad at a fast-growing fintech startup. His manager set an ambitious quarterly goal and promised a bonus. Rahul worked hard — but at quarter-end the **goalpost shifted**: only the top 10 performers got the bonus, and the criteria were never clear. Despite strong performance, Rahul got nothing. He questioned the effort-to-reward link and dialled back to the **bare minimum** next quarter. The **instrumentality link snapped**, so motivation collapsed.

This is also *why* people feel crushed when **someone else takes credit** for their work: effort is there, performance is there, but the reward link is broken.

> **The professor:** "If any of the component is zero, your motivation is zero. All three factors should be positive and strong for motivation to occur."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Can I? Will it pay? Do I want it?"]
The three expectancy questions in order: **Can I do it?** (expectancy) → **Will it pay off?** (instrumentality) → **Do I want the payoff?** (valence). One "no" anywhere and motivation dies.
:::

## Process Theory 4: Self-Efficacy — Confidence Drives Motivation

**Concept.** When you **believe you can handle a challenging task**, you feel motivated, and that motivation turns into performance. Self-efficacy is belief in your own capability, and the theory maps **where that belief comes from.**

**Class example — Nisha.** An MBA student asked to lead her team in a national business simulation, despite limited experience. Her professor **encouraged her** (reminding her of past analytical and leadership wins), she **watched classmates succeed** in similar roles, and she got **constructive feedback in practice rounds**. Her confidence grew, she took initiative, and led the team to a third-place finish.

**The four sources of self-efficacy:**

| Source | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Mastery experience** | You have succeeded at similar tasks before |
| **Vicarious learning** | You have seen others around you succeed ("if they can, I can") |
| **Verbal persuasion** | Encouragement and positive feedback from a boss or peers |
| **Emotional / physiological state** | A calm, relaxed, cool state of mind |

> **The professor:** "This theory suggests that confidence drives motivation."

**Manager application:** give **positive, constructive feedback**, and use **vicarious learning** — show an employee examples of colleagues who delivered similar projects well, so they believe they can too.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Me, Models, Mentor, Mood"]
The four sources of self-efficacy: **Me** (mastery), **Models** (vicarious learning), **Mentor** (verbal persuasion), **Mood** (emotional state).
:::

## Process Theory 5: Reinforcement — The Carrot and the Stick

**Concept.** Behaviour is shaped by its **consequences**. Reinforce the behaviour you want.

- **Positive reinforcement** — add something *desirable* to strengthen good behaviour: praise, recognition, appreciation, rewards. Since you cannot hand out money every time, **praise and recognition** become everyday motivators.
- **Negative reinforcement** — *remove* something negative to strengthen behaviour. Example: once an employee starts delivering, you **stop monitoring** them; taking away the irritant reinforces the good behaviour.

This is the plain carrot-and-stick view: do something good and you are rewarded or praised; do something bad and the rewards stop.

:::note[Good to Know]
The professor reminded the class that these theories aren't armchair inventions — researchers **observe real employees over time, collect data in organisations, and then propose the theory.** As she put it, "these theories are based on your work experiences only." Good line to defend *why* OB theory matters in an exam answer.
:::

## The Motivation Toolkit (The Manager's Diagnostic Checklist)

This is the session's most practical takeaway: when a team is flat, don't guess — run down a **checklist**, where each question maps to a theory. If any factor is missing, motivation leaks there.

| Ask yourself... | Theory it draws on |
|---|---|
| Is this person driven **intrinsically or extrinsically**? | Intrinsic vs extrinsic |
| What **level of need** is currently met? | Maslow |
| Are **motivators** present, or only **hygiene factors**? | Herzberg |
| What **drives this individual** — achievement, power, or affiliation? | McClelland |
| Are **autonomy, competence, relatedness** supported? | Self-Determination |
| Are goals **specific, clear and engaging**? | Goal-Setting |
| Are work and rewards **fair and equitable**? | Equity |
| Are there **clear rewards for effort**? | Expectancy |
| Do they **believe in their own capability**? | Self-Efficacy |
| Is the **right behaviour being reinforced**? | Reinforcement |

**The most common management mistake** the professor flagged: pouring everything into **hygiene factors** (salary, HR policies, nice culture, good supervision) and ignoring **motivators** (meaningful work, recognition, achievement, growth). Hygiene stops complaints; it does not create 100% effort.

> **The professor:** "If you want 100% from your employees, just focus on the content of the work itself... Extrinsic motivation is very easy. But intrinsic motivation is a challenge."

**You don't need to rewrite the job.** The professor's repeated point: make **small changes in how you approach the work and how you approach people** — explain why work is meaningful, involve people in goal-setting, say a genuine "hi, how's it going, need help?" (relatedness), give positive reinforcement. It is a **lifelong practice**, built on experimentation and patience.

:::note[Good to Know — Quiet Quitting]
The professor tied **quiet quitting** (doing only the bare minimum) directly to this mistake: organisations obsess over hygiene factors and miss the **content of the work itself**, so employees disengage. Name this link if a case mentions disengaged, "just-enough" employees.
:::

## The Case: Lumen vs Absorb at Crutchfield Chemical Engineering

Two student groups analysed the classic intrinsic-motivation-and-creativity case, and the professor used it to tie every theory together.

**The setup.** Crutchfield Chemical Engineering (CCE) is a subsidiary of a **USD 29 billion** conglomerate (PPQ Worldwide) that shifted from commodity chemicals to high-value specialty products. Its elite R&D hub had just **cut 18% of its workforce**, leaving insecurity and "rumours of doom and gloom." Director **Paul Burke** runs five R&D teams and notices a puzzling gap: under the *same* conditions, one team thrives and one withers.

**The people:**

| Person | Role | Style |
|---|---|---|
| **Paul Burke** | Director, polymer / CTD division | Analytical; retained top talent; but sits far from day-to-day team life |
| **Max Cooper** | Lumen team lead — "the catalyst" | Supportive, empowering; shares ownership, shields the team, welcomes feedback |
| **Chip Wendel** | Absorb team lead — "the roadblock" | Controlling, micromanaging; filters ideas for politics, harsh feedback |
| **Joanna McKinty** | External organisational psychologist | Ran a **two-week diary study** plus the **WPI (Work Preference Inventory)** |

**The data.** McKinty's diary study scored daily intrinsic motivation on a 1–7 scale. **Lumen scored about 5 out of 7; Absorb about 3.3 out of 7.** Her conclusion: the gap came from **leadership style and work environment — not individual skill.**

**Every theory, mapped to the two teams:**

| Theory | Lumen (motivated) | Absorb (demotivated) |
|---|---|---|
| **Herzberg** | Motivators present | Motivators *and* hygiene missing |
| **Maslow** | Esteem + self-actualisation met | Belonging + esteem unmet |
| **Equity** | Fair rewards for effort | Under-appreciated vs Lumen |
| **Expectancy** | Clear effort → performance → reward | Weak link; unclear goals |
| **Self-Determination** | Autonomy, competence, relatedness met | Controlled; low autonomy, low connection |
| **Goal-Setting** | Clear, challenging, accepted goals | Vague / unaccepted; low commitment |
| **Reinforcement** | Recognition and praise | Negative feedback; no positive reinforcement |

**The recommended fix (phased):** Phase 1 (first 3 months) — Burke has an honest, data-backed conversation with Chip, *and* empowers the Absorb team by approving their pivot to a new customer, restoring purpose and repairing the broken expectancy link. Phase 2 — review at 3 months; if Chip improves he stays, otherwise move him to a senior **individual-contributor technical role** and assign a new leader. The deeper shift: from **need for power → need for achievement**, from **control → collaboration.**

## The Capstone: B = f(Person × Environment)

The professor's final framing — and a high-value exam line — is **Kurt Lewin's equation**: **Behaviour = f(Person, Environment)**, often written **B = f(P, E)**.

- **Person factor (P)** here = the employee's **motivation**.
- **Environment factor (E)** here = the **leadership style, politics, culture and fairness** around them.

Creativity shows up only when **motivation (P) meets a supportive environment (E).** Lumen had motivation *and* a supportive leader, so motivation became creativity. Absorb may have had motivated people, but a **suppressive environment** smothered it.

> **The professor:** "Behaviour is a function of person into situation. If motivation is there, the P factor is there — but if the E factor is suppressive, people will not be creative."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Motivation alone does not produce creativity or performance — the environment decides whether it converts.** Use **B = f(P, E)** to explain why two identical teams diverge: same people-potential, different leadership environment. This single equation lets you tie *all* the motivation theories into one answer.
:::

:::note[Good to Know — Contrast Effect]
After an "excellent" first presentation, the professor joked that the second group was hard to judge fairly because the **contrast effect** was "always at play" — we rate things relative to whatever came just before. A student protested, "but we learned it's not good to compare." It is a small preview of the **perception and emotional-intelligence** session coming after the midterm break.
:::
