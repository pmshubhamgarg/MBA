## Why a Central Bank at All?

He opened by re-asking a Session 1 question: why can't the RBI just be a department inside the Ministry of Finance? The class answered correctly — a government wants to **win elections**, so it cares about **output and growth**; the RBI is left to care about **inflation and prices**. Put money-printing in the hands of a re-election-seeking government and the temptation is obvious. So you split the power.

His cleaner analogy was the **judiciary versus the administration**. They are not enemies and not substitutes — they are **complementary**, "twin arms" that argue with each other. You keep them separate for the same reason any system keeps **checks and balances**.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "A former governor, Dr. Y.V. Reddy, once told me — of course I am independent, and the finance minister has allowed me to say so. In other words, there are limits to independence. It is not zero-or-one."

He added a subtle point students miss: the Prime Minister has something the RBI Governor does not — **legitimacy from the people**. A PM wins a franchise; a Governor (like a Supreme Court judge via the collegium) is picked by a **consultative process**, not a public vote. So the relationship is a careful conversation, not a command.

**Can a government force a repo cut before an election?** His answer was an unequivocal **no** — because the Governor does **not** set policy alone. Rates are decided by a **six-member Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**. The Governor may hold a casting vote, but bending all six to the government's will is virtually impossible. He named four governors he was personally "privy to" who clashed with the government of the day: **Y.V. Reddy, D. Subbarao** (a famous tiff with then–FM P. Chidambaram), **Raghuram Rajan**, and **Urjit Patel**.

:::tip[Important]
**Central bank independence is not 0 or 1.** It is a *complementary* relationship with limits, enforced by structure — a **six-member MPC**, not one person. That structure is the real reason an election-year government cannot simply order a rate cut.
:::

## The Two Levers: The Pocket (Fiscal) and the Pipe (Monetary)

This is the spine of the whole session. There are two ways to push output up, and they reach you through completely different routes.

**Fiscal policy is direct.** The government can either **raise its own spending (G)** or **cut your taxes (T)**. His picture: "I am an unemployed old man with no pension. The government runs a scheme for me, or simply puts ₹500 in my pocket." Either way I **spend**, the **multiplier** kicks in (`1 / (1 − MPC)`), and output rises. Cutting taxes does the same thing from the other side — more money stays in your hand, so you spend.

**Monetary policy is indirect.** Here is the twist he kept hammering: **none of us has an account with the RBI.** The person who actually controls your financial life is your **banker** — SBI, Axis, HDFC, ICICI — because that is who you go to for a **loan**.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The bank manager is God for you — he raises or lowers the rate on your housing loan, your vehicle loan, your credit card loan. The RBI does not give you money. The RBI only makes the banker's life either good or miserable."

So when the RBI cuts the **repo rate**, it is improving the banker's life and *hoping* the banker passes it on. If the banker pockets the gain instead, the policy is **"lost in translation"** — the formal word is **transmission**. In theory, competition forces the banker to pass it on (you'd switch from bank A to bank B); but that assumes **perfect competition**. If banks form a **cartel**, the RBI falls back on **moral suasion** — publicly announcing "I have cut, you have not."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Fiscal policy is direct; monetary policy is market-driven.** The government hands you money (G up or T down) and the multiplier does the rest. The RBI cannot touch you — it moves the **repo rate**, which changes your **banker's** incentives, and the effect only reaches you if banks **transmit** it. If a question asks "why is monetary policy slower / less reliable?", the one-word answer is **transmission**.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Pocket vs Pipe"]
**Fiscal = the Pocket** (cash straight into your hand). **Monetary = the Pipe** (the RBI opens a tap at the bank, and you only feel it if the pipe doesn't leak). Direct money vs a market signal.
:::

## How the Chain Actually Works

Both levers end at the same place — **investment** — because in `Y = C + I + G`, consumption depends on income, **G is fixed** (autonomous), and **investment depends on the interest rate (R)**. Lower R, higher I, bigger Y.

The money-market route is worth tracing, because the financial side has only **two assets: money and bonds**. When the RBI pushes **money supply up**, you hold a bit as cash and use the rest to buy **bonds**. Bond demand rises, bond supply is unchanged, so the **bond price rises** — and since bond price and interest rate move inversely, the **interest rate falls**. Lower rate → more investment → multiplier → more output.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "More money → dearer bonds → cheaper borrowing"]
Extra money chases bonds, bond prices climb, and because price and yield move in opposite directions, the **market interest rate drops**. That single inverse relationship is the engine of monetary policy.
:::

## Crowding Out (and Crowding In)

Here is the catch with fiscal policy. When the government raises **G**, output rises — but so does the **interest rate**. A higher rate pushes **private investment down**. Government spending goes up while private spending falls: one **crowds out** the other.

He made it vivid with the budget later: the government is the **obese borrower** in the room. If it walks into the market wanting ₹1 lakh crore (up from ₹75,000 crore last year), its credit is impeccable, it gets served first — and **you** are left with less, at a **higher** rate.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Government is a class-one loan taker — its credit is impeccable. So if the government borrows heavily, the private sector suffers."

Crowding out is only acceptable when the **private sector isn't investing anyway** — a recession, a downswing, a lack of demand. In normal times it's a real cost. And the reverse can happen too: **crowding in**. If the government builds a good road or solid **digital infrastructure**, private investment is *pulled in* behind it. His honest verdict: "it depends" — **infrastructure may crowd in; non-infrastructure tends to crowd out.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Crowding out** = government borrowing/spending pushes up the interest rate, which pushes **private investment down**. Justified *only* in a slump. **Crowding in** = productive public infrastructure *attracts* private investment. Expect "it depends" — then argue from the state of the business cycle and the *type* of spending.
:::

## The IS–LM Picture, in Words

The professor's rule: you must be able to *tell the story* without the chart. "You cannot start drawing an IS–LM diagram in a board meeting — the curves should be in your head while you speak in words." Here is both policies in one table.

| Policy | Curve that moves | Output (Y) | Interest (R) | Side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary fiscal** (G up / T down) | **IS shifts right** (IS₀ → IS₁) | **Up** | **Up** | **Crowding out** — higher R hits private investment |
| **Expansionary monetary** (M up / repo cut) | **LM shifts right** (LM₀ → LM₁) | **Up** | **Down** | No direct crowding out — R falls, investment rises |

One trap he flagged hard: the **R on the diagram is the *market* interest rate, not the repo rate.** The repo rate sits *underneath* the LM curve. A repo cut raises bank **loans** (an asset of the banking system); the matching **deposits** (liabilities) expand, so **money supply rises**, and *that* is what shifts LM. The Governor can't even move money supply directly — he works through the banks.

:::tip[Important]
When you think **monetary policy, think the LM curve**; when you think **government spending / fiscal policy, think the IS curve**. Every RBI tool is just a way to shift LM (inject or drain liquidity); every budget decision shifts IS.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Same Y, opposite R"]
Both expansions raise output. The giveaway is the **interest rate**: fiscal pushes **R up** (crowding out), monetary pulls **R down**. If an exam gives you the direction of R, you instantly know which lever was pulled.
:::

## The GST Cut: Policy You Can Watch Live

He turned the theory onto a current headline — the **GST rate reduction**. Open the economy and `Y = C + I + G + X − M`. Right now **X − M has fallen** (about −2% of GDP) because of **Mr. Trump's** tariffs — an **external headwind** (a headwind pushes the aircraft *back*; a tailwind pushes it forward). Something else must rise to fill the gap.

The government didn't want to raise **G** further, and it had **already** done heavy public **investment (I)**. So it chose to lift **consumption (C)** — by **cutting T** via GST reform. Lower T raises disposable income `(Y − T)`, which raises C, which multiplies into Y. "Very clever, very smart of the government," he said.

The giveaway that this was counter-cyclical? **No minister will ever admit it.** He'd been at a meeting in **Pune that very afternoon (3 p.m.)** where the Finance Minister fielded exactly this question and dodged it — "we have been working on this for a year" — just as an RBI Governor never admits a move was really about the exchange rate.

:::note[Good to Know]
Real policy is always dressed up. A GST cut "to simplify tax" is often a quiet **fiscal stimulus** to offset falling net exports. Read the *equation*, not the press release: if `X − M` is dragging, watch for a nudge to **C, I, or G** to compensate.
:::

## The Government's Budget Constraint

Before the deficit, know the whole statement. A **budget is just the government's income–expenditure plan for the year ahead.** Income comes from three places: **taxes + PSU dividends + borrowing.** (The government is unusual — it can *plan* to borrow; for you and me, borrowing is a last resort.) Spending splits two ways: **revenue expenditure** (creates **no** asset) and **capital expenditure** (creates an asset — infrastructure).

He caught the class with a trick question: the biggest item of revenue expenditure is **not** subsidies and **not** transfers — it's **salaries, wages and pensions.** And three vocabulary terms matter: **BE** (Budget Estimate, set 1 Feb a year ahead), **RE** (Revised Estimate, a year later), and **Actual** (two years later).

| Of every ₹100 the government takes in… | Share |
|---|---|
| **Tax revenue** (net of states' share) | ≈ 56% (≈28% direct + ≈28% indirect like GST/excise) |
| **Non-tax revenue** (PSU dividends + RBI profit transfer) | ≈ 11% |
| **Borrowing** (this *is* the fiscal deficit) | ≈ 31–33% |
| Recovery of loans + disinvestment | small |

So **one rupee in three that the government "earns" is actually borrowed.** And a surprise correction: India does **not** take loans from the IMF — it has *lent* to the IMF. The deficit is financed at home, by buyers of **government securities**: **banks** (forced to hold ≈18% via SLR), **mutual funds**, **LIC**, **pension funds**, and **Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs)**. FIIs constantly compare India's yield with the **US Fed funds rate** — on a **risk-adjusted** basis, since India is rated around **BBB** while the US is **AAA**. (A government bond, he stressed, is **not** a fixed deposit — it has a liquid secondary market; and it's safer than equity because bondholders are paid first in a default.)

:::tip[Important]
Split spending into **revenue** (no asset; dominated by **salaries, wages, pensions**, plus unavoidable **interest payments**) and **capital** (infrastructure). On the income side, remember the trio: **taxes + PSU dividends + borrowing** — and that **borrowing ≈ a third** of the total.
:::

## Fiscal Deficit — the One Number Everyone Watches

Strip the budget down and you find an identity: **fiscal deficit = total expenditure − revenue receipts − (non-debt capital receipts) = borrowing.** That's it. The fiscal deficit simply *is* the government's borrowing for the year — and it's the **single most important number** in the budget.

Why? Because of **crowding out**. The bigger the government's borrowing, the less is left for private firms, and the higher the market interest rate they must pay. Push it too far and the **nation itself gets into trouble**. The guardrail is the **FRBM Act** (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management), which targets a deficit of **3% of GDP** — in practice **4–5%** is tolerated, and **COVID or a war** pushes it higher. India can carry it because of **deep domestic savings** and a large financial sector hungry for G-secs. **Rating agencies** keep the government honest: "had they not been there, the government would have been far more extravagant."

He drew a sharp line between two words students mix up: **deficit** is *this year's* borrowing; **debt** is the *cumulative pile* of all past deficits.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The economy does not depend on the government. It depends on people like you and me — as investors or as consumers. Manmohan Singh used to joke that the government is not *ma-baap*."

Honest aside: during COVID he had *criticised* the government for spending too little — then admitted he was **wrong**, because post-COVID many big spenders got burned by inflation and debt, while India's caution paid off.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Fiscal deficit = government borrowing**, and it is the budget's headline number because it drives **crowding out** and the market interest rate. The rule of thumb: **FRBM target ≈ 3% of GDP** (4–5% tolerated, higher in a crisis). Keep **debt (the pile) vs deficit (the year)** straight — a classic slip.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Deficit is the year, Debt is the pile"]
Every year's **deficit** gets added to the growing **debt**. Deficit is a *flow*; debt is a *stock*.
:::

## Inside the RBI's Toolkit: the Repo Corridor

How does the RBI actually move liquidity? Through **Open Market Operations (OMO)** — buying and selling securities — and, more precisely, the **repo**. *Repo* is short for **re-purchase**. His pencil analogy: "I sell this pencil to Shashank with a contract to buy it back tomorrow; the rate built into that buy-and-sell-back is the **repo rate**."

Why do banks need it? Because all day they take deposits and give loans, and by evening their books won't balance. A bank short of cash either goes to the RBI or to the **overnight money market** — Axis rings up SBI: "lend me ₹50,000 crore for the night." These are huge sums, settled by **electronic entries** at the RBI or **CCIL (Clearing Corporation of India)**, matched today through **NDS-OM** (the Negotiated Dealing System). When banks are instead *flush* with cash, they rush to **park** it with the RBI — that's the **reverse repo** direction.

| Rate (from the RBI corridor shown in class) | Level | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** | 5.75% | Emergency borrowing when interbank liquidity dries up; +25 bps, penal |
| **Policy repo rate** | 5.50% | The "magic number" the RBI sets; banks borrow against securities |
| **Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)** | 5.25% | Park surplus cash with the RBI; −25 bps |
| **Fixed reverse repo** | 3.30% | Older absorption rate, used only in deep surplus |

A **basis point** is one-hundredth of a percent (0.01%), so the corridor is just **±25 bps** around the repo rate. Alongside sit two blunter tools: **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio ≈ 3.75%)** — cash a bank must park with the RBI — and **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio ≈ 18%)** — the slice of deposits parked in safe government securities. Both are **minimums carved out of lending**, kept for the **safety** of the banking system.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Alan Blinder said using the CRR to control money supply is like using a sledgehammer to drive a nail. CRR and SLR are permanent, non-market, almost dictatorial. Repo and reverse repo are the soft, market options — because the Governor doesn't know what tomorrow holds."

:::note[Good to Know]
A bank *could* legally park far above the 18% SLR — even 100% — in government paper. That makes it a **"narrow bank"**: perfectly safe, but useless, because it has stopped giving the housing and vehicle loans that actually generate demand. That only happens in a deep crisis of trust in corporates.
:::

## Flexible Inflation Targeting, and Why the RBI Takes Baby Steps

Since **January 2016** (under Raghuram Rajan), India runs **flexible inflation targeting** — the RBI watches inflation, but *flexibly* and *in context*, not mechanically. Day to day it either **injects** liquidity (repo, term repo, MSF, OMO purchases) or **absorbs** it (reverse repo, SDF, OMO sales); the net is what matters, and the relevant desks **meet twice a day**, more in a crisis.

The repo rate itself moves in tiny **25-basis-point "baby steps"** — never a sudden 2 percentage points. His reason: the Governor **doesn't know the future**, so he nudges, watches the impact, nudges again. He credited the habit to **Alan Greenspan**. The deeper cause is the **transmission lag**: the Governor **Malhotra** announces a cut, *then* a bank chairman lowers the rate, *then* you fill out a car-loan form, *then* you buy the car. Monetary policy works with a **variable lag**; a GST cut, by contrast, hits almost at once.

You can read the whole repo history as crisis-response: a **sharp drop in 2007–10** (the **global financial crisis / Lehman**), another fall through **COVID**, a climb when inflation ran hot, and a gentle easing again now.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Baby steps, because the future is dark"]
The RBI moves **25 bps at a time** — nudge, watch, nudge — because monetary policy acts with a **variable lag** and nobody can see tomorrow. Fiscal policy (a tax cut) is immediate; monetary policy is slow-release.
:::

## Money, Black Income, and the USD 5 Trillion the Fed Printed

Two last ideas tied the evening to the real world.

**What is "money supply"?** Simply **cash + checkable deposits.** The split surprises people: in India it's roughly **13% cash and 87% deposits**, not 50/50. He then demolished a common phrase: **there is no such thing as black *money*, only black *income*** — income on which tax wasn't paid. The notes themselves are white. His bribe example: a corrupt official takes ₹1 lakh for a water connection; if he **buys a TV (or gold, or land)** with it, the TV-maker's income rises and the money keeps circulating — the economy barely notices. It only *leaks* if the cash goes under a **mattress** or into a **Swiss account**. (A sobering number: only about **13 of every 100** Indian voters pay income tax, versus **98 in Sweden**. And the **No. 3 holder of US treasuries** is the tiny **Cayman Islands** — a "treasure island" for round-tripped money.)

**The zero lower bound and brute force.** You can't easily cut rates below **zero** — a negative rate means *you lend money and pay the borrower to take it*. Yet it happened — in **Japan, the Euro area and Scandinavia** — for bulk bank balances, like paying a **safe-deposit-locker** fee to the central bank to hold your surplus cash. When rates hit the floor, central banks switch to **brute force: printing**. The **US Fed balance sheet** went from about **USD 700 billion (2007)** to **USD 2.5 trillion**, then exploded to **USD 9 trillion** at the COVID peak — roughly **USD 5 trillion added in 2020–22** — before shrinking back toward **USD 6.5 trillion** as the **Fed funds rate** was lifted from near 0 to about **5%** to absorb liquidity.

Why could the US do it and India couldn't? Because the **dollar is freely convertible** and trusted, and the world happily holds dollar securities — **China, Japan, the Cayman Islands, India, Singapore, Switzerland, Germany**. In fact, **your forex reserves *are* essentially US government securities.**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "When you are in a crisis, you love a maverick. Behind Trump's rise is America's own displeasure at how it handled COVID."

:::note[Good to Know]
Watch **two things at once** on any central bank: a **price** (the policy rate — repo here, Fed funds there) and a **quantity** (the balance-sheet size). Printing swells the quantity; raising the rate drains it. The USD 700bn → 9tn → 6.5tn arc is that seesaw in one picture.
:::

## Real-World Takeaway

Strip away the diagrams and Session 3 is about **who actually feels a policy, and how fast.** A tax cut lands in your pocket today; a repo cut has to travel through your banker, who may or may not pass it on, with a lag nobody can time. The government is the big, impeccable borrower who can crowd you out — so its **deficit** is the number the whole market reads. And when rates hit the floor, the rich-world trick of **printing trillions** only works if the world trusts your currency — which is exactly why the Fed could add USD 5 trillion and the RBI could not.

The manager who remembers just two sentences has the session: **fiscal is the pocket, monetary is the pipe** — and **the interest rate tells you which lever was pulled.**
