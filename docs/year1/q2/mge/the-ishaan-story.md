# The Ishaan Story (Sessions 1–8)

*One continuous story to lock the whole course into memory. Follow Ishaan — a working professional — as each lecture quietly solves a problem sitting on his own desk.*

---

Ishaan was thirty-one, a finance manager at a mid-size auto-components company in Pune. Half the firm's parts went into cars sold in India; the other half were shipped to a buyer in the United States. On the Monday his evening MBA began, a single headline sat on three different screens in the office: **"US announces new tariffs."**

By lunch the US buyer had "paused" an order. Ishaan's boss wanted a one-line answer: *is this good or bad for us, and what should anyone do about it?*

He didn't have one. That evening, in his first class with Professor Partha Ray, he started to understand why the honest answer is almost always the same two words — **it depends.**

## Chapter 1: The map before the toolkit (Session 1)

Ishaan walked in expecting equations. Instead he got a tour of the whole world.

His first confusion was personal. He had grown up on the old saying — *if America catches a cold, India sneezes* — so a US tariff scaring his order book made sense. What didn't make sense was the phone call from his cousin **Meera**, a software engineer in Texas, who was **also** nervous about *her* job. If America was doing the sneezing, why was she worried?

The professor's answer reframed everything: the linkage now runs **both ways**. India is no longer only a taker of shocks; it is also a source of them.

Then came the quieter puzzles, and each one dissolved a thing Ishaan had always half-believed:

- **Why does my firm even exist?** Not charity, not patriotism — **comparative advantage.** The American buyer sources from Pune because it is simply *cheaper* to make the part here. "Bringing jobs home" by force just makes the home product expensive.
- **What is this ₹500 note, really?** At the chai stall after class, Ishaan looked at the Governor's signed promise in his hand and finally heard the line: **money is a fiction — its value is your trust in the state, and you measure that trust by inflation.** That is why a central bank is kept **independent** from a government that would love to print its way to re-election.
- **Why did the US client pay in dollars**, when neither of them was American? **Network externality** — Ishaan accepts dollars because he is sure the next person will too. That self-reinforcing loop, not India's GDP or anyone's permission, is what keeps the dollar on top.
- **Why does Meera, who earns five times my salary, say she "can't afford a driver"?** Because a driver is expensive in Texas and cheap in Pune. That gap is the difference between **nominal GDP** (India ≈ USD 3.9 trillion) and **PPP GDP** (India ≈ USD 16.2 trillion) — the same rupees simply buy more here.

By the end Ishaan saw that the world was pulling together **and** apart at the same time — globalization and deglobalization at once — and that macro is "gray," not black-and-white. He stopped looking for the one right answer and started asking what the *context* was.

> The confusion: *surely a tariff is simply bad for us.* The clarity: it depends who you are in the loop — and the first job is to read the whole global board, not half of it.

## Chapter 2: The machine behind the world (Session 2)

"Fine," Ishaan thought in week two, "enough stories — give me a model I can actually turn the knobs on." The professor obliged, and promptly offended him.

The model was a **one-commodity "day"** with **no price at all**, where **Y = C + I**. Ishaan bristled: *where is GDP? Where is the price? This isn't real.* Then the professor's rule landed — **question a model within the model, not against reality** — and Ishaan did something that made it click: he thought about his own **godown**.

When sales slowed last quarter, unsold parts had piled up in the warehouse, so the plant cut the next shift. When sales jumped, they ran stock down and added a shift. **No price had moved — quantity did, and inventory was the shock-absorber.** Suddenly the "unrealistic" cross was just his own factory.

The rest followed naturally:

- **Why can't you spend more than you earn forever?** His own budget answered it: earn ₹1 lakh, spend ₹1.2 lakh, and the extra ₹20,000 has to come from a loan or a "rich uncle" — not sustainable. So **MPC is less than 1**, and that single fact keeps the whole system stable.
- **How does a government order ripple out?** The professor showed the **multiplier**: with people spending half of each new rupee (MPC 0.5), a **₹1,000 crore** order becomes **₹2,000 crore** of demand — Ishaan's supplier spends half on a shirt, the shirt-seller spends half again, the echoes fading because each round is a *fraction* of the last: **1 / (1 − MPC) = 2**.
- **Where do the real knobs sit?** On two crossing curves — **IS** (the goods market, sloping down, moved by government spending **G**) and **LM** (the money market, sloping up, moved by money **M**). **Fiscal policy shifts IS; monetary policy shifts LM.**

Ishaan left realising a model isn't a photograph — it's a disciplined story you judge on whether it hangs together. His factory had been running IS–LM logic all along; he just hadn't had the names.

> The confusion: *a model with no price is useless.* The clarity: strip reality down to one consistent story, see exactly what adjusts — and his own godown had been proving it for years.

## Chapter 3: The pocket and the pipe (Session 3)

Now Ishaan had two live money questions of his own — one from the newspaper, one from his bank.

The newspaper one was easy to feel: a **GST cut** had just put money straight into customers' hands, and his showroom friends saw sales lift almost overnight. The bank one baffled him. The RBI had **cut the repo rate** weeks ago, yet his home-loan EMI hadn't moved a rupee. If the RBI had "cut rates," where was *his* cut?

The professor's distinction solved both at once:

- **Fiscal policy is the pocket.** The government raises its own spending or cuts your tax, the money lands **directly** in your hand, and the **multiplier** does the rest. That is why the GST cut worked instantly.
- **Monetary policy is the pipe.** Ishaan has no account with the RBI — his **banker** controls his financial life. The RBI only makes the banker's life easier and *hopes* he passes it on. Whether Ishaan ever feels it is a question of **transmission** — and sometimes the cut gets "lost in translation."

Two more things he had vaguely resented now made sense. His home loan felt expensive partly because the **government is the "obese borrower"** in the room: its credit is impeccable, so when it borrows heavily it is served first and **crowds out** private borrowers like him, at a higher rate. And that borrowing *is* the **fiscal deficit** — the budget's one headline number, kept on a leash by the **FRBM** rule of roughly **3% of GDP**. He also learned why the relief was slow: the RBI moves in **25-basis-point "baby steps"** because of a long **transmission lag** — the Governor cuts, then a bank chairman lowers a rate, then Ishaan fills out a form, then he buys the car.

> The confusion: *the RBI cut rates, so why is my EMI the same?* The clarity: fiscal is the pocket, monetary is the pipe — and the interest rate tells you which lever was pulled.

## Chapter 4: The sugar rush and the snap-back (Session 4)

The GST cut kept working, and Ishaan's plant was suddenly busy. He started drafting a proposal to hire a permanent second shift — surely this boom was the new normal.

At home, a different confusion was waiting. His mother was furious at the television: *"Prices go up every single week, and the news says inflation is coming down — they're lying to us!"* Ishaan half-agreed; it sounded like a contradiction.

That week's class quietly untangled both the hiring plan and the kitchen argument.

First, his mother. **A price is a level; inflation is the rate of change.** If the price index runs **100 → 110 → 120**, the first jump is **10%** and the second is only about **9.1%.** So prices kept rising *while inflation fell* — his mother and the newsreader were **both right**. "One is the level, the other is the first derivative," the professor said, "like displacement and velocity."

Then the hiring plan. The professor drew **aggregate demand** out of IS–LM and set it against **aggregate supply at two speeds**: a **short-run** curve that slopes up, and a **long-run** curve standing **vertical at potential output Ȳ.** An expansionary push — the GST cut — shifts demand out, so output rises **and** the price rises (the **sugar rush**). But people aren't fooled for long; expectations catch up, supply shifts, and output **snaps back to Ȳ** at a *higher* price. The high lasts a quarter; the extra zeros on the price tag stay. **There is no free lunch.**

Ishaan quietly shelved the permanent-shift proposal. The lasting kind of growth — the kind that would really raise his plant's ceiling — doesn't come from a demand sugar rush at all; it comes from **structural** things like skills, roads and institutions that push **Ȳ** itself outward. He'd almost mistaken a sugar rush for a bigger engine.

> The confusion: *the boom is permanent, and the inflation news contradicts the kitchen.* The clarity: output bought by demand snaps back to potential, prices stay higher — and a rising price level can still mean falling inflation.

## Chapter 5: Same policy, opposite endings (Session 8)

Months later the rupee was slipping, and Ishaan's WhatsApp group was loud with confident fixes. One friend: *"The RBI should just peg the rupee at a nice round number and stop the drama."* Another, cheering the US: *"These tariffs will finally shrink America's trade deficit."*

The old Ishaan might have nodded along. The trained one saw that **both** were wrong — and the Mundell–Fleming model, the open-economy version of his familiar IS–LM, told him exactly why.

- **The RBI can't simply "fix" the rupee.** The exchange rate is a **market price**, set where the supply of foreign exchange (savings not used at home) meets its demand (net exports). The RBI is a **big player, not the market** — it smooths the rupee within a **"Lakshman rekha,"** but with reserves of about **USD 700 billion** it cannot fight the tide, especially against a China sitting on roughly **USD 3 trillion.** Holding a peg would mean surrendering its own monetary policy.
- **The tariff won't shrink the deficit.** Under a **floating** rate, a tariff cuts imports but the currency then **appreciates**, so exports fall too — you end with **less trade and the same deficit.** The lever that *does* raise output under a float is **monetary** policy, not trade barriers. "Same policy, opposite endings," the professor called it: *first ask whether the economy is fixed or floating.*

Then the detail that stayed with Ishaan most, because it felt so unfair. Capital chases the **risk-adjusted return**, and it is beyond any one country's control: the professor's example showed money leaving India **even though India did nothing wrong** — purely because a rating agency had re-scored *the US* as safer. Politicians take credit when the rupee strengthens, he noted drily, but never the blame when it weakens.

And the rule he said to carry for life — the **Impossible Trinity.** A country wants three good things: **free capital flow, its own monetary policy, and a fixed exchange rate.** You can keep **at most two.** The US drops the peg and lets the dollar float; Saudi Arabia keeps a peg and simply imports the Fed's policy; China keeps its peg and its own policy by **controlling capital.** Three wishes, two granted.

> The confusion: *just fix the rupee, and the tariff will fix the deficit.* The clarity: the exchange rate is a market price, and in an open economy every policy's result flips with the regime — so the first question is always "fixed or floating?"

## What Ishaan kept

By the last session Ishaan could finally answer his boss's Monday question — not with a slogan, but with a method. *Is the tariff good or bad for us?* **It depends** — on whether the world is floating or pegged, on whether the RBI and the government move together, on whether a rush is real growth or a snap-back waiting to happen.

He knew he'd forget the exact equations within a decade. What he meant to keep was smaller and sturdier: **money is trust; fiscal is the pocket and monetary is the pipe; there is no free lunch; and no economy gets all three wishes at once.** The professor's parting habit became his: **fifteen minutes of a business daily every day**, the Budget once a year — enough to "make a method of the madness," and to see the next shock a little before it lands.
