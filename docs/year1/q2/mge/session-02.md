# Session 2: In the Long Run We're All Dead — Rebuilding the Keynesian Machine

*How a one-commodity "day," a clever multiplier, and two crossing curves (IS–LM) turn messy reality into a model you can actually think with.*

## The Teacher's Real Agenda: Discipline, Not Description

Before any diagram, grab the one idea that frames the whole night. A model is **not** reality. It is a tightly consistent *story*. You are allowed to question whether the story hangs together internally — you are **not** allowed to dismiss it for being "unrealistic."

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "When you question a model, you question the model in terms of the model. Do not question it in terms of the reality."

He uses a Bollywood-movie test: the plot can be far-fetched, but it must stay **consistent** — "the brother cannot suddenly become the brother-in-law later." Same with a model: internal logic first, realism later.

## When Does an Equilibrium Actually "Work"? Three Properties

Start with the most basic picture: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal, a downward demand curve and an upward supply curve crossing at equilibrium **E** (quantity Q̄, price P). The professor's real point: *any* equilibrium must pass **three tests**.

| Property | The question it answers | It fails when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Existence** | Do demand and supply cross at a *positive* price? | The curves never intersect |
| **Stability** | After a nudge off E, does the system return? | Off-equilibrium forces push it *further* away |
| **Uniqueness** | Is there only *one* equilibrium? | The curves cross at several points |

**Existence** needs a positive price. Can price ever go negative? Normally no — but the professor flags two real recent exceptions: **oil (Brent crude) went negative during COVID** (you were literally *paid* to take it) and **interest rates turned negative in the euro area**. A "bad" like pollution is the other hint that prices can misbehave.

**Stability** is like a friendship after a fight: a *stable* relationship comes back together; an unstable one never speaks again. Above equilibrium there is **excess supply**, so price falls back; below it there is **excess demand**, so price rises. Nobody referees this — the **price system** does it alone.

> **Professor Partha Ray (quoting Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, 1776):** "It is not the benevolence of the baker that leads us to bread; it is his self-interest."

That self-correcting price mechanism — "no referee with a big rod" — is the core idea of a capitalist system. **Uniqueness** (one equilibrium vs several) he sets aside: "We are simple people. We worry about existence and stability, not uniqueness."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Exists, Stays, Only-one"]
The three equilibrium tests in order: **E**xists (positive-price crossing), **S**tays (returns after a nudge), **O**nly-one (unique). You are examined mostly on the first two.
:::

## The Keynesian Cross: A One-Commodity "Day" With No Prices

Now the famous **45° diagram**. The 45° line is simply **Y = Y** (supply equals output); aggregate demand is **C + I**; they cross at equilibrium E. The shocker students always resist: **there is no price in this model.** How can that be?

Because this is a **one-commodity world** looked at over **a single day**. In a day, price can't move — only **quantity** does. And you can only add consumption and investment if they are the *same stuff*. Rice you eat is **consumption**; the same rice used as seed is **investment**. One commodity, two purposes → you can legitimately write:

**Y = C + I**

If there were many goods you'd need money to add them (Y = price·C + price·I). A student pushed back with GDP; the professor drew the line sharply: **GDP is measurement/statistics (reality); this is a model (one aggregate commodity).** Don't mix the two.

So if there's no price, **what adjusts?** Inventory.

- At output **above** equilibrium → supply beats demand → unsold goods pile up in the godown → **inventory accumulation** → cut production tomorrow.
- At output **below** equilibrium → demand beats supply → pull stock out of the godown → **inventory draw-down** → raise production tomorrow.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
In the very short run (a day), **the Keynesian cross clears by quantity, not price**, and the shock-absorber is **inventory** (accumulation or draw-down). Assumptions that make this work: one non-perishable commodity, a free godown, and **inventory belongs to the producer** (not the consumer). Remove the godown and the model breaks.
:::

## Meet Keynes — the Man Behind the Machine

A little colour the professor clearly loves, and it's genuinely useful for recall. **John Maynard Keynes** didn't just rewrite economics — he was a serious investor who made money on the London Stock Exchange, and he helped birth **three** institutions: the **IMF**, the **World Bank**, and — the surprise — the **Reserve Bank of India**. As a young civil servant in the London India currency office he argued that India, though a British dominion, should run its **own central bank**; the **RBI was born in 1934**.

Keynes was **no Marxist** — a believer in capitalism — but he showed that *when capitalism hits a crisis, government can step in*. That single move legitimised the government's role in a market economy. His *General Theory* (1936) was so mathematically-minded yet written in dense English that people spent decades reinterpreting it.

> **Professor Partha Ray (quoting Keynes):** "It is easy to learn the newer ideas; it is very difficult to escape the older ones."

> **Professor Partha Ray (quoting Keynes):** "In the long run we are all dead."

:::note[Good to Know]
That second line is the professor's favourite weapon against "but what about the long run?" If learning macro only pays off as a CEO in 25 years, Keynes would shrug — you must survive *tomorrow* first. The professor's own version: telling a starving beggar to "just get educated" is a cruel joke; survival comes before the long run.
:::

## Why MPC Must Be Less Than 1

The **consumption function** links consumption to income: C = a + (MPC)·Y, where **MPC** = Marginal Propensity to Consume = change in consumption ÷ change in income. Which of these is a *valid* consumption function?

| Candidate | MPC | Valid? |
|---|---|---|
| C = 800 + 0.5Y | 0.5 | ✅ Yes — MPC between 0 and 1 |
| C = 800 + 1.5Y | 1.5 | ❌ No — MPC above 1 is self-destructive |

Why can't MPC exceed 1? Because then you'd consume **more than you earn** every period and survive only by **borrowing forever**. Think of entering college, or earning ₹1 lakh but spending ₹1.2 lakh — where does the extra ₹20,000 come from? A loan, or a "perennially rich uncle." Not sustainable. So **MPC less than 1 is a stability condition**.

## The Multiplier: How ₹1,000 Crore Becomes ₹2,000 Crore

Add government: **Y = C(Y) + I + G**. Ask the key question — if **G** rises, how much does **Y** rise? Differentiate:

**dY / dG = 1 / (1 − MPC)**

With MPC = 0.5, that's 1 / (1 − 0.5) = **2**. So ₹1,000 crore of extra government spending generates **₹2,000 crore** of extra demand. *That is Keynes's headline contribution.*

The engine underneath is an **infinite geometric progression**. Government hands you ₹1,000; you spend ₹500 (MPC 0.5) on a shirt; the seller spends ₹250; and so on:

**1000 × 0.5 + 1000 × 0.5² + 1000 × 0.5³ + … = 1000 × [1 / (1 − 0.5)] = 2000**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**MPC less than 1 does double duty.** It makes the equilibrium **stable** *and* it makes the multiplier's infinite GP **converge** (sum = 1 / (1 − MPC)). If MPC were 2, the series 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + … explodes — no finite multiplier. The multiplier is greater than 1 precisely because MPC is a *fraction*.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "A fraction makes the echoes fade"]
Each round of spending is a fraction of the last (MPC). Fractions shrink, so the "echoes" die out and the total settles at **1 / (1 − MPC)**. A fraction greater than 1 would make the echoes grow forever.
:::

## Government to the Rescue — But Only in a Crisis

Keynesian spending isn't an everyday tool; it is **crisis medicine** (COVID, the Global Financial Crisis, the Great Depression). And note *who* spends: not leftist regimes but ordinary **capitalist governments** — the US, UK, Germany, India. So where does the money come from? Only three doors:

| Route | How it works | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Tax** | Take from citizens, spend it back | Circular; and taxing the rich is politically hard |
| **Borrow** | Float bonds (bought by RBI, banks, or FIIs) | In a crisis nobody has spare cash; the **deficit must stay sustainable** |
| **Print money** | The central bank expands the money supply | Fine *only* in a crisis; habit-forming |

In a genuine crisis, taxing is circular and lenders are broke — so Keynes's answer is blunt:

> **Professor Partha Ray (relaying Keynes):** "In a crisis — I don't care — print money and spend."

Won't that cause inflation? Usually **no**, because in a crisis prices are already falling and inflation is low (even negative) — so there's room to print. His evidence: the **US Federal Reserve's balance sheet grew from about USD 800 billion to about USD 4 trillion between 2008 and 2012** (roughly **USD 3.2 trillion** of new money), yet US inflation stayed low. The moment inflation flares, the Fed simply stops.

:::tip[Important]
**Moderate inflation is healthy; zero is not.** You don't just buy — you *sell* your labour too. Zero inflation means your salary never rises: a frozen, "nobody-is-born" economy (the professor calls it a kind of nirvana). 10% is bad, but roughly **2–6% is fine** — the RBI's inflation-target zone.
:::

Two more honest caveats he drives home:

- **No free lunch** (Milton Friedman). Perpetual borrowing is a **Ponzi game** (he recalls Kerala/Bengal "blade companies"); eventually someone repays — often the **next generation**, who get taxed later. Borrowing creates an asymmetry between today's citizens and tomorrow's.
- **Redistribution beats blanket spending in theory, but is politically brutal.** A billionaire (think Mr. Ambani) has saturated consumption → **low MPC**; a poor household has **high MPC**. Taxing the rich and transferring to the poor creates more demand — but taxing the rich is hard, and subsidies suffer the **"toothpaste syndrome"**: once squeezed out, they can't be pushed back without the government being voted out. Crisis-time direct transfers (NREGA, pensions) are the workable version.

:::note[Good to Know]
Why do some countries tax so heavily? **Sweden's** high taxes fund near-free health and education → higher quality of life. The **US** taxes somewhat less and leaves health to insurers; public school is free only **up to grade 12**, after which it turns very expensive. Same "no free lunch," different split.
:::

## From a Day to a Week: Interest Rates, PDV, and the IS Curve

Now relax "investment is fixed." Investment chases **returns** — but returns land in *different periods*, and you **cannot simply add** a return today (R₁) to a return next period (R₂), any more than you can add your macro marks to your strategy marks. Different times must be **discounted**.

That gives **Present Discounted Value (PDV)**:

**PDV = R₁ / (1 + r) + R₂ / (1 + r)² + … + Rₙ / (1 + r)ⁿ**, where **r** is the interest rate.

Higher PDV → more investment. And since **r** sits in the denominator, a **lower interest rate lifts PDV and lifts investment**. So the key relationship is: **investment I depends negatively on r** (r down → I up; r up → I down).

Rewrite commodity-market equilibrium with this. From Y = C(Y) + I + G, move C over: **Y − C(Y) = I(r) + G**. The left side is **savings**, so:

**S(Y) = I(r) + G**

This is one equation in **two** variables (Y and r), so it's not a point — it's a **curve**. Trace the slope: Y up → S up; G fixed → I must rise → so r must **fall**. Y and r move in **opposite directions** → a **negatively sloped** curve. This is the **IS curve** — every (Y, r) combination where the **commodity market clears**. The lone parameter is **G**.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The **IS curve is negatively sloped** and represents commodity-market equilibrium (**I(r) + G = S(Y)**). A change in **G is fiscal policy**: an **increase in G shifts IS outward** (expansionary); a **decrease shifts it inward** (contractionary). Learn to draw and shift it.
:::

:::note[Good to Know]
The IS–LM framework isn't actually Keynes's drawing. Because Keynes "thought in mathematics but wrote in dense English," a young LSE lecturer, **Sir John Hicks**, distilled him in a 10-page paper, *"Mr. Keynes and the Classics: A Suggested Interpretation."* Every textbook teaches *Hicks's* version. The professor's warning: "If you think you're smart, try reading the paper — it will make you humble." Don't read Hicks raw; you'll only get confused.
:::

## Walras's Law: Why Two Markets Are Enough

Keynes works with at least **three markets**: **commodity, money, and bond** (no equity — in his world money and bonds are mirror images, the only two financial assets). Each clears when demand equals supply.

Now lock three people — commodity, money, bond — in a sealed room: nobody enters, nobody leaves (a closed economy). If **two** of them are in equilibrium, the **third must be too**. That is **Walras's Law**: in *n* markets, if *n − 1* clear, the last one clears automatically.

So we can **ignore the bond market** and work with just **commodity (IS)** and **money (LM)**.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Lock the room: two agree, the third must"]
Walras's Law in one line. It's the licence that lets us drop the bond market and study only IS and LM without losing anything.
:::

## The Money Market and the LM Curve

Work in **real money, M / P** (M = money supply, P = price level), held fixed. Keynes splits money **demand** into two motives:

| Demand type | Driven by | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Transaction** | Income (Y) | More income → more money needed to transact (**positive**) |
| **Speculative / asset** | Interest rate (r) | Money earns zero, so a higher rate means you hold *less* cash (**negative**) |

The speculative motive is about **liquidity**: cash pays no return, so when rates are high you park money in the bank; when rates are low you'd rather keep hard cash. (Note: **money is not income** — it's merely the *form* in which income arrives, e.g., salary vs stock options.)

Set money demand equal to the fixed real supply: **MT(Y) + MS(r) = M / P̄**. This is the **LM curve**. Its slope: Y up → transaction demand up; the total is fixed, so speculative demand must fall → **r up**. Y and r move **together** → a **positively sloped** curve.

Put them together and you get the **IS–LM model** — the simultaneous equilibrium of the commodity and money markets:

| Curve | Market | Equilibrium condition | Slope | Shifted by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IS** | Commodity | I(r) + G = S(Y) | **Negative** | **Fiscal policy** (change in G) |
| **LM** | Money | MT(Y) + MS(r) = M / P | **Positive** | **Monetary policy** (change in M) |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**IS slopes down, LM slopes up; where they cross fixes Y and r together.** **Fiscal policy moves IS via G; monetary policy moves LM via M** — doubling the money supply shifts LM. This single diagram is the spine of everything next session (monetary vs fiscal policy, the RBI, the Budget).
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Fiscal shifts IS, Money shifts LM"]
Two curves, two levers. **G** is the government's hand on **IS**; **M** is the central bank's hand on **LM**. Pull a lever, shift its curve, read off the new Y and r.
:::

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Existence / Stability / Uniqueness** | The three tests any equilibrium must face |
| **Keynesian cross (45° model)** | One-commodity "day" where Y = C + I and **quantity**, not price, clears |

| **PDV** | Present Discounted Value; future returns divided by (1 + r)ᵗ |

| **LM curve** | Money-market equilibrium; **positively sloped**; shifted by **M** |
| **Walras's Law** | If n − 1 markets clear, the nth clears too |
| **Transaction vs speculative demand** | Money wanted for buying (↑ with Y) vs for liquidity (↓ with r) |
| **Fiscal vs monetary policy** | Moving **G** (shifts IS) vs moving **M** (shifts LM) |

## Real-World Takeaway

The professor's deeper lesson isn't the diagrams — it's the **habit**. Strip reality down to a tiny, consistent story (one commodity, one day), see exactly what adjusts (quantity, then inventory, then interest rates), and only *then* let the real world back in. Do that and "government spending — good or bad?" stops being a slogan and becomes the right answer every time: **it depends on the context** — crisis or calm, rich saver or poor spender, a day or twenty-five years.
