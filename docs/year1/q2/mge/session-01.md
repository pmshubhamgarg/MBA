# Session 1: Where the Questions Stay the Same but the Answers Keep Changing

*The professor's opening tour of the global economy — before any model. Why macro is a "gray" method, why money is really just trust, how the dollar quietly rules, and why the world's economic centre of gravity is sliding back to Asia.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** Macroeconomics as a **method, not a formula** ("the answers change, the questions stay"); the **two-way global–local linkage**; **comparative advantage**; **money as trust** (and why you need an independent central bank); the **six channels of globalization**; **Nominal GDP vs PPP GDP** (and the India arithmetic); what makes an **international currency** + **network externality**; **IMF vs World Bank**.

**Important (supporting):** The **Maddison 1,000-year GDP** shift (Asia falls for 973 years, then doubles in 27); **globalization vs deglobalization**; the **five global challenges**; **advanced (39) vs emerging (~150)** economies and the 64% → 40% flip; **CBDC vs stablecoins**.

**Context (colour for answers):** JFK cab, the typist and the gardener, Deng's "black cat, white cat", Brexit and chicken tikka masala, DeepSeek vs Nvidia, Hyundai vs Trump, Japanese nurses, Pakistan's repeat IMF loans, India–Iran rupee-for-oil, "the dollar is our currency but your problem."
:::

## Setting the Stage: A Tour Before the Toolkit

Your professor is **Partha Ray**, an economist by training. He spent nearly **18 years at the Reserve Bank of India** in its economic research department in Mumbai, then almost **five years at the IMF** during the global financial crisis, then **about ten years teaching at IIM Calcutta**, and is now based in **Pune**. So when he mentions Pakistan's IMF file or a crisis-era policy call, it is usually something he actually worked on.

This is the first lecture of **Managing in the Global Economy**, and he deliberately keeps it descriptive. No equations today — just a wide-angle picture of how the world economy hangs together. The real model-building starts next session.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Life is not a laundry list. When you take a decision in your managerial capacity, you need a method in your thinking. This course is largely a method course."

He opened by asking what the (working-executive) class wanted to learn, and the wish-list was long: exchange rates, policy, the global–local link, the dollar, trade wars, AI. His answer: we'll build the *method* to think about all of it.

| Session | What it does |

## The Linkage Now Runs Both Ways

The old mental model was one-directional: the rich West sneezes, the developing world catches the flu. The professor's first big correction is that the arrow now points **both** ways. India is no longer only a taker of global shocks; it is also a source of them.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "When we were young we were told — if America catches cold, we sneeze. That still happens. But the reverse is also true now: if tomorrow we catch cold, the Americans are going to sneeze as well."

**Why it matters:** every chapter of this course sits on top of this idea. A manager who only watches "what the West does to us" is reading half the page. The interesting questions are in the feedback loop between the two.

## Macroeconomics Is Gray, Not Black-and-White

This is the philosophical spine of the whole course, and a near-certain theme in any answer you write. In macro there is rarely one clean answer. **Ideology, context and the time period** all bend the result.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "A macroeconomist returns to his old school and is horrified to find the same question paper. He protests — and is told: yes, the questions remain the same, but the answers keep changing."

Take the oldest debate — **government versus market**. He put the two extremes on the table: **Adam Smith** ("it is not the benevolence of the baker that gives us our bread, but his self-interest") against **Karl Marx** (the market is essentially evil; let the state do everything). Reality sits somewhere in between, and the job of policy is to find the **magic proportion**.

That proportion is not fixed — it moves with the situation. In a pandemic the mix might be roughly **government 0.75 / market 0.25**; in a healthy expansion it flips to **0.25 / 0.75**. "Policy" is just the tinkering we do with that dial.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Same questions, new answers"]
If an exam asks "what is the right policy?", your first instinct should be *it depends on the context* — then show the two corner positions (all-market vs all-government) and argue where the dial should sit *this time*.
:::

## Money Is a Fiction

One of the most quotable stretches of the evening. Pull a ₹500 note out of your pocket: the RBI Governor has literally signed a promise to pay the bearer ₹500. Catch him in a mall, hand him the note, and all he can give you is… another ₹500 note. The value is not in the paper.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Please understand — money is a fiction. A note is a piece of paper. Its value lies in your trust in the state, and you measure that trust by the rate of inflation."

If inflation is 10%, last year's ₹100 is worth about ₹90 today — trust has eroded by roughly 10% (as long as your income hasn't grown to cover it). That is **why central bankers are obsessed with inflation**: it is the thermometer of trust.

He also flipped the note into a balance sheet. A ₹100 note is a **liability of the RBI**; on the asset side, only around ₹10–15 is gold, and the rest is **securities** — your own government's, and respectable foreign governments' too. That foreign-securities detail is exactly why a strong economy's currency (the dollar) ends up backing everyone else's money.

**Why a central bank, not the Ministry of Finance?** Because printing money is too tempting for a government.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "What is the objective function of any government? To get re-elected. And what do you need for that? The capacity to print money. That is precisely why you need checks and balances — an independent central bank."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Money = trust in the state, measured by inflation.** The central bank is kept **independent from the government** to remove the conflict of interest (a re-election-seeking government would over-print). If a question touches "why RBI, not the Finance Ministry?", the one-line answer is *separation of monetary and fiscal power to protect the value of money.*
:::

## The World Runs on Comparative Advantage

Why does America run a trade deficit? Why is your work outsourced to India? The professor's answer to almost all of it is one idea: **comparative advantage** — everyone does what they are *relatively* best at.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The MD may know both typing and taking decisions, but he still keeps a typist — because in relative terms the typist types faster. The world moves on comparative advantage. That's exactly what we pursue, though Mr. Trump doesn't understand it."

The US runs a current-account deficit because it is simply **expensive to make things there** — his joke: get off at JFK, take a cab driven by an American in an American car, and that short ride costs a fortune. Apple makes iPhones abroad for the same reason, and India's outsourcing boom was never philanthropy — it was **cheaper**, full stop.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Trade patterns (deficits, outsourcing, offshoring) are driven by **comparative advantage**, not charity or hostility. A country does what it is relatively most efficient at and imports the rest. "Bringing jobs home" by force usually just makes the home product expensive.
:::

## Six Channels of Globalization

He defined globalization not as a slogan but as **six concrete flows** between economies. This is a clean list to memorise, with one example each.

| Channel | The idea | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| **Goods** | Buy where it's cheaper, after adjusting for **tariff + transport cost** | A pen cheaper in Pune than New York should sell to New York |
| **Services** | Go virtual over the web — but only *some* can | Medical dictation transcribed overnight in Bangalore; but no "virtual haircut" |
| **Financial flows** | **Scale-neutral** — a billion moves with one mouse click | A million pens need many boxes; USD 1 billion needs one click — so finance fraud can be huge |
| **Technology transfer** | Advanced countries hold the edge; **patents** gate it | DeepSeek rivalling Nvidia by not respecting patent rights |
| **Labour movement** | *Should* equalise wages, but **visas/work permits** block it | Brexit; the chicken-tikka-masala joke |
| **Regulation convergence** | Rules spread across borders | **GDPR, Basel norms, Paris/COP** carbon rules |

Two of these deserve a second look. **Services** only globalise if they can be detached from a physical body — a surgeon's notes can fly to Pune as a WAV file, but a haircut cannot, so that price gap survives unless *people* migrate. **Finance** is dangerous precisely because it is weightless: moving goods needs trucks, but moving money needs a click, which is why frauds scale so fast.

On **labour**, he used Brexit as the cautionary tale: a wafer-thin **52–48** vote, driven partly by an older generation uncomfortable with foreign workers. His verdict on whether they were right?

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The later mass migration only shows they were wrong. It was a wafer-thin majority, and they are now paying the price for it."

## Globalization vs Deglobalization

The world is **not** moving in one direction. Against every force pulling economies together, there is a nationalist force pulling them apart: the **Brexit referendum**, **Trump's tariffs**, the retreat from bilateral trade treaties, new nationalists in Europe, the **2020 COVID pandemic** (when we first learned a global supply chain can fail), and long-running conflicts like Israel–Gaza.

The results of this pushback are visible: **slower trade, higher trade barriers, falling FDI, and localised supply chains**. His sharpest illustration: when the US found illegal workers at **Hyundai's** plants and moved to deport them, Hyundai threatened to **stop manufacturing in the US** — and Trump quietly backed down. Even the toughest nationalist runs into the supply chain he depends on.

:::note[Good to Know]
He flagged this as a permanent tension to carry through the course: the **economic model is global, but the political model is local**. That gap never fully closes — and "Mr. Trump is the live example of it."
:::

## A Thousand Years in One Table (Maddison)

For a sense of history, he used **Angus Maddison's** reconstruction of world GDP over 1,000 years (Maddison was chief statistician at the OECD; the professor met him in 1993). Treat world GDP as 100 and watch the shares move. These are crude estimates — in Keynes's words, better to be **"roughly right than precisely wrong."**

| Region | The long-run arc | What drove it |
|---|---|---|
| **Western Europe** | 17.8% (1500) → **33% (1913)** | **Industrial Revolution** (supply) + **colonies** (demand) |
| **Western offshoots** (US, Canada, Australia, NZ) | 1.9% (1820) → **30.7% (1950)** | Abundant **land**, rapid urbanisation |
| **Asia (excl. Japan)** | **67.6% (AD 1)** → 16.4% (1973) → **30.9% (2000)** | Colonialism pulled it down; reform pulled it back |
| **Africa** | 11.5% → **3.5%** | "A thousand years of negligence" |

The Western Europe story is a neat supply-and-demand pairing: **James Watt's steam engine** solved supply, and **colonies** solved demand — a weaver in Maharashtra forced to buy cloth from Manchester. The Western offshoots had what Europe lacked: **land**.

But the headline is **Asia**. Its share *fell for 973 years* — then almost **doubled from 16.4% to 30.9% in just 27 years**, which he called a tectonic change with no parallel in human history. It came in three waves, and nobody planned it centrally:

- **1970s** — the East Asian "tigers" (Korea, Hong Kong, Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand).
- **1980s** — China, after **Deng Xiaoping** took power. His profound line for a communist leader: *"Black cat, white cat — what does the colour matter, as long as it catches the mice?"*
- **1990s** — India's 1991 liberalisation.

Africa is the counter-example: rich in minerals, yet stuck low because it lacked the three ingredients of long-run growth — **infrastructure, human capital, and institutions**. India, by contrast, had reformers in every region building human capital, and (crucially) **policy continuity across changing governments** — no new government came in and re-nationalised what the last one had freed.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Asia fell for 973, doubled in 27"]
The whole Maddison story in one line: Asia's share slid for nearly a thousand years under colonialism, then doubled in under three decades on the back of the **70s tigers, 80s China, 90s India**.
:::

## IMF vs World Bank, and Why Countries Don't Go Bankrupt

A favourite exam trap. Both are Bretton Woods institutions, but they do very different jobs — and **the IMF is not a bank.**

| | **World Bank** | **IMF** |
|---|---|---|
| **For whom** | Poor countries, poverty eradication | Countries in a balance-of-payments / currency crisis |
| **Horizon** | Long-term development | Short-term crisis support |
| **What it does** | Funds projects (a water tank in Burkina Faso, tax laws in Nepal) | Takes your currency, gives **hard currency (USD)** |
| **The catch** | — | **Conditionality** (cut the fiscal deficit, raise taxes) |

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "In a crisis nobody will accept your currency, so the IMF accepts your currency and gives you hard currency like the dollar. What you call a loan is really a purchase–repurchase transaction — and no loan is free. The cost of an IMF loan is conditionality."

He also punctured the idea of a country "going bankrupt." There is no stock exchange where countries are listed, and creditors cannot occupy an island when a nation defaults. So how is a country punished? Through its **credit rating** — push it low enough and the interest rate on its debt becomes crushing (see **Greece** in the euro-area crisis).

:::note[Good to Know]
**Why does Pakistan get IMF money again and again?** Because the IMF is **quota-based, not one-country-one-vote** — the US holds roughly 17% of the vote, India only around 2.8%. Pakistan plays the **US, China and Saudi Arabia** off each other at different times. (He's written papers on this; he said it won't be on the exam.)
:::

## The Five Global Challenges

He closed the descriptive tour with five forces every manager now has to read:

1. **Demographics** — humans are living longer and getting richer. Indian longevity roughly **doubled from 35 (1951) to around 70 (2025)**. A 90-plus Japanese woman doesn't want a flashy car; she wants **Medicare and nursing** — and nursing can't be virtual or robotic, which is why Japan has relaxed immigration for **Filipino nurses who speak Japanese**.
2. **Economic weight shifting to Asia** — the Maddison story, continuing.
3. **A technology revolution** — AI, nanotech, the Internet of Things reshaping every industry.
4. **Managing resource scarcity** — India is roughly **100 years behind the US in development**, but can't burn coal freely the way the US once did, because of **Paris / climate** commitments. China and India get labelled the "ultimate polluters."
5. **New nationalism** — global interdependence is not matched by global governance, so the global economy keeps colliding with local politics.

## How Big Is India, Really? Nominal GDP vs PPP

Here he slipped from stories toward measurement — and this arithmetic is very exam-friendly. There are two ways to size an economy, and they give wildly different answers.

| Measure | How it's built (India, 2024) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal / current market price** | ₹330.7 trillion ÷ **84.6** (₹ per USD) | **USD 3.9 trillion** |
| **PPP (purchasing power parity)** | ₹330.7 trillion ÷ **20.4** (PPP rate) | **USD 16.2 trillion** |

Why the huge gap? Because the **market exchange rate** can be manipulated (fixed in Saudi Arabia, managed in China) and ignores that the *same money buys more* in India. So the IMF and World Bank price a **standard basket of goods** across countries to get a **PPP exchange rate**. His own example: he can afford a driver in India and his US counterpart can't — not because he earns more, but because **drivers are expensive in New York**. The **Big Mac Index** is the same idea in burger form.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "There is only one country on earth for which GDP at market price and GDP at PPP are the same — and that is the United States. For everyone else, it depends on whether you are cheaper or costlier than the US."

Note which number politicians use: the **"USD 5 trillion economy"** dream refers to **nominal** GDP (we're already past 16 trillion on PPP). Macro, he said, usually cares about the nominal/current-market-price figure.

:::tip[Important — Advanced vs Emerging, and the great flip]
The world labels about **39 "advanced" economies** and roughly **150 "emerging" ones** — and membership is less about per-capita income than "the club you keep." The share has flipped hard: advanced economies fell from about **64% (1990) to 40%**, while emerging economies climbed from **36% to about 60%**. *That* shift — led by emerging Asia at around 34% (China the "big guy", India the "second brother") — is the real tension behind today's trade politics.
:::

Also keep **price vs inflation** straight: a *price* is a level; **inflation is the rate of change of prices** (dP/dt ÷ P). Confusing the two is a classic slip.

## Why the Dollar Still Rules

A natural assumption is "big GDP means a big currency." The professor says no — **currency power comes from the currency market, not GDP**. The world is run by about **ten currencies** (USD, euro, pound, yen, Aussie dollar, NZ dollar, Canadian dollar, Swiss franc, Norwegian krone, Swedish krona). China has roughly a fifth of world GDP but only about **2%** of global currency use; India sits in "others."

A real international currency does **three jobs** (Krugman's framing): **medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.** The dollar does all three — a beggar outside the airport will take dollars, India–Bangladesh trade is *invoiced* in dollars, banks settle in dollars, the RBI intervenes in dollars, and reserves are held in dollars. Contrast the **India–Iran rupee-for-oil** pact: Iran ends up with rupees it can only spend on Indian rice — that's **barter**, not a true international currency.

So *why* the dollar? His answer is **network externality** (a phrase from **Ben Bernanke**, the only Fed Chair who is also a Nobel laureate):

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "A accepts the dollar from B because A knows C will accept it from A. That is the whole phenomenon."

And the attitude behind it, in the famous line from Nixon's Treasury Secretary **John Connally**: *"The dollar is our currency, but your problem."*

:::tip[Memory Hook — "A takes it because C will"]
**Network externality** in one line: you accept dollars not because *you* love them, but because you're sure the next person will. That self-reinforcing loop is what keeps the dollar on top.
:::

**The looming threat — CBDCs.** If the RBI's **digital rupee (CBDC)** were accepted directly by, say, the Bank of England under an MoU at the day's exchange rate, you'd no longer need dollars to visit the UK. That's exactly why **Trump is anti-CBDC but pro-stablecoin** (a crypto pegged 1:1 to the dollar — which *keeps* the dollar central). A real **BRICS** payment future, he thinks, looks less like a single "BRICS currency" and more like interlinked **CBDCs**. (In India, crypto is taxable — but whether it's legal, "nobody knows.")

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 1 is the professor handing you a pair of glasses before handing you any equations. Look through them and the headlines rearrange themselves: a trade deficit is comparative advantage, not weakness; a currency is trust, not metal; the dollar's crown is a network effect, not a law of nature; and Asia's rise is a thousand-year pendulum swinging back.

The manager who memorises one fixed answer keeps getting blindsided when the context shifts. The one who learns the **method** — and remembers that the questions stay the same while the answers keep changing — can read the next shock before it lands.
