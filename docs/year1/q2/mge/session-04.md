# Session 4: The Short-Run Sugar Rush and the Long-Run Snap-Back

*The professor finally bolts demand onto supply. Aggregate demand comes out of IS–LM, aggregate supply runs at two speeds, and the whole machine delivers one hard truth: policy can buy you output today, but you pay for it in prices tomorrow — and in the long run you snap back to potential. There is no free lunch.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]

## Price vs Price Level

A **price** is attached to a single good — the price of this pen, this shirt (call it Pᵢ for good *i*). A **price level** is a **weighted average of many prices** — an index: P = w₁P₁ + w₂P₂ + … where the wᵢ are the weights. Change the weights (whose basket you track) and you get a different price level.

Two families of index:

- **WPI (Wholesale Price Index)** — prices at the wholesale/bulk stage.
- **CPI (Consumer Price Index)** — prices the retail consumer actually pays.

First-principles link: **wholesale price + indirect tax (passed on) + trader margin = consumer price.** Which index is "more dependable"? The economist's favourite answer — **it depends.** For the citizen's cost of living, CPI matters; for the underlying trend in the economy, WPI matters.

**India's quirk — too many CPIs.** India separately publishes CPI for agricultural workers, rural labourers, industrial workers, and urban non-manual employees — with no clean theory for why they diverge month to month. So the standard headline is **CPI-All-India** (with urban and rural versions underneath). A politician who wants to brand the government "anti-rural" simply quotes whichever sub-index looks worst — a reminder that **the choice of index is itself political.**

:::tip[Important]
**WPI + indirect tax + trader margin = CPI.** And "which index is right?" has no absolute answer — CPI for the household, WPI for the macro trend. Knowing the plumbing lets you see through a cherry-picked "inflation is high / low" headline.
:::

---

## Inflation Is a Rate, Not a Level

This is the distinction the whole session hangs on. **Inflation is the rate of change of the price level**, not the level itself:

> inflation in period *t* = (this period's price − last period's price) ÷ last period's price × 100.

**The number trick that catches everyone.** Suppose the price level goes 100 → 110 → 120. From 100 to 110 is **10%**. From 110 to 120 is (120 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 ≈ **9.1%**. So the **price level kept rising, yet inflation fell.**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "One is the first derivative, the other is the level — exactly like displacement and velocity. Please remember, inflation is the rate of change of price."

So when a homemaker says "prices are up, inflation is terrible," the two need not move together. And when the RBI later says inflation has become "more benign than expected," it means **inflation came down even though prices went up.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Price level = where prices are; inflation = how fast they're changing.** Prices can rise while inflation falls (100 → 110 → 120 gives 10%, then ≈ 9.1%). Confusing the level with its first derivative is the single most common macro slip — and the entire RBI story ("inflation benign, so I can be expansionary") only makes sense once you keep them apart.
:::

## Building Aggregate Demand from IS–LM

Here is the engine room. Recall the two IS–LM equations:

- **IS (goods market):** I(R) + G = S(Y) — investment (falling in R) plus government spending equals saving (rising in Y).
- **LM (money market):** M/P = L(Y) + L(R) — real money supply equals money demand: transaction demand (rising in Y) plus speculative demand (falling in R).

Notice **P appears only in the LM equation**, as M/P — the **real money supply**, an **exogenous** quantity sitting on the left-hand side. (A student's trap he corrected: M/P is *given from outside*; Y and R are the variables being solved.) So when the price level moves, only LM moves.

**Trace it.** Let **P fall.** Then **M/P rises** → the **LM curve shifts outward** → equilibrium income **Y rises** (and R falls). Do this for several price levels and you collect pairs — high P with low Y, low P with high Y. Plot those (Y, P) pairs and you get a **downward-sloping Aggregate Demand curve.**

Three things to bank:

1. **AD is derived from IS–LM** (by letting P vary).
2. **AD slopes downward.**
3. **Every point on AD is a full IS–LM equilibrium — equilibrium in three markets at once: commodity, money, and bond** (by **Walras's law**: if two of three markets clear, the third must).

And the warning he hammered: this AD is **not** the microeconomic demand for one good. The x-axis is **Y = GDP**, aggregate output. No "hyperbola," no single-commodity income elasticity — this is the whole economy.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Each point on the aggregate demand curve denotes equilibrium in three markets — commodity, money, and the one you always forget, the bond market."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "AD is IS–LM wearing a price axis"]
The aggregate demand curve is just the IS–LM model re-plotted against the price level. Lower price → more real money → LM shifts out → higher GDP. Every dot on AD is a three-market equilibrium.
:::

## What Moves AD: Demand Management

If each AD point hides an IS–LM equilibrium, then **anything that shifts IS or LM shifts AD** — which is exactly the toolkit of monetary and fiscal policy:

| Lever | Who pulls it | Effect on AD |
|---|---|---|
| Cut the **repo rate** / raise money supply | RBI (shifts LM) | **Out** (expansionary) |
| Raise the **CRR** (banks lend less) | RBI (shifts LM) | **In** (contractionary) |
| Cut **GST**, raise the **income-tax exemption**, add a **subsidy** | Finance Ministry (shifts IS) | **Out** |
| Reverse of the above | Finance Ministry (shifts IS) | **In** |

A sharp subtlety: a **revenue-neutral** GST reshuffle (some goods up, some down, same effective rate) leaves aggregate Y **unchanged** — because Y is apples *and* oranges together. And a subsidy that merely **shifts** demand from one good to another may not lift AD "to the desired extent."

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Whatever the Minister of Finance and the Reserve Bank do, they are only playing around with aggregate demand. Nothing else."

That is **demand-management policy** — steering the economy by sliding the AD curve.

## Aggregate Supply in Two Speeds

Demand is half the picture. Supply comes in **two time horizons.**

**Long-run AS (LRAS) — vertical.** In the long run all adjustments are done, so output is pinned by the **production function** Y = f(K, L): capital, labour and productivity are all fixed, so output is fixed at **Ȳ**, the **natural rate of output** (potential output). On a price–output diagram, LRAS is a **vertical line at Ȳ** — the price level can be anything; output cannot exceed potential.

**Short-run AS (SRAS) — upward-sloping.** Supply reacts to **price surprises**, but with a lag:

> Y = Ȳ + α(P − Pᵉ),  with α a positive fraction, and Pᵉ the **expected price.**

Rearranged: **P = Pᵉ + (1/α)(Y − Ȳ)**, so SRAS slopes **upward.** The gap **Y − Ȳ is the "output gap"** — the term an RBI governor uses when he calls the output gap "positive" or "negative." When a price shock hits, firms temporarily produce more (drawing down inventory, working harder); it can't last, but briefly supply responds.

**The curve bends.** At **low output** there's plenty of spare capacity, so expanding is cheap → SRAS is **flat.** At **high output** (unemployment below natural, capital stretched) expanding is costly → SRAS is **steep.**

**Measuring Ȳ.** Actual GDP is noisy; Ȳ is the underlying trend. Economists extract it with a **filter** — a simple trend (Y = a + b·*t*) or, in practice, the **Hodrick–Prescott (HP) filter.** Then Y − Ȳ can be positive *or* negative.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Long-run AS is vertical at potential output Ȳ** (set by the production function). **Short-run AS slopes up:** Y = Ȳ + α(P − Pᵉ), equivalently P = Pᵉ + (1/α)(Y − Ȳ). The driver is **price surprises** (actual P above or below expected Pᵉ), and the distance **Y − Ȳ is the output gap.** Memorise both the picture (vertical LRAS, upward SRAS) and that one equation.
:::

## The Short-Run Boost and the Long-Run Snap-Back

Now the payoff — put AD, LRAS and SRAS on one diagram. They meet at **E**: output at **Ȳ**, with **P = Pᵉ** (actual price equals expected, so the output-gap term is zero).

**Step 1 — the boost.** The Finance Minister and RBI together do an **expansionary** push (cut GST, cut CRR, cut repo). **AD shifts out** to a new point **F**: output **Y1 is higher** — everyone's happy — but the **price level P1 is also higher.** Crucial condition: the RBI governor only agrees to this **if the starting price level isn't already too high.**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "In the short run you can increase output, but only accompanied by an increase in price. You cannot have both the good things together."

**Step 2 — the snap-back.** At F, the actual price now exceeds what people expected (P above Pᵉ). **People are not fooled** — they revise their expectations upward. That **shifts SRAS up**, dragging the economy from **F to G**: **output falls back to Ȳ**, but at a **still-higher price.**

So **demand management works in the short run and evaporates in the long run** — you end with the same potential output and only higher prices. Repeat the trick too often and you set off a wage–price spiral.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Sugar rush, then snap-back"]
Expansionary policy is a sugar rush: output spikes (E → F), but once expectations catch up it snaps back to potential at a higher price (F → G). The high lasts a quarter; the extra zeros on the price tag stay.
:::

## Demand Management vs Structural Policy

If demand management can't raise **Ȳ**, what can? **Structural policy** — the slow work of lifting potential output itself. When the professor asked "which ministry controls the long run?", the answer wasn't Finance — it was, only half-jokingly, **Education.**

- Better **schools, roads, ports** and **human capital** push the whole LRAS line outward.
- **Female labour-force participation** rising (for example, through effective **minimum-age-of-marriage** enforcement) raises Ȳ.
- Money alone isn't the point — **outcome beats expenditure.** His contrast: Bangladesh micro-loans of as little as **1,000 taka** empowering women so their children end up better educated; versus Class-8 kids who still can't do basic multiplication despite the spending.

When the **Prime Minister says the economy "can grow at 6–7%,"** he's describing **Ȳ, the potential** — and that potential rises only through structural policy. Later the professor bundled it into **four infrastructures: physical, human, regulatory, and digital.**

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Demand moves AD, structure moves Ȳ"]
Monetary + fiscal policy = **demand management** (slides AD against a fixed potential). Education, infrastructure, institutions = **structural policy** (pushes the vertical LRAS, i.e., Ȳ, outward). Growth you can *keep* comes only from the second kind.
:::

## When Supply Itself Is Hit: Shocks

SRAS can also be shoved by **supply shocks** — events that change aggregate production costs or capacity: a **war**, the **COVID pandemic** (both a demand *and* a supply shock, plus a logistics / supply-chain shock), the **AI** wave, **H-1B visa restrictions**, **national strikes / bandhs**, or **sanctioning a whole country** (such as restrictions on Russia).

He deliberately kept the lens **domestic** for now — a closed economy. "You don't elect the world leaders," so reason about a shock as it lands on **one country's** GDP. (Exports, imports and the exchange rate arrive next session.)

## No Free Lunch: The Inflation–Output Trade-off

This is the moral spine of the session. Every short-run output gain is **bought with higher prices** — an **inflation–output trade-off.** Push demand too hard, too often, and you don't get rich; you get **Zimbabwe**, where annual inflation hit roughly **1 million%** (about **98% a day** — count the zeros). Weimar **Germany's** hyperinflation, he noted, is even cited as a condition for **Hitler's rise.**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "There is no free lunch. You cannot sleep more *and* learn more economics."

This is precisely *why* the RBI practises **inflation targeting:**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Inflation targeting is the Reserve Bank's way of saying *no* to the Finance Minister."

When the FM pleads "increase money supply, cut the repo, generate demand," the governor can reply: "I'd have loved to — but inflation is already near 6% and I'm inflation-targeting." It binds the government's hand. Yet rules still need **judgment, not a robot:** the FM **cut GST now, not before the last election**, as a deliberate counter to the **tariff shock** from "your favourite president." Policy is *timed.*

**And why not just skip the painful short run and live in the stable long run?** Because you have to survive to get there:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Tell a beggar, 'go to school so you get a job in fifteen years' — the question is, how does he survive those fifteen years? We live for the short run."

That is the spirit of Keynes's famous line, which he quoted approvingly: **"In the long run we are all dead."**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**There is no free lunch: output and inflation trade off.** Demand management lifts output only by raising prices; overused, it ends in hyperinflation (Zimbabwe, Weimar). **Inflation targeting** is the institutional "no" that stops a re-election-hungry government from over-stimulating — but the *timing* of any easing (e.g., the GST cut against a tariff shock) still needs human judgment.
:::

## Why Prices Are Sticky (the hidden assumption)

The entire upward-sloping SRAS rests on one thing: **prices don't adjust instantly.** If they did (if P always equalled Pᵉ), the short-run curve would vanish and only the **vertical** LRAS would remain. So *why* are prices **sticky**? His quick tour (each has a formal model he skipped):

- **Coordination failure** — prices are set at head office; information from the field can take about a year to travel (roughly 60% of managers report this).
- **Cost-based pricing, delivery lags, implicit / nominal contracts, hierarchical delays.**
- **Menu cost** — the classic:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Fish, vegetable, mutton and chicken prices change every day — but your restaurant doesn't reprint its menu each day, because there's a cost to printing the menu."

- **Judging quality by price** — in "notoriously price-sensitive" markets like **perfume and alcohol**, buyers read a higher price as higher quality (why **Chanel** "beats" a cheaper rival is partly the price tag itself).

:::note[Good to Know]
Price stickiness is the quiet assumption that makes short-run policy work at all. Perfectly flexible prices → a vertical supply curve → monetary and fiscal policy would move **only prices, never output.** The menu-cost story (daily market prices, a seasonal menu) is the memorable way to recall it.
:::

## The Phillips Curve (the AS curve in disguise)

In **1958**, New-Zealand-born economist **A.W. Phillips** found a **negative relationship between unemployment and wage inflation** (and wages are the big driver of prices). Later work restated it as a negative link between **unemployment and inflation**:

> inflation (π) = expected inflation (πᵉ) − β × (u − uₙ),

where **uₙ is the natural rate of unemployment.** This is **the very same relationship as short-run AS**, just drawn on different axes — SRAS lives in the **price–output (P, Y)** plane; the Phillips curve lives in the **inflation–unemployment (π, u)** plane. An **upward-sloping SRAS** is a **downward-sloping Phillips curve.**

**Natural rate of unemployment** (we owe it to **Milton Friedman**): labour-market frictions mean unemployment never hits zero, even at potential. His picture — jobs open in **Mumbai** while the jobless sit in **Kolkata** and won't move for personal / family reasons, so measured unemployment stays **positive, typically ~4–5%.** (In the West the jobless also draw an **unemployment allowance** — "bread and butter, not jam.")

:::tip[Important]
**Phillips curve = the AS curve, different axes:** π = πᵉ − β(u − uₙ). Lower unemployment buys higher inflation in the short run; shift expectations (πᵉ) and the whole curve moves, so the trade-off doesn't last. If an exam hands you the Phillips curve *or* the SRAS equation, you can translate one into the other.
:::

## From Model to Market: Reading an Actual RBI Statement

To "come close to reality," the professor read the **RBI statement of 6 August 2026** and the **MPC minutes of 20 August** against the model:

- **Demand side (Y = C + I + G + X − M):** growth "holding up"; **rural consumption resilient**, **urban discretionary spending tapering**, government **capex** supporting investment — i.e., the C, I, G terms.
- **Supply side (reads off SRAS):** a good monsoon, rising **capacity utilisation**, Services **PMI** at an 11-month high (60.5 in July), industry "subdued and uneven."
- **The decisive line:** inflation "**more benign than expected**" — the governor isn't afraid of near-term price rises, so he "can afford to be expansionary."
- **The decision:** the **MPC** (chaired by Governor **Sanjay Malhotra**) **held the repo rate at 5.50%** (SDF 5.25%, MSF and bank rate 5.75%), consistent with the **CPI target of 4% ± 2%** "while supporting growth."

Two mechanics he flagged:

- **Expected price comes from surveys, not a formula** — the RBI runs **professional-forecaster** and **household / homemaker** surveys across cities.
- Headline inflation is **year-on-year:** (this month's price − the same month last year's price) ÷ last year's price × 100. Why not month-on-month? To wash out **seasonality** — you compare this Diwali with last Diwali.

## What the Inflation Numbers Say

Finally, the data (CPI from the **NSO**, released as rural / urban / **combined**; the RBI watches the **combined** line):

- **Basket weights:** **food and beverages ≈ 45%** (food alone ≈ **39%**), housing ≈ 10% (urban-only, since rural housing markets differ), plus clothing, fuel, health, transport, education, and so on. So in India, **inflation is tied to rainfall, agriculture and crops** — there was even a spell when the price of **dal (lentils)** drove the headline.
- **The recent arc:** **2020–21** spiked (COVID = demand + supply + logistics shock); **2022–23** ran hot (above ~7%, so the RBI raised the repo); **2024–25** cooled all the way to ~**1.6%** — which the professor is *not* happy about (he'd prefer **2–5%**).
- **Why not zero?**

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "A zero inflation rate is not a good thing — a zero inflation rate means a lack of demand."

Zero inflation implies incomes aren't rising and the economy is at a standstill (as if births exactly matched deaths); **negative inflation (deflation) is worse** — as in **US 2009**, where recession-driven low demand produced **disinflation**.

- **Why the 4% ± 2% band?** Econometric evidence shows inflation **beyond ~6% becomes counterproductive** for output, so the band runs **2–6% with 4% as the median** — not 3–7 or 4–8.
- **Cross-country sanity check** (free **IMF / IFS** data): India sits in reassuring **single digits**, while **Venezuela, Argentina, Pakistan, Sri Lanka** carry the scars of **policy mismanagement plus supply shocks**; **Thailand** hovers near zero because it's a **very open** (tourism-heavy) economy.

:::note[Good to Know]
The CPI **basket is rebased roughly every decade** (the 1980 basket had no computers; petrol's treatment gets revisited), and the professor disclosed that he himself sits on the **Government of India committee on national accounts and prices** — a reminder that these indices are living, negotiated constructs, not laws of nature.
:::

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 4 is where the course stops *describing* the economy and starts *steering* it — then admits the wheel has limits. Demand management (the RBI's rates, the FM's taxes) can speed the car up for a while, but the engine's true size is **Ȳ**, set by schools, roads and institutions. Every attempt to outrun potential shows up as inflation — which is why a central bank's most important word is often **"no."** Read the next RBI statement and you'll now see the whole AD–AS machine humming behind three dry sentences about growth and prices.
