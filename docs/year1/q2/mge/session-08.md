# Session 8: Same Policy, Opposite Endings — When the Exchange-Rate Regime Decides What Works

*Open the economy and a strange thing happens: the exact same lever — government spending, money supply, a tariff — succeeds in one country and fails in another. The Mundell–Fleming model shows why. Along the way: why the exchange rate is a market price, why your capital can flee even when you did nothing wrong, and the Impossible Trinity every central banker lives inside.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **small open economy** assumption (home rate = world rate, R = R-star); the **open-economy IS and LM curves** in the output–exchange-rate plane (IS\* slopes down, LM\* is vertical); **policy effectiveness flips with the regime** — fiscal works only under a **fixed** rate, monetary works only under a **floating** rate, trade policy works only under a **fixed** rate; the **Impossible Trinity** (free capital flow + independent monetary policy + fixed exchange rate — pick two).

**Important (supporting):** Exchange rate is **market-determined** (supply of forex = S − I, demand = NX; the RBI is a big player, not the only one); how a central bank **defends a peg** only by expanding/contracting money (buying/selling dollars); **open-economy crowding out** of net exports; why **capital moves** (country risk + expected currency change); the **Rey dilemma** and **reserves** refinement.

**Context (colour for answers):** The "Great Trump effect" on tariffs; Saudi and China pegs vs India's float; USD 700 billion vs USD 3 trillion reserves; RBI's "Lakshman rekha" and SBI interventions; the ratings-agency example (India to US with nothing changed); FERA before 1991; G20 "no blame game"; the Euro's "15 fiscal policies, one monetary policy"; the 15-minutes-a-day reading habit.
:::

## Quick Recap

Professor Partha Ray opened the way he often does — teasing the class about "cumulative amnesia" before rebuilding the ground under their feet. In the previous session he had *begun* the **Mundell–Fleming model**, which he describes simply as the **open-economy extension of the IS–LM model**.

The closed-economy machine the class already knew: an **IS curve** from the goods market (savings and investment, driven by the interest rate R), and an **LM curve** from the money market (M/P = demand for money). You run **fiscal policy by changing G** and **monetary policy by changing M**. The remembered result: fiscal policy pushes **both the interest rate and output up**; monetary policy pushes the **interest rate down and output up**.

Tonight's job: open that economy to the rest of the world — add **net exports (NX)** and the **exchange rate** — and watch every neat conclusion acquire a condition attached to it.

---

## The One Assumption That Makes It "Small and Open"

Mundell made one simplifying move that carries the whole model: he fixed the home interest rate equal to the world interest rate, **R = R-star**. The model does not break without it; it just gets complicated, and — in the professor's words — "you and me are simple people."

That assumption is the definition of a **small open economy**, and the word *small* is the trap. It has **nothing to do with size**. It is about financial power: assume the US effectively controls world financial markets and sets the interest rate, so everyone else simply **takes** that rate as given — a price-taker in the global money market.

Two more simplifications keep the algebra clean: **prices are fixed** at home and abroad. Because the **real exchange rate equals the nominal rate times domestic prices over foreign prices**, freezing prices lets us talk about just the **nominal exchange rate E** and forget the real/nominal distinction for now.

:::note[Good to Know]
The professor flagged this honestly: the whole evening is **nominal** — no inflation yet. "If I bring prices, life becomes slightly more complicated." That is deliberate. The RBI governor's *real* job (output **and** inflation) is harder than this model; this is the clean skeleton you add flesh to later.
:::

---

## Reading the New Diagram: Output vs the Exchange Rate

Swap the old axes. Now plot **output (Y)** horizontally and the **nominal exchange rate (E)** vertically. The two open-economy curves — he writes them IS\* and LM\*, the star just flagging "open-economy version" — look like this:

- **The IS\* (goods-market) curve slopes down.** Raise Y and savings rise; to keep the goods market in balance with investment fixed (R is pinned at the world rate), **net exports must rise**, and NX only rises when the currency **depreciates** (E falls). Higher output therefore pairs with a weaker currency — a downward slope.
- **The LM\* (money-market) line is vertical.** With the interest rate nailed to the world rate, **only one level of output** can clear the money market, whatever the exchange rate does. A straight vertical line.

Where they cross gives **equilibrium output (Y-bar) and the equilibrium exchange rate (E-bar).** And here is the first big idea:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Exchange rate is something not dependent on the RBI, not dependent on the IMF, not dependent on the US Fed. It is determined in the market."

He drove the intuition home with a pair of prices. An **interest rate is the price of future consumption** — park money in the bank and you give up the car today. An **exchange rate is the price of your currency in terms of a foreign one.** Both are market prices.

When a student pushed back — "didn't you say the RBI *doesn't* control the exchange rate?" — the professor sharpened it. The exchange rate is set by **demand for and supply of foreign exchange**: the **supply** of forex is **S − I** (savings not used at home) and the **demand** is **NX**. It clears where **S − I = NX**. The RBI is one supplier in that market, a big one with deep pockets, but not the whole market.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The exchange rate is a market price, set by the demand for and supply of foreign exchange (S − I = NX).** The central bank is a **large player, not the determiner.** The myth to break: "only the RBI sets the rupee." Reality — the market sets it, and the RBI *influences* it. In a fully pegged economy (a GCC state) the market barely matters; in India the market leads and the RBI leans against it.
:::

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The myth I wanted to break is that only the RBI can determine the rate. No — the market determines it, but the RBI also determines it. The RBI is another player, a big player, maybe with a deep pocket of USD 700 billion — but still a player."

## Two Regimes, One Fork in the Road

Everything that follows splits along one choice of **exchange-rate regime**:

- **Floating (flexible):** E is left free to move with economic conditions.
- **Fixed (pegged):** the central bank stands ready to buy or sell domestic currency for foreign currency at a pre-announced price.

How do you actually *hold* a peg? This is the mechanical heart of the session. The central bank buys and sells **foreign-exchange reserves**, and every such trade changes the **money supply**, because domestic money and foreign currency are swapped one-for-one. Buy dollars → equivalent rupees are **created**; sell dollars → rupees are **withdrawn**.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The only way the central bank can fix the exchange rate is by increasing or reducing the supply of money."

So on the diagram, "defend the peg" literally means "**shift the LM\* line**." If a shock would push E away from the target, the central bank moves money supply to drag the LM\* line back until E returns to its promised level. (He showed this is why **money is a liability of the RBI**, with **net foreign-exchange assets** sitting on the asset side — the lever they pull.)

Which currencies does the RBI actually watch? It publishes a **NEER (nominal effective exchange rate)** index built on a **40-currency basket**, but effectively it tracks about **six currencies** — the **US dollar, euro, pound sterling, yen and the Chinese renminbi** among them. One currency rises, another falls, so they aggregate into an index.

:::note[Good to Know]
A rare peek behind the counter from his RBI years: the Financial Markets department meets around **10:30 a.m.** to read the market and decide whether to intervene, and again near **3:30 p.m.** The RBI usually doesn't trade in its own name — it routes orders through **SBI**, so market players only notice "a sudden flash of dollars" slightly below the going rate. You confirm the RBI's hand only at the fortnight's money-supply numbers.
:::

And the RBI never fights the market to a standstill. If a governor tried to force the rupee to ₹50 by pumping dollars, the market would test him and the USD 700 billion would drain away. The RBI only **smooths**, it does not reverse the tide:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "The RBI will not go against the market. It tries to stabilize it — it draws a Lakshman rekha within which the exchange rate is allowed to move."

## The Punchline: Same Policy, Opposite Results

Here is the result the professor deliberately built up to over two sessions, because it is **not intuitive**. Students assume "a policy is a policy — it does what it does." Mundell–Fleming says the impact of a policy **depends entirely on the exchange-rate regime.**

| Policy lever | Floating (flexible) rate | Fixed (pegged) rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal expansion (↑ G)** | **Ineffective** — output unchanged, currency appreciates, net exports crowded out | **Effective** — bank expands money to hold the peg, output rises |
| **Monetary expansion (↑ M)** | **Very effective** — currency depreciates, net exports and output rise | **No role** — defending the peg forces the money back, output unchanged |
| **Trade restriction (tariff/quota)** | **Ineffective** — currency appreciates, exports fall, same deficit with less trade | **Effective** — the peg preserves the net-export gain, output rises |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Policy effectiveness flips with the regime.** Under a **float**: monetary policy is powerful, fiscal and trade policy are powerless. Under a **peg**: fiscal and trade policy are powerful, monetary policy is powerless. If a question gives you a policy, your **first move is to ask: fixed or floating?** The answer changes everything.
:::

Let us walk the three levers.

### Fiscal Policy — powerless when the rupee floats

Government spends more, so the **IS\* curve shifts right**. But the LM\* line is vertical, so output **cannot** move — instead the **currency appreciates (E rises)** and **net exports fall** by exactly as much as G rose. Go back to the identity **Y = C + I + G + NX**: G up, NX down, Y unchanged. The extra domestic demand is handed straight to foreigners.

That is **open-economy crowding out**: in a closed economy fiscal policy crowds out **investment**; in an open floating economy it **crowds out net exports**. To actually raise output you must *also* shift LM — i.e., combine fiscal with monetary policy. Which gives the night's quiet lesson about central-bank independence:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Monetary and fiscal policy are like two hands. There has to be synchronicity between them."

Picture a recession: the government ramps up spending (say, an NREGA-type push), but the governor refuses to expand money. The rupee appreciates, net foreign demand collapses, and the entire fiscal stimulus is "eaten away." They need **operational autonomy, but coordination** — not independence to the point of working against each other.

:::tip[Important]
"Independent" central bank does **not** mean "disconnected." Under a float, a pure fiscal stimulus appreciates the currency and self-destructs unless monetary policy leans the other way. That is the model's case for **monetary–fiscal coordination** — a favourite short-answer: name the mechanism (appreciation → net exports fall → crowding out), not just the slogan.
:::

### Monetary Policy — powerful when the rupee floats

Expand money, the **LM\* line shifts right**, output rises, the **currency depreciates**, **net exports rise** and support the higher output (Y ends above where it started). In a **closed** economy monetary easing worked through **lower interest rates lifting investment**; in an **open** economy it works through a **weaker currency lifting net exports** — same engine, different cylinder.

But there is a sting the professor wanted the class to feel:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Expansionary monetary policy does not raise world aggregate demand — it merely shifts demand from foreign to domestic product."

Our rising net exports come from **someone else's** market. An aggressive, rupee-depreciating easing in India can therefore **hurt US interests** — which is exactly why loose monetary policy abroad becomes a political issue at home. (He noted the real RBI governor also silently watches the second equation: cut the repo rate to lift consumption and investment, while engineering a **mild depreciation** so foreign demand helps too.)

### Trade Policy — "the Great Trump effect"

A **tariff or quota** cuts imports, which raises NX and shifts the **IS\* curve right**. Under a float, the currency simply **appreciates** and cancels the gain. The deadly conclusion:

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "Import restriction cannot reduce a trade deficit. The restriction reduces imports, and the appreciation reduces exports — so you get *less trade*, but the deficit is unchanged."

Imports fall (good for the tariff's fans), but the stronger currency makes your **exports** fall too, so **X − M barely moves.** Trump's tariffs "may make sense to his electorate, but not in economics" — because the US **floats**, so the deficit won't shrink. Under a **fixed** rate the central bank holds the currency (shifting LM), the net-export gain survives, output rises, and trade policy finally *bites*. Mundell wrote this in **1961**, before the world had even tried these regimes — he anticipated the shape of things to come.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Float loves Money; Peg loves G (and tariffs)"]
Under a **Float**, only **M**onetary policy works. Under a **Peg**, **F**iscal and trade policy work. Say it as: *"Floating currencies bend to the money supply; pegged currencies bend to the government."*
:::

## Why Would Anyone Choose to Fix?

If a peg kills your monetary policy, why do it?

- **Saudi Arabia** earns in dollars; pegging removes dollar-riyal instability that would scramble its main demand source (net exports). It is effectively **dollar-pegged** — which also keeps the **dollar central**, so it suits the US. (He wandered, pointedly, into *why* no US president criticises Saudi human rights — "a friendly country" — and the Khashoggi episode where "nothing happened.")
- **China** pegged to keep its **exports competitive**, holding the currency artificially so its goods stay cheap abroad. It can afford to because its reserves are enormous.

The reserves gap is the whole story: **China around USD 3 trillion, India around USD 700 billion.** India does **not** peg for two reasons — it lacks that large an **export basket**, and it lacks those **reserves**. Historically the world ran **fixed rates before the 1950s**, **pegged to gold from the 1950s to the early 1970s** (Bretton Woods), then let markets in — but "market" still means the central bank can be a **dominant player**, not a free-for-all.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "In international diplomacy there is no permanent friend and no permanent enemy — only permanent interest. It is gray — sometimes 0.25, sometimes 0.75. These models give you the analytical toolbox to think about it."

## Money Is Like Water: Why Capital Crosses Borders

If the home rate is really pinned to the world rate, why do they ever differ? "Money is like water — it finds its level, chasing the higher return." Two things pull rates apart:

1. **Country risk** — measured by **credit ratings** (S&P, Moody's, Fitch).
2. **Expected exchange-rate change** — if a currency is expected to fall, its borrowers must pay a **higher interest rate** to compensate lenders for the expected currency loss.

He built a tiny numerical example. Define a **risk-adjusted real return = nominal rate − inflation − theta**, where **theta** is a country-risk number (**lower theta = safer country**).

| | Nominal rate | Inflation | Theta (risk) | Risk-adjusted return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | 10 | 6 | 3.25 | **0.75** |
| **US — situation 1** | 2 | 1 | 0.50 | **0.50** |
| **US — situation 2** | 2 | 1 | 0.10 | **0.90** |

In **situation 1**, India's 0.75 beats the US's 0.50, so capital flows **US → India**. In **situation 2**, nothing real changes — India is identical, US rates and inflation are identical — but the **rating agencies cut US theta from 0.50 to 0.10**. Suddenly the US return (0.90) tops India (0.75) and capital reverses, **India → US.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Capital chases the risk-adjusted real return, and it is market-determined and beyond your control.** The example's punch: **India did nothing wrong and still suffered an outflow**, purely because a rating agency re-scored *the US*. Capital inflow and outflow sit "beyond narrow political considerations" — politicians take credit for appreciation but never the discredit for depreciation.
:::

## The Impossible Trinity (Mundell's Trilemma)

The professor ended the theory on the result he says you must carry for life. A country wants three good things: **free capital flow, an independent monetary policy, and a fixed exchange rate.** Mundell proved you can have **at most two**.

> **Professor Partha Ray:** "You cannot have all three together — free capital flow, independent monetary policy, and a fixed exchange rate."

| Country | Keeps (the chosen two) | Gives up |
|---|---|---|
| **USA** | Independent monetary policy + free capital flow | A fixed rate — the dollar **floats** |
| **Saudi Arabia / GCC** | Fixed rate + free capital flow | Monetary independence — it **mimics the Fed** |
| **China** | Fixed/semi-fixed rate + some monetary independence | Free capital flow — it runs **capital controls** |
| **India before 1991** | Managed rate + independent monetary policy | Free capital flow — blocked via **FERA** |

The GCC pegs are strikingly durable (the Saudi riyal near **3.75 per USD** for decades; Bahrain, Oman, Qatar, UAE all fixed), so they simply **import US monetary policy.** China holds its corner with a thick wall of capital restrictions — limits on overseas investment, scrutiny of cross-border payments, caps on mainland buying of Hong Kong insurance (China uses **Hong Kong** the way India's money once used **Mauritius**). India before liberalisation kept monetary independence and a managed rate by **blocking capital flows under FERA**.

Two modern refinements he name-checked:
- **Hélène Rey (LSE):** it is really a **dilemma, not a trilemma** — an independent monetary policy is possible **only if the capital account is managed.**
- **Joshua Aizenman:** the way to buy that room is **huge reserves accumulation**, which gives you the muscle to intervene. India has done **both** — some reserves, some capital restrictions.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Pick any two"]
Free capital · Independent money · Fixed rate — you get **two of three**. US drops the peg, Saudi drops its own money, China (and old India) drop free capital. Draw the triangle in the exam and label one country on each edge.
:::

## Coordinating a Connected World

Interdependence means **policy overlaps** (what India does hits the US and vice versa), the chance of **policy conflict**, and **strategic retaliation** — if the US slaps on a tariff, others won't "suck their thumbs," they tax back. So the world needs **macro-policy coordination**, and the formal bodies (IMF, World Bank, **WTO**) have largely failed — Trump "effectively wound up the WTO" not by cutting funds first but by **refusing to appoint judges** (a Supreme Court with no judges does nothing).

What's left are **informal clubs — G7, G20.** The G20, he noted from attending its **first meeting**, was a "clever US ploy" at the peak of the financial crisis, captured in Obama's phrase **"no blame game"** (meaning: don't blame us). **BRICS** and especially **SAARC** disappoint — summits with "exquisite food" and little else, hobbled by hostile neighbours. "The only place you can really pin Trump down today is the G20."

## Real-World Takeaway

The professor closed on method over memory. You will forget **Y = C + I + G** in ten years, but you should remember that **the exchange rate matters, the interest rate matters, the RBI faces a dilemma, there is an Impossible Trinity, and you do business with a Saudi Arabia that pegs and a China that controls capital.** Models are plasticine — you knead them to **sharpen your logic**, because "no slogan will do your thinking for you; as managers we have to make a method of the madness."

And the practical recipe he left for staying sharp: **15 minutes of a business daily every day**, **half an hour of The Economist each week**, **the Budget once a year**, and — if ambitious — the **RBI governor's monetary-policy speech six times a year.** Watch other countries' crises too, because Pakistan ("bad politics drove out economics") and Sri Lanka (leaned on China, got deserted) are living lessons in what *not* to do. In a connected economy, the model is global but the politics stays local — and the manager who can read that gap sees the next shock before it lands.
