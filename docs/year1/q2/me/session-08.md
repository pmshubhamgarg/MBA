# Session 8: One Rupee In, Three Rupees Out — The Keynesian Multiplier

*A small push to spending can swing a nation's GDP by a multiple of itself. Session 8 opens the black box of the Keynesian multiplier — and shows why governments "pump-prime", why wars boost output, and why a developing India grows faster than the US.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** Aggregate demand **AD = C + I + G** (closed economy); the **equilibrium income** derivation ending in **Y₀ = 1/(1 − c) × A**; the **multiplier = 1/(1 − c) = 1/MPS**; the **consumption function C = a + cYd** and **MPC (c)**; the **ripple / geometric-series** mechanism; the **government-spending multiplier**, the **tax multiplier = −c/(1 − c)**, and the **balanced-budget multiplier = 1**.

**Important (supporting):** Why I, G, T and A are treated as **autonomous**; the **45° line** equilibrium; **sticky prices / horizontal supply** in a depression; why a **higher MPC** gives a bigger multiplier (so developing economies grow faster); the **three limitations** of the model.

**Context (colour for answers):** The **GDP-deflator** news (WPI negative → the 7.8% looked inflated vs a truer ~6.5%); Keynes' line *"in the long run we are all dead"*; the **defence-spending / wars** table; the **Persian Gulf** negative-GDP puzzle; the Indo-China war and **abandoned Five-Year Plans**; "pump priming".

## Quick Recap

Last class the professor built the **consumption function** and introduced **C** (consumption) and **I** (investment). Session 8 assembles the full picture: put C, I and G together, find the economy's **equilibrium income**, and then unlock the one idea Keynes is famous for — the **multiplier**. The goal she set: understand the multiplier, see its power **during a recession**, and use it to read **fiscal policy**.

:::note[Good to Know — the GDP-deflator news hook]
She opened with the day's newspaper: the headline GDP growth of **7.8%** had been questioned. ## Why Aggregate Demand Rules the Short Run

In the 1930s, **John Maynard Keynes** argued that changes in **aggregate demand (AD)** have a powerful impact on **output, employment and prices in the short run**. Economies swing up and down — **business cycles** — and the *frequency* and *intensity* of those swings differ across countries. During a depression you see heavy unemployment, very low output and very low interest rates. For a manager, business cycles are bread-and-butter, so AD is where we dig.

Keynes deliberately parked the long run:

> **The professor:** "John Maynard Keynes said, and I quote, 'in the long run we are all dead' — so why bother about the long run? We are in the short run."

Because it is the **short run**, **wages and prices are sticky**. Picture a depression: huge **idle capacity**, people and machines sitting unused. Any extra supply comes at **no extra cost**, so prices do not rise — you get a **horizontal supply curve**. That single assumption is what lets the whole model ignore the supply side and focus only on demand.

---

## Building the Model: AD = C + I + G

For a **closed economy** we drop exports and imports (X − M) and work with **domestic aggregate demand**:

**AD = C + I + G**

On the diagram, price level sits on the y-axis and **aggregate output (GDP = income = Y)** on the x-axis, and AD slopes **downward** — just like a micro demand curve. As the price level rises, real spending falls, so AD falls.

What is "held constant" while we draw it? That is the macro twist:

- In **microeconomics** you hold income, substitutes/complements, and tastes & preferences constant.
- In **macroeconomics** you hold two families constant: **policy variables** (interest rates, taxes, government expenditure — fully under the policymaker's control) and **exogenous factors** (other countries' output, technology — which even policymakers cannot control).

When any of those change, the AD curve **shifts** out or in — exactly like a demand-curve shift in micro.

:::tip[Important]
Under the session's simplifying assumptions (no indirect taxes, no subsidies, no net factor income from abroad, closed economy), the professor treats **GDP = GNP = NNP = income = output = Y** as the same thing. That is why she swaps the words freely — in this model they genuinely coincide.
:::

## The Algebra: From AD to Equilibrium Income

This derivation is pure maths until the last step — and it is a classic exam ask. Follow it slowly.

1. Start: **Y = C + I + G**
2. Consumption function: **C = a + cYd**, where **a** = autonomous consumption (the intercept — consumption even when income is zero) and **c** = **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)**.
3. Disposable income: **Yd = Y − T** (income minus taxes).
4. Substitute: **Y = a + c(Y − T) + I + G = a + cY − cT + I + G**
5. Collect Y: **Y − cY = a − cT + I + G**, i.e. **Y(1 − c) = a − cT + I + G**
6. Solve: **Y₀ = (a − cT + I + G) × 1/(1 − c)**

Now bundle everything that is **autonomous** into one letter **A** (autonomous aggregate demand):

**A = a − cT + I + G** → **Y₀ = 1/(1 − c) × A**

That is the headline result: **equilibrium income = multiplier × autonomous expenditure.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Equilibrium income: Y₀ = 1/(1 − c) × A**, where **A = a − cT + I + G** is autonomous aggregate demand and **1/(1 − c)** is the **multiplier**. Be able to *derive* it from Y = C + I + G using C = a + cYd and Yd = Y − T. Examiners love watching the algebra, not just the final line.
:::

---

## Why Is Everything "Autonomous"?

"Autonomous" simply means **not a function of any other factor** in the model — it is injected from outside, so it shows up as a **shift**, not a slide along a curve. Two sharp student questions pinned this down.

- **Why is `a` (autonomous consumption) autonomous?** Because policymakers *cannot* set it — only **consumers as a group** change it over time. It is the **intercept** of the consumption function, so by definition it is not a function of Y.
- **Why is `I` autonomous?** Investment depends on **interest rates** (a policy variable) and **business expectations** (genuinely unpredictable — "you do not know what to expect"). Neither is a function of income, so we treat **I as autonomous**. Whether households' savings actually get re-injected as investment is exactly what we can't assume.
- **G and T** are **policy variables** — the government can raise or cut them tomorrow, at will.

> **The professor:** "If they want to increase G they'll increase G tomorrow… these are what we call autonomous items of aggregate demand."

When a student asked how *often* these change, the honest answer: it depends on the state of the economy and the **agility of the policymakers** — decisive ones move fast, cautious ones "wait and see".

## The 45° Line: Where Spending Meets Output

Switch diagrams: x-axis = **Y (income/output/GDP)**, y-axis = **total expenditure (C + I + G**, which is also GDP). The **45° line** is the set of all points where **total expenditure = total output** — every point on it is a possible **equilibrium**.

The **actual expenditure line** (the professor's "red line") is the **consumption function with I and G stacked on top** of it. Because I and G are autonomous (fixed amounts, not functions of Y), adding them just **shifts the consumption line up in parallel**. Where that red line **cuts the 45° line** is the **equilibrium level of income**.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Stack, then cross"]
**Stack** autonomous I and G on top of the consumption line, then find where it **crosses** the 45° line. That crossing is your equilibrium GDP. Move the stack up, and the crossing slides right by *more* than you pushed — that preview is the multiplier.
:::

## The Multiplier Effect: One Push, Many Ripples

Now inject extra autonomous investment: **I₀ → I₁**, a rise of **ΔI**. The expenditure line shifts up by ΔI, and the crossing with the 45° line moves to a new, higher equilibrium **Y₁**. The punchline you can *see* on the graph:

**ΔY is much greater than ΔI.**

One rupee of extra investment lifts GDP by **more than one rupee**. Why? Because of a **ripple (induced) effect**:

- **Round 1 (primary):** inject ΔI (say ₹1,000). Production rises to match the spending, which raises income.
- **Round 2:** that higher income raises consumption by **c × ΔY** → more production → more income.
- **Round 3:** consumption rises again, now by **c² × ΔI**… then **c³ × ΔI**… each round smaller because **c is less than 1**.

Add the rounds: **ΔI + cΔI + c²ΔI + c³ΔI + …** — a **geometric series** that sums to **ΔI × 1/(1 − c)**.

> **The professor:** "It multiplies the delta I… the delta I gets multiplied to a new level of delta Y. That's why it's called the multiplier."

**Worked example (her numbers):** with ΔI = ₹1,000 and MPC ≈ 0.667, the rounds are **1,000 → 666.67 → 444.44 → …**, summing to **3 × 1,000 = ₹3,000**. The first round is the **primary effect**; the rest are the **induced effects**.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Multiplier = 1/(1 − c) = 1/MPS** (because **1 − c = marginal propensity to save**). A one-unit injection of autonomous spending raises equilibrium income by **1/(1 − c)** units. The mechanism is a **geometric series** of induced consumption rounds — state both the *formula* and the *ripple story* for full marks.
:::

## Multiplier Values — and Why India Grows Fast

The multiplier's size rides entirely on **c (MPC)**:

| MPC (c) | Multiplier = 1/(1 − c) |
|---|---|
| 0.5 | 2 |
| 0.7 | ~3.3 |
| 0.8 | 5 |
| 0.9 | 10 |

MPC is typically **higher in less-developed / developing economies** and **lower in developed ones**. India's figure sits around **0.7–0.8**. A higher MPC means a bigger multiplier, hence more **growth potential** from each injection.

> **The professor:** "India is growing tremendously only because it's still not a fully developed economy like the US or China… because our MPC is much higher."

The catch in a deep depression: the **potential** is huge (idle resources, idle people, high unemployment) but **nobody is spending** — so you have to **"pump prime"** to kick-start the rounds.

:::tip[Important]
Do not confuse this with the earlier "saving drives growth" idea. That was a different context. **Here the context is a recession/depression**, where the problem is too little *spending*, not too little *saving*. A higher propensity to **consume** is what powers the multiplier out of a slump.
:::

## From Investment to Fiscal Policy: The Government-Spending Multiplier

**Fiscal policy** has exactly two instruments: **government spending (G)** and **taxes (T)**. (Monetary policy — interest rates via the RBI — is separate and comes next class.)

The investment multiplier had *nothing* to do with fiscal policy — it was driven by optimistic private investors. But Keynes' leap was this: if **private investors won't invest**, let the **government spend** instead — on factories, PSUs, dams, bridges, roads — and trigger the **same** chain of production → income → consumption.

> **The professor:** "There are no private investors willing to make that investment. Can we ask the government to spend on projects that spur investment?… pump prime, do something so that investment spurs on."

So the **government-spending multiplier is also 1/(1 − c)**: **ΔY = 1/(1 − c) × ΔG**. Same engine, different driver.

## Why Economists (Secretly) Love Wars

Her favourite illustration: **defence spending** as a giant dose of autonomous G. From the "Economic stimulus from defence spending" slide:

| Episode | Rise in defence spending (% of GDP) | Real GDP growth |
|---|---|---|
| World War I (buildup) | +10.2% | +13% |
| World War II (1939–44) | +41.4% | +69.1% |
| Korean War | +8% | +10.5% |
| Vietnam War | +1.9% | +9.7% |
| Persian Gulf War | +0.3% | **−1.3% (negative!)** |
| Iraq War | +0.1% | +0.5% |

(She also framed WWII more broadly as roughly **9% → 26%**.) The pattern mostly holds: big government spending, big GDP jump.

> **The professor:** "This is why economists and countries like wars… there's very high government spending, and because there's high government spending there is high GDP growth."

**The Persian Gulf puzzle:** spending rose, yet GDP growth went **negative**. The culprit was **exogenous, psychological** factors — fear of soaring oil prices made consumers **withhold spending**, which *offset* the multiplier. The same logic explained a student's question on the **Indo-China war**: heavy forced defence spending led to **Five-Year Plans being abandoned**, and if growth didn't rise it would be those same fear-driven, exogenous forces at work.

:::note[Good to Know]
A student asked: what if **both** I and G rise together? Then each multiplies by 1/(1 − c), so the effect roughly **doubles**. But in practice, **when private investment is strong, the government sits back** — it has public goods to fund and need not "spend for the sake of spending".
:::

## The Tax Multiplier: Always One Step Weaker

To fund extra G, the government can **tax more**. But taxes **reduce consumption**, so the tax effect is **negative** and works through a smaller first step.

**Tax multiplier = −c/(1 − c)**, so **ΔY₀ = −c/(1 − c) × ΔT**

Two things to note:

1. It is **negative** — taxes and income move **inversely**.
2. Its **absolute value is one less** than the government-spending multiplier, because the first round is only **−c × ΔT** (not the full ΔT).

**Worked example (her numbers):** MPC = 0.8, taxes rise by **USD 300 billion**. Tax multiplier = −0.8/0.2 = **−4**, so GDP **falls by 4 × 300 = USD 1,200 billion**. Trace the rounds: **−240 → −192 → −153.6 → −122.88 → …**, summing to **−1,200 billion**. Meanwhile the spending multiplier at c = 0.8 would be **5** — so tax (4) is indeed one step weaker.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "G full, T minus one"]
The **government-spending multiplier = 1/(1 − c)**; the **tax multiplier = −c/(1 − c)**. In plain terms: tax is **negative** and its size is always **one less** than spending. "G goes in whole; T comes in minus-one."
:::

---

## The Balanced-Budget Multiplier: The Magic Number 1

Now do both together — raise **G** and fund it with an equal rise in **T**. Add the two multipliers:

**1/(1 − c) + [ −c/(1 − c) ] = (1 − c)/(1 − c) = 1**

| MPC (c) | Govt-spending multiplier | Tax multiplier | Sum (balanced budget) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 2 | −1 | 1 |
| 0.8 | 5 | −4 | 1 |
| 0.9 | 10 | −9 | 1 |

The tax multiplier **exactly offsets** the spending multiplier, so the net effect is just **1** — **ΔY = ΔG**. Spend ₹100 funded by ₹100 of tax, and GDP rises by ₹100, no more.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The **balanced-budget multiplier = 1**, and — the surprising bit — it is **1 regardless of the value of c**. A rupee of extra spending matched by a rupee of extra tax raises income by exactly a rupee; the multiplier "magic" is **cancelled** by the tax drag. Formula to memorise: **ΔY/ΔG + ΔY/ΔT = 1/(1 − c) − c/(1 − c) = 1.**
:::

## The Fine Print: Three Big Limitations

The professor closed by naming what this model deliberately **ignores** — a favourite short-answer target:

1. **No monetary policy** — interest rates are completely ignored. (Fixed next class, which starts monetary policy.)
2. **No rest of the world** — it stays a **closed economy** throughout these ten sessions.
3. **No supply side** — it is *all* aggregate demand. This is justified **only** by the depression context: idle capacity gives a **horizontal supply curve**, so output can rise with no price increase. Change the context and this assumption breaks.

That is why Keynes' framework is nicknamed **"recessionary economics" / "depression economics"** — it was built for, and works best in, a slump. Inflation and money-supply effects are explicitly deferred to monetary policy.

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Aggregate demand (AD)** | Total planned spending; here AD = C + I + G (closed economy) |
| **Consumption function** | C = a + cYd; links consumption to disposable income |
| **Autonomous consumption (a)** | The intercept — spending even when income is zero |
| **MPC (c)** | Marginal propensity to consume; extra consumption per extra ₹1 of income |
| **MPS (1 − c)** | Marginal propensity to save; the leftover of each extra rupee |
| **Disposable income (Yd)** | Income after taxes: Yd = Y − T |
| **Autonomous demand (A)** | a − cT + I + G; the part not driven by income |
| **Equilibrium income (Y₀)** | Y₀ = 1/(1 − c) × A; where expenditure meets the 45° line |
| **Multiplier** | 1/(1 − c) = 1/MPS; how much Y rises per unit injection |
| **Ripple / induced effect** | The round-by-round geometric series that builds the multiplier |
| **45° line** | Every point where total expenditure = total output (equilibrium) |
| **Sticky prices** | Prices that don't move in the short run (horizontal supply in a slump) |
| **Government-spending multiplier** | 1/(1 − c) applied to ΔG |
| **Tax multiplier** | −c/(1 − c); negative, and one less in size than spending |
| **Balanced-budget multiplier** | Equal ΔG and ΔT → net multiplier of exactly 1 |
| **Pump priming** | Government injects spending to restart private activity |
