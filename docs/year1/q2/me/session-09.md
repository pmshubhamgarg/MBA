# Session 9: How ₹100 Becomes ₹1,000 — Inside the Money Machine

*Paper money is worth nothing on its own — it runs on trust and a government stamp. This session shows how the banking system takes one deposit and quietly multiplies it into many times more money, and how the RBI turns that multiplication up or down to steer the whole economy.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **money multiplier = 1 ÷ R** (reserve ratio), and the ₹100 → ₹1,000 story; the **fractional reserve system** (vs the 100% reserve system); **high-powered money / reserve money (M0)** and what the central bank *can* vs *cannot* control; the **modified money multiplier** once people hold cash (the currency–deposit leak).

**Important (supporting):** Functions of money and **liquidity**; **M0 / M1 / M3 / M4** (narrow vs broad money); **CRR vs SLR**; how the reserve ratio moves **money supply → interest rates** in recessions and booms; the central bank's toolkit (**OMO, repo/reverse repo, bank rate**).

**Context (colour for answers):** Cigarettes-as-money in POW camps, cattle and beer as commodity money; the promissory note on a banknote; demonetization and the monetization coefficient; Arthur Hailey's *The Moneychangers* bank-run; recent cooperative-bank failures.
:::

## What Actually Counts as "Money"?

People confuse **money** and **wealth**. The professor drew a clean line: **money is an asset used to buy goods and services** — a medium of exchange. Shares, property, gold lying in a locker are **wealth**; they must first be *liquidated into money* before you can spend them.

Money does three jobs:

- **Medium of exchange** — you hand it over and the deal is done.
- **Unit of account** — everything is priced in rupees or dollars.
- **Store of value** — you can hold it, though it's a *poor* one (minimal interest).

The deciding test is **liquidity** — the ease with which an asset converts into a medium of exchange. Money is the **most liquid** asset of all: it scores very high on exchange, low on store of value.

:::tip[Important]
**Money vs wealth = liquidity.** An asset is "money" only to the degree you can spend it *immediately* without converting it first. Cash is perfectly liquid; a house is wealth until it is sold. Lead any money answer with this liquidity idea.
:::

## From Barter to Fiat: The Evolution of Money

**Barter came first** — and it was a nightmare. You needed a "double coincidence of wants": if you had shoes and wanted milk, you had to find someone with milk who *also* wanted shoes. Very hard to match.

So societies moved to **commodity money** — one commodity everyone accepts. Gold and silver are the obvious ones, but the professor enjoyed the odd examples: **cattle, oil, beer, and cigarettes** (famously used as money by prisoners of war). The common thread: it **moves around fast** and has **intrinsic value** — real production has gone into it.

Then came the leap to **fiat money** — paper with **no intrinsic value at all**. It is money only because the **government decrees** it so, which makes it **legal tender**.

> **The professor:** "Only by government decree will you accept that piece of paper as money — otherwise it's just a piece of paper."

This is also why **private individuals cannot *legally* create money** — illegally you can (that's **counterfeit**, the "fuzzy notes"), but you may get caught. The government stays **central to establishing and regulating** the monetary system. Look closely at a banknote and you'll see a **promissory note** — "I promise to pay…" — a legal promise, not a valuable object.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Barter, Commodity, Fiat"]
The money ladder is **B → C → F**: **B**arter (swap goods), **C**ommodity (gold, cattle, cigarettes with real value), **F**iat (paper worth nothing except by government decree).
:::

## The Bank as a Safe: Velocity, Not Creation

Originally a bank was just a **safekeeping device**. You deposited ₹100, got a **claim** (a receipt), and the bank held your money until you wanted it back — charging a *fee*, not paying interest.

Here's the subtle bit. That single ₹100 claim could still fund **many transactions**: you pay the grocer with the claim, the grocer pays the landlord, the landlord pays someone else — the same claim circulating again and again, until the final holder redeems it for ₹100.

> **The professor:** "No money is created here — the transactions are happening by **velocity**. The claim of ₹100 keeps going around, facilitating all the transactions."

So **velocity** = how many times a unit of money changes hands. In this pure safekeeping world, banks kept **100% reserves** and created **zero** new money. That's one extreme — and the launch pad for the real magic.

## Measuring the Money Stock: M0, M1, M3, M4

Before the magic, the vocabulary. India measures money in layers:

| Measure | What it includes | Nickname |
|---|---|---|
| **M0** | Currency in circulation **+** banks' and government's deposits with the RBI | **High-powered / reserve money** |
| **M1** | Currency with the public **+** demand (checkable) deposits | **Narrow money** (most liquid) |
| **M2** | M1 **+** post-office savings deposits | — |
| **M3** | M1 **+** time (fixed) deposits with banks | **Broad money** |
| **M4** | M3 **+** all post-office deposits | Aggregate monetary resources |

**M0** is the one the RBI controls directly — currency plus the reserves banks and the government park at the RBI. That total control is exactly why it is called **high-powered money**. The banking system cares most about **M1 and M3**; the central bank cares about **M0**.

> **The professor:** "Whatever the RBI wants to manipulate — interest rates, money supply — it straight away has the **high-powered money** to control it."

:::note[Good to Know]
The ratio **M3 ÷ GDP** is the **monetization coefficient**. During **demonetization**, money was suddenly squeezed out of the system — the professor noted the scars ran deep. It's a neat real-world handle for how "monetized" an economy is.
:::

## The Real Magic: How Banks *Create* Money

A bank is a business — it wants **profit**, and pure safekeeping earns nothing. So banks noticed something: **not every depositor demands their money at the same time.** People leave deposits untouched because they **trust** the bank.

> **The professor:** "They play a slightly risky game, based on the perception that not everyone will come back for the money at the same time. People have **trust** in the banks."

That trust lets a bank keep only a **fraction** of deposits as reserves and **lend out the rest** — the **fractional reserve system** (the central bank sets the minimum so the game doesn't get reckless). Watch the money appear:

1. You deposit **₹100** in bank PB1. Reserve ratio is **10%**, so PB1 keeps **₹10** and lends **₹90**.
2. Money supply is now **currency + demand deposits = ₹100 + ₹90 = ₹190.** New money has been created.
3. That ₹90 gets spent and lands as a deposit in **PB2**, which keeps ₹9 and lends **₹81**.
4. ₹81 becomes a deposit in **PB3**, which lends **₹72.90**… and on it goes, shrinking each round.

| Round | Bank | Deposit received | Keeps 10% | Lends out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | PB1 | ₹100 | ₹10 | ₹90 |
| 2 | PB2 | ₹90 | ₹9 | ₹81 |
| 3 | PB3 | ₹81 | ₹8.10 | ₹72.90 |
| … | … | … | … | … |
| **Total** | — | **₹1,000** | **₹100** | **₹900** |

It's the **same geometric series** as the income multiplier from the fiscal sessions. The sum gives the **money multiplier = 1 ÷ R**, where **R is the reserve ratio**. With R = 10% = 0.1, the multiplier is **10**, so the original **₹100 deposit multiplies into ₹1,000**.

> **The professor:** "The money multiplier is the reciprocal — **one by the reserve ratio.**"

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Fractional reserve banking creates money; a 100% reserve system does not.**
**Money multiplier = 1 ÷ R.** Lower the reserve ratio → bigger multiplier → more money supply. In the class example, R = 10% turns a ₹100 deposit into **₹1,000** of money. Be ready to draw the ₹100 → ₹90 → ₹81 chain and state the 1 ÷ R formula.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Lower the wall, taller the tower"]
R is the wall each bank must hold back. **Lower the reserve wall, taller the money tower** — a 10% wall builds ×10; a 5% wall builds ×20; a 20% wall builds only ×5.
:::

## The Policy Dial: Reserve Ratio → Money → Interest Rates

Because the multiplier is **1 ÷ R**, the central bank can steer the whole economy just by moving **R**:

- **Recession:** the RBI wants spending and GDP to grow, so it **lowers the reserve ratio** → bigger multiplier → **more money supply** → an environment friendly to spending.
- **Boom / peak:** a peak is above the sustainable trend and must come down; to engineer a **soft landing** (not a hard crash), the RBI **raises the reserve ratio** → smaller multiplier → **less money supply**.

Interest rates ride on the same logic. **Interest rate is the price of money** — set by money supply and money demand. More money supply → **lower interest rates** → more investments become viable → more borrowing and spending.

:::tip[Important]
There is a catch the professor stressed: the RBI can only **create the conditions**, it cannot force action. It lowers the reserve ratio *hoping* investors borrow — but if some **external shock** (say a war abroad) spooks them, the transmission stalls. Policy sets the stage; it can't guarantee the play.
:::

## The Leak: When People Hold Cash (The Modified Multiplier)

The clean ₹100 → ₹1,000 story assumed **every rupee gets re-deposited.** In reality people keep **part of their money as cash** — for convenience, contingencies, or because they **don't fully trust the bank**. That cash **leaks out** of the banking system and the multiplier shrinks.

To capture this, split the plumbing:

- **High-powered money (monetary base), H = CU + R** — currency **plus** reserves (this is M0, the bit the RBI controls).
- **Money stock, M = CU + D** — currency **plus** deposits.

Divide one by the other and tidy up (divide top and bottom by D):

**Modified money multiplier = (CU/D + 1) ÷ (CU/D + R)**, so that **M = [(CU/D + 1) ÷ (CU/D + R)] × H.**

Here **CU/D** is the **currency–deposit ratio**. Plug in the class numbers — CU/D = 0.2 and R = 0.1:

(0.2 + 1) ÷ (0.2 + 0.1) = 1.2 ÷ 0.3 = **4.**

The same reserve ratio that gave a multiplier of **10** with no leakage now gives only **4** once people hoard cash. And in the extreme — if everyone refuses to deposit — **there is no multiplier at all.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The central bank can control **R (reserve ratio)** and the components of **high-powered money (CU and reserves)** — but it **cannot control CU/D**, the public's cash-holding habit. CU/D is **social behaviour and trust** ("herd mentality"), not a policy lever. So: **high-powered money × modified multiplier = money supply**, and the weak link is always public trust.
:::

:::note[Good to Know]
The professor recalled Arthur Hailey's novel *The Moneychangers*: four customers found one branch had no cash, and within hours a queue formed demanding deposits back — a **run on the bank**. She noted it's happened recently too, with several **cooperative banks** failing, which is why nervous depositors flee to a "safe" bank like **SBI**. When CU/D spikes, the money machine seizes up.
:::

## The Central Bank's Toolkit

Beyond nudging the reserve ratio, the RBI has a standard set of instruments:

| Tool | What it is | Effect / use |
|---|---|---|
| **CRR** (Cash Reserve Ratio) | % of deposits held as **cash** with the RBI | Mandatory; moves the multiplier (≈ 4% at class time) |
| **SLR** (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) | % of deposits held in **government securities** | Risk-free "gilt" cushion (≈ 18% at class time) |
| **OMO** (Open Market Operations) | RBI **buys/sells** government securities | **Buy = inject** money supply; **sell = suck out** (long-term) |
| **Repo / Reverse repo** | Sale of securities with a **repurchase** promise | **Short-term** money correction (e.g., ~3-day tenor) |
| **Bank rate** (discount rate) | RBI **lends** to banks; last resort | Mostly a **signal** for where interest rates should sit |

A student asked *why* SLR must be government securities. Because they are treated as **absolutely risk-free**:

> **The professor:** "Government securities are considered to be risk-free — guilt-edged, it's like gold."

On OMO, a buy **injects liquidity** (money supply up) and a sell **pulls money back**. One student spotted the deeper point: when the RBI *buys*, where does the payment come from?

> **The professor:** "The central bank has to **print that money** and give it — because the central bank has no deposits to lend from, unlike the commercial banks. Only the central bank can do this."

(The old **gold standard** that once backed this printing was removed long ago.) **Repo** = a commercial bank sells securities to the RBI with a promise to **repurchase** them — short-term cash. The **bank rate** is rarely activated; it mainly **signals** that banks should lend a touch higher or lower.
