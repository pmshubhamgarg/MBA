# Session 4: Why Falling Inflation Still Raises Your Bill

*To compare one year's output with another's, you must value it at prices — and the moment you pick a price, you have opened the door to the cost of living, the price index, and inflation. This session builds the ruler (CPI, WPI, GDP deflator), shows why "inflation is down" never means "prices are down," and ends on the wage–price spiral that becomes the stagflation no economy escapes.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **three price-level measures** — **CPI, WPI, GDP deflator**; **how to build a CPI** (basket → base-year weights → price relatives → weighted index); the CPI formula **CPI = Σ (weight × price relative)**; the base-weighted index **Σ(current price × base quantity) ÷ Σ(base price × base quantity) × 100**; **GDP deflator = Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP × 100**; **inflation = the rate of change of the price level** (not the level); **demand-pull vs cost-push**; **stagflation = stagnation + inflation**.

**Important (supporting):** Why we need a price index at all (standardising output); CPI vs WPI (retail vs wholesale, services, number of goods); the "typical consumer" and the **surveys** (NSSO, NSHIE/NCAER, CPHS/CMIE, CSO/MOSPI); CPI's **limitations** (fixed base-year weights, substitution bias, new goods, only domestic goods); month-on-month vs year-on-year; why **low inflation can be bad news**; money supply and the **repo rate** as the demand lever; India's roughly **4%** inflation guardrail.

**Context (colour for answers):** The **RBI Monetary Policy Committee** headline (growth ~6.5%, inflation ~3.1%); the **grocery-shop owner** who swears prices rose 50–100% while RBI reports 4–6%; India's inflation sliding from ~6%+ (Oct 2024) to ~1.6% (July 2025); the smartphone-vs-landline substitution; oil / Russian-oil and Reliance as a cost-push trigger; exchange rates left out for want of time.

## Quick Recap

The session opens on the back of the quiz and the last class. Previously the batch covered the **three ways to compute GDP** — the **product, income and expenditure methods** — and then the split between **nominal and real GDP**.

- **Nominal GDP** = output valued at **current** prices.
- **Real GDP** = output valued at **constant (base-year)** prices.

The gap between the two is caused by one thing: **price levels have changed.** So before we can trust any GDP comparison, we must learn to **measure the price level** — and the fallout of that measurement is **inflation** (and, alongside it, unemployment — the two recurring problems of every business cycle).

The professor anchors it to the evening's newspaper: the **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** headline, with its repo rate, a growth forecast near **6.5%** and inflation near **3.1%**.

> **The professor:** "For you and me it measures the cost of living. So we want to know the cost of living as of today."

She also sets the study attitude for a 10-session macro course where the vocabulary is new:

> **The professor:** "You have to do it like a drill — this means this, this means this — otherwise within 10 sessions if you have to get familiar with all the concepts is very hard."

## The Core Problem: You Can't Add Tons to Litres

Output arrives in different units — rice in kilos, cloth in metres, milk in litres, steel in tonnes. You cannot add them up directly. The fix is to **multiply each quantity by its price** and sum the **values**. That gives one standardised number for the economy's output.

But the instant you multiply by price, a question pops up: **which year's price?** Today's? Last year's? Ten years ago? The answer changes the total value — and therefore your read on whether the economy actually *grew* or just got *more expensive*. Separating a **real** change (more stuff) from a **price** change (same stuff, dearer) is the whole reason price indices exist.

---

## Three Rulers for the Price Level

There are three standard ways to measure the price level. Learn what each one "faces."

| Measure | Whose prices? | Covers | Frequency (as taught) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CPI** (Consumer Price Index) | **Retail** prices the **consumer** pays | A typical consumer's basket of goods (and services) | Monthly |
| **WPI** (Wholesale Price Index) | **Wholesale** prices the **producer** pays | More goods than CPI, **goods only, no services** | Roughly six-monthly |
| **GDP deflator** | Prices across the **whole economy** | **All** final goods and services produced | Yearly (with a lag) |

**Who actually computes these?** Not the RBI. The **CSO (Central Statistical Organisation)** under **MOSPI (Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation)** builds the data; it sits in the government's public domain, so the RBI, you or I can all use it.

> **The professor:** "CPI is more a cost-of-living index. But for macroeconomic purposes, for macro policy makers, they would rather take the GDP deflator."

---

## Who Is the "Typical Consumer"?

CPI measures the cost of goods bought by a **typical** consumer — the professor writes "typical" in red on purpose, because you cannot track every individual. Who counts as typical? Usually a **middle-income** household (not the deprived low-income, not the high-income). You discover their basket through **surveys**:

- **NSSO** — National Sample Survey Organisation; reaches even remote villages with consumption and social-demographic data.
- **NSHIE** — National Survey of Household Income and Expenditure (run by **NCAER**); a humongous exercise done roughly **once in 10 years** (last around 2011–12).
- **CPHS** — Consumer Pyramid Household Survey by **CMIE**; newer, very authentic, surveys the **same** households repeatedly, **thrice a year**.

What is a "necessity" versus a "luxury" depends on **context** — a refrigerator or air-cooler is a luxury here but a necessity in the US. That is also why India keeps **separate** indices (industrial-workers, agricultural-workers, urban, rural): urban and rural prices and baskets differ sharply, so you pick the index that matches your purpose. For general inflation, the default is **CPI**.

> **The professor:** "Depending on the purpose for which you're using the data, you will have to be very choosy about which index number you're getting into."

## Building a CPI by Hand

This is the computational heart of the session and a classic exam ask. The professor walks a **hypothetical 1991 base year** step by step.

**Step 1 — Fix the base-year basket, quantities and (retail) prices, then get each item's base-year spend** (quantity × price):

| Item | Base-year quantity | Base-year price | Base-year spend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rice | 15 kg | ₹10 / kg | ₹150 |
| Wheat | 10 kg | ₹8 / kg | ₹80 |
| Milk | 40 L | ₹5 / L | ₹200 |
| Cloth | 10 m | ₹8 / m | ₹80 |
| House | 2 rooms | ₹200 / room | ₹400 |
| **Total** | | | **₹910** |

**Step 2 — Compute each item's base-year weight** = base-year spend ÷ total spend (keep it a decimal, not a percentage):

- Rice = 150 ÷ 910 ≈ **0.16**, Wheat = 80 ÷ 910 ≈ **0.09**, Milk = 200 ÷ 910 ≈ **0.22**, Cloth = 80 ÷ 910 ≈ **0.09**, House = 400 ÷ 910 ≈ **0.44** (they sum to ~1.00).

**The crucial assumption:** these base-year weights are held **constant** for the whole period of the index. Only prices are allowed to move.

**Step 3 — Bring in current-year prices and compute each price relative** = (current price ÷ base price) × 100:

| Item | Current price | Price relative | Weight × price relative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rice | ₹15 | 150 | 0.16 × 150 = 24.0 |
| Wheat | ₹10 | 125 | 0.09 × 125 = 11.25 |
| Milk | ₹7 | 140 | 0.22 × 140 = 30.8 |
| Cloth | ₹10 | 125 | 0.09 × 125 = 11.25 |
| House | ₹200 | 100 | 0.44 × 100 = 44.0 |

**Step 4 — Add the last column.** CPI ≈ **121** against a base of 100 — i.e., the typical cost of living is about **21% higher** than in the base year.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Memorise the one-line formula and the four steps:

**CPI = Σ (weightᵢ × price relativeᵢ)** — where weightᵢ is the base-year weight and price relativeᵢ = (current priceᵢ ÷ base priceᵢ) × 100.

Steps: **(1)** fix the base-year basket, quantities and retail prices → base-year spend; **(2)** weight = item spend ÷ total spend (held fixed); **(3)** price relative per item; **(4)** weighted sum. If a question hands you a basket, this is the exact drill that earns full marks.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Fix the basket, float the prices"]
A CPI **freezes the quantities** of the base year and lets only **prices** change. Four moves: **B**asket → **W**eights → **R**elatives → **W**eighted sum.
:::

---

## The Shortcut Formula (and What It Quietly Hides)

The same CPI can be written as **current-year spend ÷ base-year spend × 100**, but with the **base-year quantities kept throughout**:

**CPI = Σ(current price × base quantity) ÷ Σ(base price × base quantity) × 100.**

Numerically it lands on the same answer — but it **hides the weights**. The long method shows each item's weight openly, so you can judge whether the basket is still representative. The shortcut gives the number while quietly burying that information.

:::note[Good to Know]
In textbooks this base-year-weighted price index is the **Laspeyres index** (current prices valued at fixed base-year quantities). The professor did not name it, but the formula she built is exactly that — useful to recognise if the exam uses the label.
:::

---

## Where CPI Goes Wrong

Because the basket is frozen, CPI carries real limitations — each one is a fair short-note question:

1. **Fixed base-year weights / substitution bias.** When a cheaper substitute appears, shoppers switch, but CPI keeps valuing the old item — so CPI can **overestimate** the true cost of living.
2. **Introduction of new goods.** A smartphone replacing a landline will not enter the index if the landline is the base-basket item. The basket stays frozen.
3. **Only goods vs services.** **WPI is goods-only, no services.** On CPI the professor checked live and corrected herself:

> **The professor:** "I suddenly got on Google as well. CPI includes services. It is WPI that does not include services — and RBI uses the CPI indicator for tracking."

4. **Only domestically produced goods.** Both CPI and WPI exclude imports.
5. **A "typical" basket is subjective** — your basket differs from your neighbour's, and only a limited set of goods is tracked.

The fix for a stale basket is to **recompute CPI with a newer base year** — but because an NSSO-scale survey is so heavy, that happens only about **once a decade**.

> **The professor:** "Keep the basket fixed is the motto of CPI."

A sharp student (a grocery-shop owner) pushed back: *post-COVID, dal and vegetable prices shot up 50–100%, yet RBI reported 4–6% inflation — what's left out?* The professor's answer: nothing is secretly dropped. A spike in **some** items is **offset by others**, because CPI measures a **weighted, averaged basket**, not any single angry price.

## The GDP Deflator: The Whole-Economy Ruler

The third measure fixes CPI's biggest flaw. The **GDP deflator**:

**GDP deflator = Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP × 100**, where

- **Nominal GDP** = current-year quantities × current-year prices, and
- **Real GDP** = current-year quantities × base-year prices.

So, in index form: **Σ(current price × current quantity) ÷ Σ(base price × current quantity) × 100.** The difference from CPI is the quantity: the deflator uses **current-year quantities** (which change every year), while CPI is stuck on **base-year quantities**. That makes the deflator **comprehensive** — all final goods *and* services, imports included — which is why macro policymakers prefer it.

Its **drawback**: current-year quantities are only known at **year-end**, so the deflator arrives with roughly a **one-year lag** (India publishes **provisional → revised → actual** estimates in sequence).

| | **CPI** | **GDP deflator** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Consumer-centric (cost of living) | Whole economy |
| Coverage | Select basket of goods and services | All final goods and services |
| Quantities (weights) | **Base-year**, fixed | **Current-year**, moving |
| Timeliness | Monthly, fast | Yearly, one-year lag |
| Best for | Household burden, dearness relief | Macro studies, economy-wide inflation |

:::tip[Important]
Pick the ruler for the **job**, and then **do not switch** indices midway through a study. CPI answers "how is the common person's cost of living moving, this month?" The GDP deflator answers "how are prices across the entire economy moving?" Both are legitimate; using the wrong one — or mixing them — is the mistake.
:::

## Inflation Is a Speed, Not a Distance

A price **index** is a level. **Inflation is the rate at which that level changes:**

**Inflation (year 2) = (index in year 2 − index in year 1) ÷ index in year 1 × 100** — usable with CPI or the GDP deflator.

Here is the trap that separates a manager from the "common man." If inflation falls from **6% to 5%**, prices have **still gone up** — just by 5% instead of 6%. Falling inflation does **not** mean falling prices (that would be **deflation**, i.e. negative inflation).

> **The professor:** "The finance minister is saying the inflation has gone down, but I see prices not going down at all… that's what will distinguish a common man from you."

You can read it **month-on-month** or **year-on-year** (point-to-point, e.g. 10 Aug 2024 to 10 Aug 2025). India's inflation slid from **~6%+ (Oct 2024)** down to about **1.6% (July 2025)**.

But low inflation is **not automatically good news.** If prices are barely rising because **demand has collapsed**, that can signal a **looming recession** or even deflation. Never read the inflation number in isolation — gather other indicators first.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Inflation = rate of change of the price level, not the level.** Prices can keep rising while inflation falls. Inflation down ≠ prices down; only **negative** inflation (deflation) means prices actually fall. And an unusually **low** inflation print may be hiding **weak demand** — a warning, not a celebration.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Inflation is the climb-rate, not the bill"]
The index is how high the bill has climbed; inflation is **how fast** it is still climbing. A slower climb is still a climb.
:::

## What Drives Inflation: Demand-Pull vs Cost-Push

To explain *why* prices rise, the professor uses the macro cousins of the micro demand–supply curves: **aggregate demand (AD)** and **aggregate supply (AS)**.

| Type | What moves | Effect on output | Effect on prices | Manageable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Demand-pull** | AD shifts **up** | Output **rises** | Prices rise a little | **Yes** — policy can cool demand |
| **Cost-push** | AS shifts **inward** | Output **falls** | Prices rise sharply | **Hard** — the dangerous one |

- **Demand-pull:** demand increases, so firms produce more; output goes up and prices rise only modestly. If the AS curve is **flatter**, you get even more output for even less price rise — the happy case.
- **Cost-push:** the **cost of production** rises, so AS shrinks (shifts left). Output falls *and* prices rise. The chief "man-made" culprit is **wages** — unions bargain up, firms concede, costs climb. Crucial inputs like **oil** (and imported inflation in a borderless trade world) do the same. The professor's live example: if Russian oil becomes unavailable and **Reliance's** production costs jump, that trickles into higher prices everywhere, pushing AS inward.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Demand-pull = AD up → more output, mild price rise (good, manageable). Cost-push = AS inward → less output, sharp price rise (bad, hard to manage).** Draw the AD–AS diagram if allowed: a steeper inward AS shift gives *less* output and *more* price — the set-up for stagflation.
:::

---

## Stagflation: The Spiral No Economy Escapes

Push the cost-push case harder and the AS curve turns **steep and inward**: output keeps shrinking while prices keep climbing. That combination — **stagnation + inflation = stagflation** — is every economy's nightmare.

It becomes a **vicious wage–price spiral**: wages rise → prices rise → unions demand still-higher wages → prices rise again, as aggregate output grinds down. Once in, you are "caught in the spiral," so countries try to **prevent** it — planning years ahead, incentivising production and productivity.

**Stagflation vs hyperinflation:** hyperinflation is **only** runaway inflation (output may even hold or rise — think Argentina, Zimbabwe); stagflation is inflation **plus** falling output, which is why policymakers watch it far more closely. Hyperinflation is unsustainable and can eventually slide into stagflation.

> **The professor:** "No economy wants a stagflationary situation, because once you get into it you get caught in the spiral… so they would rather prevent stagflation."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "STAG-flation = STAGnation + inflATION"]
Prices up **and** output stuck/falling. Hyperinflation is prices-only; stagflation adds the stalled engine.
:::

## The Policy Lever: Money Supply and the Repo Rate

Why does demand rise in the first place? Because **money supply** rises. So to cool **demand-pull** inflation, the authority **reduces money supply** — and its indirect tool is the **repo rate**.

- **Raise** the repo rate → borrowing is costlier → less lending → money supply tightens → demand (and prices) ease.
- **Lower** the repo rate → borrowing is cheaper → more money and spending → demand lifts.

Policymakers can also tweak the five spending components of demand — **C + I + G + (X − M)** — to steer it. Demand-pull is therefore **manageable**; **cost-push is not**, which is why central banks dread that trend.

India keeps inflation around a **4%** guardrail — fine up to about 4%, but **dangerous** beyond (5–6–7%), because in that zone extra demand just keeps inflating prices. The professor also flagged a real puzzle: through **June–July** the RBI cut rates, yet borrowing stayed weak and inflation still fell — a sign of **depressed demand**, proof again that you cannot read inflation alone. And **developing** economies run a **higher** tolerable inflation rate than **developed** ones (a narrow base versus a wide base).

:::note[Good to Know]
**Exchange rates (the X − M channel)** are a fascinating part of macro, but the professor flagged they are **out of scope** here — a 10-session module simply has no room. Do not force them into an answer; note the boundary.
:::
