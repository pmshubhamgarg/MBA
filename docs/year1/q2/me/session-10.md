# Session 10: How ₹100 Becomes ₹400 — and Why Inflation Is Always Monetary

*The final class ties the whole course together: banks manufacture money from a sliver of your deposit, the RBI works the taps with CRR, OMO and repo, and in the end inflation is just too much money chasing too few goods.*

## Quick Recap

The professor opened the last class with a recap of the money module so far:

- **Money evolved** from **commodity money** (intrinsic value) to **fiat money** (no intrinsic value, backed only by a government decree, and declared **legal tender**). With fiat money came the **central bank** and the modern monetary system.
- **Banks began as passive safe-keepers.** A depositor who parks money gets a **claim**; that claim changes hands many times (its **velocity**), but **no fresh money is created** — it is the same claim going round until the last holder withdraws cash.
- To control bank lending, the central bank imposed the **fractional-reserve system**: a bank must hold a fraction of deposits as **required reserves** and may lend the rest.
- Money was defined as a **continuum of liquidity — M1, M2, M3, M4** — plus **M0**, the monetary base the RBI fully controls.

> **The professor:** "Today, the last class of mine. It won't be your last."

## From Safe-Keepers to Money-Makers: Where the Multiplier Is Born

A bank sitting on everyone's deposits notices two things: not all depositors withdraw at once, and idle cash earns nothing. **Greed** does the rest — why not lend the spare money at interest and pocket the spread?

> **The professor:** "There is a tendency, a sense of greed — saying, can I not lend and make money with it?"

That instinct is exactly what **creates** money. When the bank lends, the borrower spends, the money is re-deposited, and the next bank lends again. The central bank reins in the risk by fixing **required reserves** — the minimum fraction that can never be lent out.

**Why it matters:** money creation is not the printing press alone. **Commercial banks multiply** the base money through lending — which is why the reserve ratio is such a powerful lever.

## The Money-Stock Ladder: M1 to M4, and the Mighty M0

Money comes in grades of **liquidity** — how fast it can be spent.

| Measure | What it adds | Liquidity |
|---|---|---|
| **M1** | Currency with the public + demand & checkable deposits | **Most liquid** |
| **M2** | M1 + post-office savings deposits | High |
| **M3** | M1 + time (fixed) deposits | Lower |
| **M4** | M3 + total post-office savings deposits | **Least liquid** |
| **M0** | Currency in circulation + banks' and government deposits with the RBI | **High-powered money** |

**Demand deposit vs time deposit:** a **demand deposit** is checkable and can be turned into cash immediately (so it carries almost no interest, roughly 0.1–2%); a **time deposit** (FD) is locked for a term and pays interest, but breaking it early costs a **penalty**.

**The banker's favourites are M1 and M3.** M2 "strays" into post-office savings, and the post office has other, non-monetary primary functions — so monetary policy concentrates on the banking system.

**M0 is special.** Over currency in circulation and the deposits parked with it, the RBI has **total control** — it can withdraw or enhance them at will. That is why M0 is called **high-powered money**, the **monetary base**, or **reserve money** — the word *potential* is the key: this money has the potential to become deposits throughout the system.

:::note[Good to Know]
A student asked whether UPI and digital wallets change these equations. The professor's take: **UPI is linked to your bank account**, so that money is still bank money and the system holds. Standalone wallets are murkier, and whether digital money is "legal tender" is a separate question — one she flagged as worth checking rather than answering on the spot.
:::

## The Money Multiplier: How ₹100 Becomes ₹1,000 (or Just ₹400)

This is the "fascinating" heart of the session. Drop **₹100** of high-powered money into the system (Friedman's famous image: a **helicopter** scatters cash and someone banks it).

**The simple multiplier.** If the required reserve ratio is `r`, the money multiplier is `1/r`.
- `r = 0.10` → multiplier **10** → ₹100 can become **₹1,000**.
- `r = 0.05` → multiplier **20** → money supply doubles again.

**The realistic twist — currency drain.** People don't bank every rupee; they keep some as cash. Cash held at home **doesn't go into money creation**, so the multiplier **shrinks**. The more people hold as currency, the smaller the multiplier.

**The modified (comprehensive) multiplier** adds the currency-to-deposit ratio `CU/D`:

> **Money multiplier = (CU/D + 1) / (CU/D + r)**

Worked example from class — `CU/D = 0.2`, `r = 0.1`:
`(0.2 + 1) / (0.2 + 0.1) = 1.2 / 0.3 = **4**`.

So **₹100** of high-powered money now supports only **₹400** of money supply — far less than the ideal ₹1,000.

> **Money supply (M) = modified multiplier × high-powered money (H)**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Learn both forms. **Simple: `1/r`. Realistic: `(CU/D + 1) / (CU/D + r)`.** Then **M = multiplier × H**. The `CU/D` ratio is **behavioural** — set by how much the public trusts banks and fears the future — so the central bank **cannot legislate it**. The RBI can only pull **H (= CU + R)** or the reserve ratio **r**; it cannot touch `CU/D`. If a question asks why money supply undershoots, the answer is almost always **currency drain** raising `CU/D`.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "1/r is the dream, CU/D is the reality"]
The textbook multiplier `1/r` assumes everyone banks everything. The moment people keep cash, `CU/D` rises and the multiplier falls. **The reservoir is H; the multiplier is the pump; your cash-in-pocket is the leak.**
:::

## The RBI's Toolkit: CRR, SLR, OMO, Repo and the Bank Rate

Since the RBI can only move **H** or **r**, here are the levers it actually pulls.

| Tool | What it does | Lasting effect | Collateral? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CRR** (cash reserve ratio) | Minimum cash fraction of deposits held as reserves | Structural | — |
| **SLR** (statutory liquidity ratio) | Minimum fraction held as government securities ("**gilt**", as safe as gold) | Structural | — |
| **OMO** (open market operations) | RBI **buys/sells** govt securities outright — buying releases cash, selling sucks it in | **Permanent** (months) | **No** |
| **Repo / Reverse repo** | **Commercial bank** sells (or buys) govt securities to/from the RBI, to **repurchase in ~15 days** | **Temporary** | **Yes** |
| **Bank rate** (discount rate abroad) | RBI's **last-resort** lending rate to cash-short banks | Signal | — |

A few sharp points the professor stressed:
- **Excess reserves.** Banks may hold **more** than the required minimum if the environment feels unsafe to lend into. The central bank can set a **minimum, never a maximum**.
- **SLR earns a little.** Gilt gives a minimal but **risk-free** return, so SLR is not quite the same as dead cash.
- **Real 2025 OMO.** Early 2025 the RBI made open-market **purchases of ₹60,000 crore**; in March, another **₹1 lakh crore** — both **releasing money** to boost liquidity and spending.
- **Repo vs OMO.** Repo is **bank-initiated, temporary, collateralised**; OMO is **RBI-initiated, permanent, uncollateralised**.
- **The bank rate as a signal.** Its February cut from **6.75% to 6.50%** (and a later 25-basis-point cut) is read as a **message** about the coming policy stance.

:::tip[Important]
Bank rate, repo and OMO are all used as **signals** to the banking system. When the RBI eases, it is telling banks: *we want money supply to rise, keep it loose, not tight.* That deliberate loosening is **quantitative easing** — easing money into the system so people can transact and spend.
:::

## Why We Demand Money: Transactions, Precaution, and the Speculator's Trap

Keynes said we hold money for **three** reasons:

| Motive | Driven by | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Transactions** | Income | Higher income → hold more |
| **Precautionary** (contingency) | Income | Higher income → hold more |
| **Speculative** | Interest rate | Higher rate → hold **less** money (inverse) |

Transactions and precautionary demand are simply a function of **income** — common-sensical. The subtle one is **speculative demand**, which turns on **interest rates and bond prices**.

**The bond logic.** A bond's **coupon rate is fixed** by the issuer. If the RBI **raises** interest rates, new savings look better than the old bond, so to sell that bond its **price must fall**. Hence the iron rule: **interest rates and bond prices are inversely related.**

- **Rates high** → speculators reason rates can only **fall** later → bond prices will **rise** → so they **lock their money in bonds now** → speculative demand for money is **low**.
- **Rates low** → rates can only **rise** later → bond prices will **fall** → so they **hoard idle cash**, waiting to buy cheap bonds → speculative demand for money becomes **huge, even infinite**.

That bottom extreme is Keynes's **liquidity trap**: pump in money and people simply **sit on it**, so policy loses its grip.

> **The professor:** "All the money is kept as idle money — it's trapped into it."

**Why it matters:** stack the three motives and you get the **downward-sloping total demand for money** — the backbone of the money-market diagram below.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "High rates? Hoard bonds. Low rates? Hoard cash."]
At high interest rates the smart money piles into bonds; at rock-bottom rates it clutches cash and waits — the **liquidity trap**. Speculative money demand always slopes **downward**.
:::

## The Value of Money and the Equilibrium That Sets Prices

Put **value of money (1/P)** on the y-axis and the **quantity of money** on the x-axis. Value of money and the **price level are inversely related** — when prices climb, each rupee buys less.

- **Money supply** is a **vertical line** — it is **policy-driven** by the RBI, not a function of the value of money.
- **Money demand** slopes **downward** — a high value of money means low prices, so you need **less** money to transact.
- Where they cross is the **equilibrium value of money.**

Now shift the supply line **right** (the RBI increases money supply): the equilibrium **value of money falls**, which means **prices rise**.

> **The professor:** "When value of money comes down, prices go up. Very intuitive, right?"

**The policy choice.** Pushing money supply up risks inflation — so is it good or bad? It depends on where inflation already sits:
- If inflation is **well within the targeted band**, the RBI can run an **expansionary** policy to revive weak investment and spending.
- If inflation **breaches the band**, controlling it becomes the top priority, even at the cost of growth — a **restrictive** policy.

The class consensus for India *today*: inflation is within range and private investment is weak, so **expansionary monetary policy** is justified.

## "Inflation Is a Monetary Phenomenon": The Quantity Theory

The session's punchline comes from **Milton Friedman** and the **monetarists** — the **quantity theory of money**:

> **MV = PY**

where **M** is the money stock, **V** its **velocity** (how many times it circulates), **P** the price level and **Y** real output. `PY` is therefore **nominal GDP** — the number of transactions in money terms.

You rarely observe velocity directly, so you **back it out**: `V = PY / M`. A velocity that is running unusually high signals a **liquidity crunch** — the same money is being stretched over too many transactions.

**The key empirical crutch:** over a few years, **V is roughly constant**, and in the **short run Y (real output) is also constant**. If V and Y don't move, then any change in **M flows straight into P** — prices.

Convert the identity into **growth rates** and a product becomes a sum:

> money-growth + velocity-growth = inflation + real-GDP-growth

Rearranged: **inflation = money-growth + velocity-growth − real-GDP-growth.** With velocity and real GDP steady in the short run, this collapses to:

> **Inflation rate ≈ growth rate of money supply.**

> **The professor:** "Inflation is a monetary phenomenon."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Be able to write **MV = PY**, explain each term, derive **V = PY/M**, and show that with **V and Y constant**, inflation ≈ **money-supply growth**. Pair it with the contrast: **monetarists (Friedman)** work in **normal** times via money supply; **fiscalists (Keynes)** shine in a **recession** via fiscal policy and the income multiplier. Remember too that **money supply and interest rates are two sides of one coin** — more money supply pushes interest rates down.
:::

**But why does inflation sometimes spiral?** A student pushed on Venezuela and wartime Germany. The professor's answer: money supply must be **converted into goods**. If production can't keep pace — because of a **supply lag**, or because spending is swallowed by **defence** — then more money simply chases the same goods and prices explode.

> **The professor:** "During the Second World War ... they were carrying money in sacks. The value of that money was that low because there were no goods available."

And the painful cure: if inflation blows past the band, you **control it at all costs** — let GDP fall, let unemployment rise, ride out the recession, and start again.
