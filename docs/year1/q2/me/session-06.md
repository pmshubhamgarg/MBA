# Session 6: One Identity, Two Deficits — How a Budget Hole Drags a Trade Hole Behind It

*The professor starts from one line every macro student should know in their sleep — Y = C + I + G + X - M — squeezes out the rule that can never break (savings = investment), and shows how a government that overspends quietly forces the whole country to over-import. That handcuff is the twin deficit.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The macro identity **Y = C + I + G + X - M**; the **savings–investment identity S = I**; breaking savings into **S private + S government + S row**; the formulas **S private = Y - T - C**, **S government = T - G**, **S row = M - X**; the **twin deficit** (a fiscal deficit forcing a current account deficit); the **sign rules** (S government: plus good / minus bad; S row: plus = danger / minus = safe).

**Important (supporting):** The **four cases** of the identity; **deficit financing**; the US / China / India numbers and what S row says about each; **current account vs capital account vs reserves**; why a current account deficit is fine only if **sustainable**; the growth cost of cutting I; the FRBM fiscal-deficit cap.

**Context (colour for answers):** The suspicious 7.5% GDP forecast resting on a 1% inflation guess; India "practically closed" before 1991; the Robinson Crusoe economy; rupee depreciation and inelastic oil/machinery imports; US 50% tariffs pushing garment buyers to Bangladesh; PMs "begging" for FDI; China as the biggest investor in US assets.
:::

## Quick Recap: The Three Axes of National Income

The professor opened by tying off last session's accounting. Total output can be sliced on **three axes**, and every "GDP vs GNP vs NNP" variant is just one of these differences:

| Axis | The two sides | What separates them |
|---|---|---|
| **Domestic vs National** | GDP vs GNP | Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) |
| **Market price vs Factor cost** | at market price vs at factor cost | Indirect taxes minus subsidies |
| **Gross vs Net** | Gross vs Net | Depreciation |

Her punchline: if you **assume all three differences away** and take a simple **closed economy**, then GDP = GNP = NNP = National Income — they all collapse into one number. **NNP at factor cost is National Income.** Remember the three axes and you can rebuild every variant.

That clears the runway for the one identity the rest of the evening lives on:

> **The professor:** "The basic macroeconomic identity is Y = C + I + G + X - M… you should be able to say that in your sleep as well."

(X - M, net exports, is also written **NX**.)

---

## The One Rule That Can't Break: Savings = Investment

Start with the simplest world — a **closed economy**, shut off from trade. No X, no M, no NFIA, no depreciation.

So **Y = C + I + G**. Move things around: **Y - C - G = I**. But income left over after you consume (C) and after the government spends (G) is exactly what the nation **saves**. So **S = I**.

That little equation is doing heavy lifting: S = I is just another way of saying **the economy is in equilibrium**. Everything that is produced is either consumed or invested; everything not consumed must be saved; and saving has to finance investment.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**S = I is THE identity of this session.** Two equivalent ways to state equilibrium: **Y = C + I + G + X - M**, or **S = I**. Whenever the question hints that "something has to give" in the economy, your first move is to write S = I and ask which piece of savings is adjusting.
:::

---

## Blowing Up Savings: Private, Government, and the Rest of the World

Here is the move the whole session turns on. Total savings isn't one blob — the professor "blows it up" piece by piece.

**Step 1 — split into private and government.** Take S = I and add and subtract taxes (T). Mathematically it changes nothing, but it carves savings into two savers:

- **Private savings = Y - T - C** (your income, minus taxes, minus what you consume).
- **Government savings = T - G** (tax revenue minus government spending).

So in a closed economy, **S private + S government = I**.

**Step 2 — open the economy.** Bring in the **rest of the world (ROW)**. Now Y = C + I + G + X - M. Rearranging pulls out a third saver:

- **S row = M - X** — "foreign savings," or the savings of the external sector.

> **The professor:** "So it's actually savings getting blown up bit by bit. You have private savings, then you expanded to include the government… then you included the rest of the world."

| Saver | Formula | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **S private** | Y - T - C | What households keep after tax and consumption |
| **S government** | T - G | The budget balance (surplus if plus, deficit if minus) |
| **S row** | M - X | The external sector's savings — foreign money we draw in |

**The master identity:** **S private + S government + S row = I.**

Your textbook reaches the same place in one jump (what is left after C splits into savings and taxes, so S + T = I + G + X - M, which rearranges to S + (T - G) + (M - X) = I). Same destination, faster route.

> **The professor:** "Don't get too zapped when you see this equation… it's the same as ours, just that we have done it gradually to reach the final stage."

:::note[Good to Know — The Robinson Crusoe economy]
A **closed economy** is "shut off entirely from external trade — you're operating like a Robinson Crusoe kind of an economy." Useful aside: **before 1991 India was not truly closed** — it exported, but had such heavy tariff barriers on imports that it was "practically closed." The professor called that a "very dubious way of saying we are a closed economy," right before the 1991 external crisis forced it open.
:::

## Four Cases: Playing With the Identity (I = 100)

Because **S = I must always hold**, the professor fixed investment at I = 100 and walked through every combination of the budget (T - G) and trade (M - X).

| Case | Budget (T - G) | Trade (M - X) | What happens | Real? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Balanced (0) | Balanced (0) | S private alone equals I | Never in real life |
| **2** | Not balanced | Balanced (0) | If T above G, government saves; if T below G, a fiscal deficit must be plugged by **deficit financing** / higher private savings | Rare |
| **3** | Balanced (0) | Not balanced | Private + foreign savings adjust to hold I | Very rare |
| **4** | Not balanced | Not balanced | Both the budget and trade are off at once | **The real world** |

**Case 2 is the teaching moment.** If the government runs a deficit (T below G, say government savings of -20), then to keep I at 100 the **private sector has to save more** (up to 120) — or the gap is filled by **deficit financing**. Savings can never simply vanish; if one saver goes negative, another must rise, or the identity breaks.

> **The professor:** "Government in the short run can bail out by printing money, getting huge deficits… but can that be sustained over a period of time is a big question."

**Case 4 — not balanced on either axis — is where India, the US and China actually live.** That is what the rest of the class is about.

## Reading the Real Numbers: US, China, India

The professor put real figures on the board (2007 data from her table) and read each country through **S row = M - X**.

| Country | X - M | S row (= M - X) | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **US** | about -USD 731 billion | large **positive** | Huge **current account deficit**; imports far exceed exports |
| **China** | large positive | large **negative** (about -USD 372 billion) | Big **current account surplus**; exports its savings abroad |
| **India** | about -USD 18.5 billion | **positive** (about +USD 18.5 billion) | **Current account deficit**; must pull in foreign money |

> **The professor:** "US, we all know, has huge current account deficit. It's like saying for us S row is hugely positive."

So how does the US survive a permanent current account deficit? It plugs the hole on the **capital account** — foreign savings pour in because the dollar is powerful and the economy is seen as stable. **China runs the mirror image:** a giant trade surplus, so it ships its savings out, and it is one of the **largest investors in US financial assets**. India has the same current account deficit problem but not the same magnetism — which is why, in the professor's blunt image, prime ministers and finance ministers keep flying abroad:

> **The professor:** "…going time and again to the other countries and begging — literally begging them — come and invest, come and invest."

The goal of all that courting of **FDI and FII** is simply to make the identity balance: foreign money in (a negative entry on the capital side) offsets the positive S row from the trade deficit.

## The Sign Rules You Must Memorise

This is the trap the professor repeated three times, because the two "balances" read in **opposite** directions.

| Balance | Formula | Plus (positive) means | Minus (negative) means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal balance** (S government) | T - G | **Surplus — good** | **Deficit — dangerous** |
| **Trade / current account balance** (S row) | M - X | **Current account deficit — DANGER** | **Surplus — safe** |

> **The professor:** "In S government, positive is fine, negative is dangerous — that is straightforward. This is the opposite: if it's positive, it's dangerous; if it's negative, it's fine."

Reason: a positive **M - X** means imports beat exports — you are buying more than you sell, so you owe the world. That is a **current account deficit**, and it is only comfortable if someone keeps lending you the difference.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Government plus is good; World plus is danger"]
For **S government (T - G)**, a **plus** is a healthy surplus. For **S row (M - X)**, the sign **flips**: a **plus** means you are over-importing (current account deficit = danger), a **minus** means a safe surplus. Two balances, mirror-image signs.
:::

:::tip[Important — India's fiscal reality]
India has **not run a fiscal surplus since roughly 1970** — it has only ever "fought it out with the fiscal deficit." That is why there is a **Fiscal Responsibility (FRBM) law** capping the deficit at around **3–4%** of GDP, after years when it ran into **double digits** (above 10%). Know the term **deficit financing** (how a T-below-G gap gets funded) and the FRBM cap for a one-line full-marks point.
:::

## The Twin Deficit Problem (the punchline)

Now stitch it together. Suppose the government runs a **fiscal deficit** (S government negative). To keep **I** from falling — and we must, because falling investment kills growth — the identity needs another saver to turn positive. Private savings can only be nudged so far. So the pressure lands on **S row**, which must go **positive**… and a positive S row is a **current account (trade) deficit**.

> **The professor:** "Government deficit has to be matched by a trade deficit… and that is what is called the twin deficit problem."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Twin deficit = a fiscal deficit AND a current account deficit occurring together, linked by S = I.** A budget hole (S government negative) forces a trade hole (S row positive) if you want investment — and therefore growth — to stay put. Why is it a *problem*? Because a current account deficit is only survivable while **foreign savings keep flowing in**. The US can rely on that; India cannot be sure, so for India the twin deficit is genuinely risky.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Two holes, one rope"]
A **budget hole** (fiscal deficit) and a **trade hole** (current account deficit) are tied together by **S = I**. Pull one open and, to protect investment, the other has to open too. That rope is the twin deficit.
:::

**But a deficit is not automatically a disaster.** The professor's balancing caveat:

> **The professor:** "A large trade or current account deficit is not an issue at all if it is sustainable."

Sustainable means the deficit is reliably financed by foreign savings that come in, earn a return, and lift future exports. The US clears that bar; China happily funds it. India has to keep **luring** that money, or face the hard choice below.

## The Escape Valves (and a Peek Beyond the Syllabus)

The professor flagged this part as **outside the exam syllabus** but worth understanding. The balance of payments has three layers:

- **Current account** — goods, services, and unilateral transfers.
- **Capital account** — financial assets: inflows and outflows (FDI, FII).
- **Official reserves** — the backstop (gold, and for now mostly the dollar).

If foreign capital does **not** come in to cover a current account deficit, the only way left is to **dig into reserves**: sell rupees to buy dollars. That raises the rupee supply, so the **rupee depreciates**. Depreciation *should* help exports (they get cheaper abroad) — but there is a catch:

- **India's imports are price-inelastic** — oil, plant and machinery; you must buy them regardless of the rupee.
- **India's exports (jewellery, garments) are taking a tariff hit** — the professor noted **US 50% tariffs**, with buyers threatening to "go to Bangladesh" instead.

So depreciation cannot easily rescue the trade balance. That leaves the **last resort** nobody wants:

> **The professor:** "The last resort is to bring investment down, which we would not like to do… because if investment comes down, growth is going to suffer."

That is the whole quandary: honour S = I by raising private savings, or luring foreign savings, or (reluctantly) cutting government spending — but **never** by letting I fall, because falling I means slower growth. And India is ambitious; it wants I to *rise*, which only tightens the squeeze.

:::note[Good to Know — Why the forecast looked "suspect"]
Before class, students flagged that India's **real GDP growth suddenly jumped to ~7.5–7.8%**, up from ~6.5% months earlier. The professor's read: that number leans on a forecast that **inflation falls to ~1%** (nominal GDP around 8%, so low inflation flatters real GDP). With inflation actually near **3–3.5%**, a drop to 1% is "suspect" — maybe even wishful, possibly to offset Trump-tariff worries. Her larger point: "**every day, macroeconomics challenges are being faced by economists themselves**" — which is exactly what makes the subject alive.
:::
