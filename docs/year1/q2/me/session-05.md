# Session 5: The Economy's Vital Signs — Why One Number Always Lies

*Inflation, jobs and the whole GDP family — the professor's toolkit for reading an economy without being fooled by a single headline figure.*

## Quick Recap

The professor opened with a one-breath recap of the previous class. **To compute inflation, there are two steps:** first compute the **price level**, then measure the **change** in that price level. A rising price level is inflation; a falling one is deflation. Everything this session builds on that: three ways to read prices, two engines that move them, and then the jobs side of the same economy.

---

## Three Gauges for One Question: "What Are Prices Doing?"

You cannot measure "the price level" with one thermometer. The class has three, and each answers a slightly different question.

- **CPI (Consumer Price Index)** — retail prices a *typical consumer* pays, for a **select basket**, using **base-year weights**. How do we know what a typical consumer buys? Through surveys: **NSHIE** (by NCAER), the **NSSO** round (very popular but run only once in ~10 years), and the newer **CPHS — Consumer Pyramids Household Survey** (by CMIE, run thrice a year on a large sample).
- **WPI (Wholesale Price Index)** — same method, but it tracks the **producer's wholesale basket**, which is much larger and goods-heavy. (CPI tracks retail prices; WPI tracks wholesale prices.)
- **GDP deflator** — the comprehensive one: **Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP × 100**, covering **all** goods and services with **current-year** quantities as weights.

**The shortcut vs the full formula.** CPI is weight × price-relative, summed over items. A quick shortcut is *current-year spend ÷ base-year spend × 100* — easy, but you lose the weights, so use it only when you just want the final number.

> **The professor:** "GDP deflator is used for macro policies. If you're concerned about the cost of living to the consumer, you use CPI; if you're concerned about the producer, WPI."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Learn the split cold: **CPI = retail/consumer, base-year weights; WPI = wholesale/producer, base-year weights; GDP deflator = all goods and services, current-year weights.** CPI/WPI are stuck on a fixed basket, so they suffer **substitution bias** (a shift to cheaper goods isn't captured) and miss **new goods**. The deflator fixes both — but only arrives with a **one-year lag**, because you must wait for current-year quantity data.
:::

| Gauge | What it measures | Weights (basket) | Best used for | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CPI** | Retail consumer prices, select basket | Base-year quantities | Cost of living for consumers | Substitution bias; misses new goods |
| **WPI** | Wholesale producer prices, larger basket | Base-year quantities | Impact on producers | Same fixed-basket rigidity |
| **GDP deflator** | All goods and services | Current-year quantities | Macro policy | Comes with a one-year lag |

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Retail, Wholesale, Whole-economy"]
**C**PI is the **C**onsumer at the shop counter, **W**PI is the **W**holesaler's gate, and the **deflator** is the **whole** economy. R-W-W.
:::

## Don't Trust a Falling-Inflation Headline

India's inflation has been trending down — but "down" can mean two opposite things. Inflation can be low because the economy is in a **healthy equilibrium**, or low because it is in **recession**. The number alone can't tell you which.

> **The professor:** "Don't go gung-ho over inflation data that's just showing a decline. You should incorporate many other features as well."

So read inflation *alongside* investment, growth, exports, consumer spending, even the **shipping industry** (a leading indicator — lots of cargo moving means business is picking up). This "never read one macro number in isolation" idea is the spine of the whole session.

:::tip[Important]
A subtle line examiners love: falling inflation means prices are **still rising, but at a decreasing rate** — not that prices are falling. Falling prices would be *deflation*, a different (and often worse) problem.
:::

## Two Engines of Inflation: Demand-Pull vs Cost-Push

Inflation is a **continuous** (not one-time) rise in the price level. It comes from one of two engines.

**Demand-pull** — aggregate demand runs ahead of supply. You move demand by pulling the four levers of **C + G + I + (X − N)**: private spending (via taxes), government spending, private investment (an investment-friendly market), and net exports. Push them up and output *and* prices rise — which is fine, says the professor, *as long as the price rise stays controllable*, because output is growing too. To cool an overheating economy, reverse the levers: raise taxes, cut government spending, raise interest rates.

**Cost-push** — the cost of production rises, so the supply curve shifts **inward**. Two sources: **internal** (wages rise → prices rise) and **external** (oil, rare earths and other raw materials, over which you have no control). When supply shifts in, **prices rise and output falls** — the nasty combination.

> **The professor:** "What's the way out? There is no way out — most of it is not under your control."

- **Live example:** **Trump's tariffs** are a cost-push shock. "Imagine a 50% tariff on oil" — the whole downstream economy feels it and inflation climbs.
- **Only real defence:** stop the supply curve from shifting in — offset cost pressure with **productivity gains**, and allow wage rises only when matched by productivity.

| | **Demand-pull** | **Cost-push** |
|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Aggregate demand rises (C, G, I, X − N) | Cost of production rises (wages, oil, raw materials) |
| **Curve that moves** | Demand shifts out | Supply (AS) shifts **inward** |
| **Output** | Rises with prices | **Falls** while prices rise |
| **Policy handle** | Cool demand: raise taxes, cut G, raise rates | Very little — costs are often external |
| **Worst case** | Overheating | **Stagflation** |

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Pull is money, Push is cost"]
**Demand-pull** = too much money *pulling* at goods. **Cost-push** = rising costs *pushing* prices up from behind. One lifts output with prices; the other crushes output as prices rise.
:::

## Stagflation: The Trap With No Clean Exit

When cost-push bites, you get **stagflation** — **prices rising while output stays stagnant or falls**. It's a trap: the only textbook cure for high prices is to crush demand, but supply is already shrinking, so cutting demand just deepens the slump.

- **History:** the **1973 oil embargo** (after the 1973 war) threw many economies into stagflation. India was hit less, because it relied less on the external sector then.
- **Reflex:** policymakers *pounce* on any early hint of stagflation. The class recalled the US hiking interest rates very hard (students put it near **24%**) to force demand right down and reset — a situation every country dreads.
- **COVID was different:** not cost-push but a **demand collapse** (depression-like), which is why governments stepped in to *prop demand up*.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Stagflation = inflation + stagnation together.** It breaks the usual playbook because demand-side tools can't fix a supply-side problem without causing a recession. If a case shows **prices up and output down at the same time**, name stagflation and point to a **cost-push / external supply shock** as the cause.
:::

## When Monetary Policy Runs Out: Keynes and Pump Priming

Monetary policy has exactly two moves — raise or lower interest rates. But if demand is dead, even **zero** interest rates find **no takers** for loans or investment. That's the wall where **fiscal policy** takes over.

Enter **Keynes' pump-priming theory** from the 1930s Depression. Private spending (C) and investment (I) refused to move because everyone was scared, so **government spending (G)** had to do the lifting.

> **The professor:** "Keynes said even if it means digging holes and filling them up, do that — give money to the people and let them increase the demand."

To **prime a pump** you first pour water in from outside to get it flowing; likewise, the government keeps **injecting** spending until incomes rise, demand returns, producers produce, and the economy restarts. Even "unproductive" spending is fine, because the capacity to produce already exists — what's missing is demand.

:::note[Good to Know]
This is where **fiscal policy earned its place** alongside monetary policy. Monetary policy can lead a horse to water (cheap money); only fiscal policy can make it drink (actual demand) when confidence has collapsed.
:::

## Unemployment: Easy Word, Messy Number

The economy's other vital sign is jobs — and the professor was blunt that the number is **shaky**. India's unemployment rate edged from **3.1% (2023) to 3.2% (2024)** — "driven largely by more women leaving the workforce" — and the class debated a 2025 figure around **5.2–5.6%**. Meanwhile GDP keeps rising. GDP up *and* unemployment up together is a signal that "something is wrong with the system."

**First, the population buckets.** Total population splits into **under-16**, **institutionalised** (people who, for health reasons, cannot work), and everyone else. Of the rest:

- **Labour force** = the **employed** + those **actively seeking** work (the unemployed).
- **Not in the labour force** = retirees, senior citizens, students aged 16–22 who aren't job-hunting, and anyone who has opted out — including many women.

**The two formulas:**

- **Unemployment rate = Unemployed ÷ Labour force × 100.**
- **Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) = Labour force ÷ Adult population × 100** (students and institutionalised people are left out).

**Why the number is "shoddy."** India's **informal sector** sits largely outside the system (roadside vendors, unregistered self-employed), and even the formal sector has contract and daily-wage labour. Worse, India's *definition* of employment is generous:

> **The professor:** "If you're employed in work — you may not earn an income, but you're employed — that brings the unemployment rate down and we'll all feel very happy. But are we? That's a big question."

A migrant who returns to the village and works unpaid on a relative's farm counts as "employed." Data mostly comes from **employment exchanges**, which only some states run well (Kerala does). So treat the headline as a rough estimate, not gospel.

:::tip[Important]
Know the **measurement traps** for a short-note answer: the **informal sector**, **contract/daily-wage** labour, the **"work without income counts as employed"** definition, and thin **employment-exchange** data. One neat twist: demonetisation may have *pulled* informal workers into the formal system (digital payments), nudging the *measured* employment rate up.
:::

## Four Types of Unemployment — and the One That Matters

Not every kind of joblessness worries a policymaker. Four types:

| Type | Cause | Inside the natural rate? | Policy worry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frictional** | Between jobs; switching employers | Yes | Low |
| **Seasonal** | Season-driven (e.g., summer heat) | Yes | Low |
| **Structural** | Technology change → need re-skilling | Yes | Medium (retrain) |
| **Cyclical** | Business-cycle demand swings | **No** | **High — the real target** |

The **natural rate of unemployment** is the normal level around which unemployment fluctuates. Crucially, it already *contains* frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment — these are "understandable" and self-correct over time. No economy is ever at zero unemployment; "full employment" still includes the natural rate.

> **The professor:** "If you're talking about unemployment as a problem for the economy, for the policymaker — he's talking about cyclical unemployment."

**Cyclical** unemployment — falling demand → less production → fewer jobs — sits **outside** the natural rate and is what policy must fix. Notice the big India shocks were all cyclical: the **2008–09 Global Financial Crisis**, **demonetisation (2016)**, **GST disruption (2017)** and **COVID (2020)** — each a jolt to the business cycle.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "FSS is Natural, C is the Culprit"]
**F**rictional, **S**easonal, **S**tructural all live *inside* the **N**atural rate and sort themselves out. **C**yclical is the **C**ulprit the policymaker has to chase.
:::

## Okun's Law and the "Jobless Growth" Exception

How much does cyclical unemployment cost in output? **Okun's Law** (empirical, "nothing sacrosanct about it"): for every **1 percentage point** that actual unemployment exceeds the natural rate, GDP falls about **2%** — a **−2% GDP gap**. More unemployment, less growth.

But lately the link can **break**: you can see **growth rising and unemployment rising at the same time**. That's **jobless growth**, driven by efficiency, **automation, robots and AI**. GDP climbs while layoffs make the news — and if the automation is *imported* rather than built at home, even the machine-building jobs don't appear locally.

:::note[Good to Know]
The professor flagged a related, "outside-the-syllabus" point on **currency depreciation**: it's *supposed* to lift exports, but India's exports are basic goods while its imports (oil, plant and machinery) are **inelastic** — you must buy them whether the rupee is strong or weak — so depreciation often just **raises the import bill** instead of boosting net exports.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "1 up, 2 down"]
Okun in four words: unemployment **1** point above natural → GDP about **2%** below potential. And when automation flips it, remember the odd couple: **jobless growth**.
:::

## The GDP Family: Same Idea, Different Accounting

The session closed by opening the macro-measurement box. GDP is one way to size the economy, but it has close cousins — GNP, NDP, NNP — that are **identical in concept** and differ only by **accounting adjustments**.

Start with the headline pair:

- **GDP** = everything produced **within the geographical boundary**, by whoever produces it.
- **GNP** = output that belongs to the **nation's people**: it **adds** income Indians earn abroad and **subtracts** income foreigners earn in India.

The bridge between them is **Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)** = factor income coming in **minus** factor income going out — where "factor income" means **wages, rent, interest *and* profit**, not just remittances.

> **The professor:** "GDP focuses on the geography. GNP focuses on the people."

**India today: GNP is slightly lower than GDP** (roughly **₹174 lakh crore** GNP vs **₹176 lakh crore** GDP at constant 2011-12 prices, MOSPI) — which means **NFIA is negative**. Why? Foreign profit repatriated out of India, highly-paid foreign skills here versus lower-paid Indian labour abroad, rising FII holdings sending dividends out, Indians buying US ADRs and treasuries. People wrongly picture only **Kerala–Dubai remittances coming in**; the *outflows* across all four factors can outweigh them.

**The three axes of accounting difference** (learn these as a set):

| Axis | The two forms | Bridge between them |
|---|---|---|
| **Boundary** | Domestic (GDP) ↔ National (GNP) | **+ NFIA** |
| **Wear and tear** | Gross ↔ Net | **− Depreciation** |
| **Pricing** | Market price ↔ Factor cost | **− Indirect taxes + Subsidies** |

On pricing: **market price is higher than factor cost**, because market price carries **indirect taxes** (which never reach the factors of production), while **subsidies** (think fertilisers, agriculture, chemicals) pull market price *below* factor cost. So **GDP at factor cost = GDP at market prices − indirect taxes + subsidies**.

> **The professor:** "All these are very similar concepts. Accounting differences make them different in numbers."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Three bridges convert the whole family: **National = Domestic + NFIA**, **Net = Gross − Depreciation**, **Factor cost = Market price − Indirect taxes + Subsidies**. And **National Income = NNP at factor cost**. Strip away NFIA, depreciation, indirect taxes and subsidies, and every measure collapses into one identical number — which is exactly why you pick the version that suits the question (factor cost to remove tax distortion, net to remove depreciation, domestic for a closed-economy view).
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "People, Wear, Taxes"]
Three jumps across the GDP family: **People** (NFIA, domestic → national), **Wear** (depreciation, gross → net), **Taxes** (indirect taxes minus subsidies, market → factor cost).
:::
