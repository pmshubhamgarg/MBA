# Session 1: One Fine Morning, You're Fired

*You plan everything on your drawing table — the price, the market, the demand. Then one morning a tariff, a rate hike, or a government change blows the plan apart. This session is about the weather no single firm can control, and the only survival skill that works: read it, anticipate it, and move fast.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **micro vs macro** split and why a firm has "total helplessness" over macro variables; the survival rule **understand → anticipate → be agile**; the **five macro assumptions** behind every business plan; **Savings = Investment**, the **Capital-Output Ratio**, and the **36% ÷ 4 = 9%** growth logic; **Aggregate Demand vs Aggregate Supply** balance and when inflation turns dangerous; the **fiscal (FM) + monetary (RBI)** toolkit; the **four phases** of a business cycle.

**Important (supporting):** **Keynes & pump priming**; the **fiscal-deficit → aggregate-demand → inflation** chain; **inflation targeting (~4%)**; **sticky costs** in the cement story; **cyclical vs non-cyclical** industries and **leading indicators** (shipping); **beta** greater than / less than 1; India's **agriculture cushion**.

**Context (colour for answers):** Panasonic & the Bangladesh turmoil; Trump's 25% tariff and "even if he's asleep"; the 15-minute dark-store shutdown; US treasury-rate job losses; the 2008 subprime crisis at L&T; TCS layoffs; AI at SAP/Accenture; Japan's long 1991 trough; Sri Lanka's borrowing collapse; COVID; China's smoothed cycle.
:::

## Setting the Stage: A New Term, a New Lens

Second term, first class. Everyone has just finished term one — **microeconomics, marketing management and financial analysis** — and now the course flips the camera around. Micro looked *inside* one firm. This course, **Managerial Economics**, zooms all the way out to the economy as a whole: **macroeconomics**.

A quick word on who is teaching. The professor was a **full professor at IMU until 2023**, then left, moved to Kerala, and now teaches as a **visiting faculty member** — online, from home. So the warnings about mute buttons and interruptions are real, but so is the payoff: most of the session runs on *lived* corporate war-stories, not textbook boxes.

She opens not with a definition but with a dare to the room's veterans:

> **The professor:** "At least people with 10 years — share an experience where one fine morning you're fired, or you're a product manager and one fine morning your product is just not selling, or interest rates have gone up so heavily that all your investment plans have gone for a toss."

That single prompt *is* the thesis of macroeconomics: the forces that can wreck your year are the ones you don't control.

### How You'll Be Graded

- **Two quizzes**, about **half an hour each**.
- **Two assignments** — newly added, **graded by the professor**, to be done inside the **study groups** (~10–12 groups).

## Big Idea 1: Micro Plans, Macro Weather — The Helplessness Problem

Here is the mental model the whole course hangs on. **Micro** is the firm's drawing table: you decide how much to produce, what price to charge, which market to enter — *assuming* the world around you stays calm. **Macro** is that world — and you cannot move it.

> **The professor:** "No single firm can influence those macro policy variables. None of you singly can change the interest rates. None of you singly can change the tax rates. At most you can understand, then anticipate, then be agile enough to change very quickly."

That's the entire survival playbook. You can't stop the tariff or the rate hike; you can only **see it coming and adapt before it lands**. It's exactly why corporates obsess over the **Union Budget** and the **RBI's quarterly monetary policy** — those are decisions made *for* them, that they must then bend to. On Trump's influence over IT, she drove the point home with a joke:

> **The professor:** "Even if he's asleep, it doesn't matter."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
A firm has **no control** over macro policy variables (interest rates, tax rates, inflation, exchange rates). The manager's only levers are **Understand → Anticipate → Adapt (be agile)**. If a case asks "what should a company do about a macro shock?", the answer is never "control it" — it's *read it early and react fast*. Those who don't adapt "go down deep and exit."
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Read the weather, don't fight it: U-A-A"]
**U**nderstand the policy, **A**nticipate the change, **A**dapt quickly. You can't change the storm; you can carry an umbrella.
:::

## Big Idea 2: The Five Macro Assumptions Hiding in Every Business Plan

Every micro decision silently assumes the macro backdrop holds still. The professor lists the **five assumptions** a manager bakes in — and warns that any of them can break.

| # | Assumption | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Stable demand growth** | Recession, disruption |
| 2 | **Stable interest rates** | RBI tightening |
| 3 | **Stable prices** (no runaway inflation) | War, supply shocks |
| 4 | **Stable tax rates** | A Budget surprise |
| 5 | **Stable exchange rates** | Currency swings *(flagged, but not in this course)* |

> **The professor:** "If there is runaway inflation, you've had it. You don't know how to tackle that problem at the micro level."

:::tip[Important]
The exam-friendly framing: managers make **micro decisions** on top of **macro assumptions**. List the five, and say that the manager's job is to keep an "antenna alert" for any of them shifting — because the firm optimises *within* these assumptions but cannot *set* them.
:::

## Big Idea 3: The Cement Story — How One Firm's Pain Becomes the Economy's

The textbook's Chapter 1 follows a single cement firm, and the professor uses it to climb from micro to macro. Two scenarios do all the work.

**Scenario A — demand runs hot (boom).** The firm builds a plant for **100 tons** after a market survey. It arrives to find the market actually wants **120 tons**. You cannot conjure 20 extra tons overnight — that needs a new plant, people, capital. So the only release valve is **price**: prices rise, and that is **inflation** for cement. Blow it up to the whole economy and you get an **overheated, booming** economy where aggregate demand outruns capacity.

**Scenario B — demand collapses (recession).** Now demand is low. You'd love to cut prices and sell more, but you can't — because your **costs are sticky**. Input prices don't fall, so output prices can't fall either.

> **The professor:** "I'll rather close down. My costs are high, the prices cannot go high because people will not buy — there's low demand. So the way out is to shut down. So I shut down. So does everybody else."

When every firm reasons this way, the economy itself shuts down: **recession → depression → mass unemployment**.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "100 can't become 120 by morning, and sticky costs won't bend"]
Boom = demand (120) outruns capacity (100) → prices jump. Bust = demand falls but costs stay stuck → firms shut rather than sell cheap. One firm's dilemma, multiplied, *is* the business cycle.
:::

## Big Idea 4: Blowing It Up — Savings, the Capital-Output Ratio, and India's 9%

How do you estimate what an *entire economy* can produce? The professor walks a clean chain from the cement firm to the nation.

1. A firm that wants to grow must **invest**, and to invest it needs money. For the economy, that money is **savings** — of individuals, the public and the private sector combined.
2. **Total savings = total investment.** All savings get channelled into investment.
3. But investment alone isn't output. You need to know **how much capital it takes to produce one unit of output** — the **Capital-Output Ratio (COR)**.

> **The professor:** "The higher the capital-output ratio, the lesser the efficiency — you need more capital to produce one unit of output. If you're more efficient, your capital-output ratio falls."

Plug in India's numbers from the book: **savings rate ≈ 36%**, **COR ≈ 4:1**. Growth is simply savings divided by the COR:

**36% ÷ 4 = 9%** → India's **potential growth ≈ 9%**.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The growth engine in one line: **Savings → Investment → Output, filtered by the Capital-Output Ratio.** Potential growth = **savings rate ÷ COR**. For India that's **36 ÷ 4 = 9%**. A *higher* COR means *lower* efficiency (more capital per unit of output). Be ready to compute this and explain each arrow.
:::

## Big Idea 5: If You Were the Finance Minister

Switch chairs. You're no longer running one firm — you run the whole economy. The class built the FM's goal-sheet together:

1. **Stable, sustainable growth** — keep *realised* growth close to *potential* (~9%); a little deviation is fine, too much triggers business cycles.
2. **Stable prices / inflation targeting** — India aims for about **4%** (stretch to 5% at most). Beyond that, "production can't catch up with the rise in prices."
3. **Fiscal-deficit control** (~4%) — a deficit means spending exceeds earnings; left unchecked it pumps up aggregate demand.
4. **High employment** / low involuntary unemployment.

Why care so much about the fiscal deficit? Because of a chain the professor keeps returning to:

**High fiscal deficit → too much money chasing goods → aggregate demand jumps → if supply can't keep up → inflation.**

The whole point of all this is to build a **congenial, friendly business environment** — stable demand, stable prices, stable taxes — so the micro corporates can then "take over and produce" with confidence.

:::tip[Important]
A student asked why not target **zero** inflation. The professor deferred the full answer to the aggregate demand/supply discussion — but the hint is already here: a little inflation usually rides alongside healthy *output* growth. Inflation only becomes the enemy when prices race *ahead* of output (see Big Idea 6).
:::

## Big Idea 6: Aggregate Demand vs Aggregate Supply — Where Inflation Turns Dangerous

This is the master balance the FM and RBI manage: **aggregate demand (AD)** against **aggregate supply (AS)**. You don't want runaway demand (high prices) or idle supply. Two stories show what happens when they drift apart.

**War-time spending.** In any war — WW1, WW2, Iraq, Iran — the state pours money into **defence**. That spending trickles down into demand, but the **supply of ordinary goods doesn't rise**. Result: **runaway inflation**.

**Sri Lanka.** No war — just **reckless borrowing**, a sudden shift to **organic farming** that farmers couldn't manage (supply crashed), and grand **infrastructure** (roads "like Singapore or Dubai") built on the hope that private investment would follow.

> **The professor:** "They assumed that the market would react positively. It did not."

Then the diagram. For the *same* rise in aggregate demand, three economies respond differently:

| Scenario | AD rises → | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — healthy** | Output up *and* prices up (roughly equal) | Growth with mild inflation |
| **2 — trouble** | Output up a little, **prices up a lot** | "Inflation is going out of hand" |
| **3 — dangerous** | **All price, no output** | Stringent bottlenecks — no roads, no power — supply can't move |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Inflation becomes dangerous only when the rise in prices is greater than the growth in output.** In the short run the fix is to **pull down aggregate demand** (supply can't rise overnight — raise tax rates, tighten money). In the long run you **ease supply** by clearing bottlenecks (raw material, transport, power) so markets can answer demand with more goods, not just higher prices.
:::

A subtle but examinable point: governments work in **two time horizons at once**. In the **short run** they keep realised output near potential; in the **long run** they try to grow capacity itself. And they can only **cajole and lure** private investment (rate cuts, ease of doing business) — "at the end of the day the market has to react."

## Big Idea 7: The Policy Toolkit — Fiscal, Monetary, and Keynes's Pump

Two bosses steer the macro environment, each with their own kit.

| Policy | Boss | Main tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal** | Finance Minister | **Government spending**; **tax rates** |
| **Monetary** | RBI (central bank) | **Repo / interest rates**; **CRR** (Cash Reserve Ratio); **SLR** (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) |

The special power of fiscal policy is that the government can act **alone, overnight, without waiting for the market**. It can spend a fortune or cut to nothing; it can raise or drop tax rates. Raise income tax, for instance, and people have **less disposable income → they spend less → aggregate demand cools** (that cancelled holiday is demand being "sucked out" of the system).

This is where **John Maynard Keynes** enters. During the Great Depression — employment, GDP, prices, interest rates all at rock bottom, and *nobody* in the private sector willing to risk a rupee — Keynes argued the government must step in and spend to **"prime the pump"** and restart the engine.

> **The professor:** "You cannot leave things to the market. There has to be a government in place. Government will have to step in — government spending is the only saviour in all these depressive states."

:::note[Good to Know]
The delicious irony: **Keynes himself was a stock-market player with plenty of money**, yet it was *he* who prescribed government fiscal action to escape the Depression — "and it worked." Also worth banking: fiscal and monetary policy must move **hand in hand**; if one pulls against the other, the whole stabilisation effort fails.
:::

## Big Idea 8: Business Cycles — Why Some Industries Shake More

A **business cycle** is the short-run up-and-down of output, employment, prices and interest rates around the economy's potential. Shocks set it off. It moves through **four phases**:

**Peak → Slowdown / Recession → Depression (the trough) → Recovery → Peak again.**

The catch the professor stresses twice: you can draw a neat wave, but you **cannot predict its intensity or its duration**. The Great Depression ran 3–4 years; **Japan sat in a trough from 1991 until recently** — interest rates near zero, even negative, yet no pull in the economy.

Not every industry rides the wave equally:

| Behaviour | Industries | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Very cyclical** (high "beta") | Capital goods, machinery, manufacturing, **shipping/marine**, automobiles, white goods, air travel | Big, **postponable** purchases — buyers wait out the dip |
| **Smoother** (low "beta") | **FMCG / consumables**, daily essentials | You **can't postpone** eating; demand stays steady |

In market jargon, an industry with **beta greater than 1** swings *more* than the economy; **beta less than 1** swings *less*. **Shipping is a leading indicator**: when its order book stagnates, the rest of the economy soon follows.

> **The professor:** "It's a leading indicator. It leads to downfall."

Two more texture points. **COVID** is the textbook whole-economy shock — and it's exactly when the **government stepped in** to steady things. And a **communist economy like China** deliberately **smooths** its cycle: heavy state control won't let demand spike or crash too hard (the more control, the more communist) — though China is also secretive about recession data, so its real-estate bust and stalled projects had to be *inferred*. India gets a natural cushion too: **agriculture, still its largest GDP contributor, barely follows the cycle.**

:::tip[Memory Hook — "If you can postpone buying it, the industry is cyclical"]
Can't delay it (soap, food) → smooth, low beta. Can delay it (a car, a ship, a holiday) → violent, high beta. Shipping's order book is the economy's early-warning light.
:::

## The External Sector (Flagged, Not Covered)

The professor is careful to draw a boundary. **Tariffs, sanctions** (like Trump's threat over buying Russian oil), the **Red Sea disruption** — these are **external factors beyond the economy's control**, and they are hitting India "in a very big way."

> **The professor:** "These are beyond our economy — somebody else doing something, and that affecting us."

But this course stays *inside* the economy for now. Master what governments and the RBI do domestically first; the external sector (and exchange rates) comes later.

## Real-World Takeaway

Macroeconomics, the professor admits, is "very interesting sitting from the outside and commenting — but when you have to face it, it is very, very challenging." A student put the whole course in a sentence: **after the event it's easy to understand; during the event there's no clue how to handle it.**

That gap is the manager's job. You can't stop the tariff, the rate hike or the layoff wave. But if you understand how the machine works, keep your antenna alert, and stay agile, you'll be the firm that saw the pink slip coming — and had already moved.
