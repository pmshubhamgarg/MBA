# Session 7: When India Mortgaged Its Gold — and Keynes Lit the Multiplier

*1991 showed what happens when a country lives beyond its means. The rest of the session hands you Keynes's rescue kit — aggregate demand, the consumption function, and the multiplier that turns one rupee of spending into many.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **1991 crisis as the extreme twin-deficit case** (beg the IMF, mortgage gold, forced devaluation, shift from fixed to flexible rate); the four components of aggregate demand **Y = C + I + G + (X − M)**; the **Keynesian consumption function C = A + cY**; **MPC = ΔC/ΔY** and **MPC + MPS = 1**; the **multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC)**.

**Important (supporting):** Why the twin deficit is India's problem, not China's or the US's; fixed vs flexible exchange rate and **convertibility**; autonomous vs induced consumption; investment as a function of **interest rates + business expectations**; government spending as the **autonomous rescue lever**; higher MPC means a bigger multiplier.

**Context (colour for answers):** The **Micawber principle** (Dickens); the "made in Italy" GDP-attribution puzzle; currency **pegging** (Nepal, Bhutan); the 6.4% to 7.8% GDP-jump skepticism; defense and war spending; "cut the coat according to the cloth."
:::

## Quick Recap

Last session ended on the **twin deficit** — the fiscal deficit and the current account deficit arriving together because of the national-income identity. The professor's one-line test: a twin deficit only becomes a *problem* when the **current account deficit cannot be financed by foreign savings** — that is, when the capital account fails to plug the gap.

- **US:** runs a huge current account deficit, but money keeps flooding in, so its capital account matches. No crisis.
- **India:** can also run a deficit *if* it lures in foreign savings. The question that opened today: has India always managed that? The brutal answer is **1991**.

---

## The 1991 Crisis — Twin Deficit at Its Most Brutal

**Concept.** When a country owes the world dollars and nobody will lend, the twin deficit stops being a textbook line and becomes a survival crisis.

**The class example.** In 1991 India had a huge current account deficit. It could not lean on the **government** (already deep in deficit) or on **private investment** (far too low). It had to rely on **foreign savings — and FDI and FII dried up completely.** So India went to the IMF, and to get dollars it **mortgaged its gold**.

> **The professor:** "So we begged and begged and went to the IMF. We mortgaged our gold and got a loan in dollars."

Even that covered only a sliver of the gap. To pay exporting countries in dollars, India had to **dig into its foreign-exchange reserves**. Foreign investors wanted out — they dumped rupees and demanded dollars — so there was a flood of rupees and almost no dollars left.

**Why it mattered — the trap.** Left to the market, the rupee would have crashed. But India was on a **fixed exchange rate**, officially committed to defend a set rupee-dollar price it no longer had the dollars to defend. The only exit was to **devalue** and then reform.

**Manmohan Singh's twin reforms** (finance minister; Narasimha Rao was PM):
1. **Exit the fixed rate** → move to a **flexible / market exchange rate**, letting supply and demand set the rupee.
2. **Draw a road map to pull in foreign savings** → ease of doing business, open the economy, and make the rupee **convertible** so money could enter and leave freely.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**1991 = the twin deficit in its extreme form.** Memorise the chain: *huge current account deficit → foreign inflows dry up → no dollars to defend a fixed rate → mortgage gold, borrow from IMF, devalue → liberalise (flexible rate + convertibility + open economy).* If a question asks "what forces a country to reform," 1991 is your exhibit A.
:::

### Fixed vs Flexible — Why the Regime Decides Your Fate

Why would any country *choose* a fixed rate? **Certainty.** A business knows exactly what the rate will be and can plan investments around it. But the price of that certainty is a **commitment you must honour** — you cannot let an official rate drift away from a black-market (hawala) rate, or nobody believes you.

| | Fixed rate | Flexible (market) rate |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it | Government / RBI, officially | Market supply and demand |
| Upside | Certainty — easy to plan | Self-adjusts; no reserve drain |
| Risk | Must defend it with reserves; forced **devaluation** if they run out | Day-to-day volatility |
| India | Pre-1991 trap | Post-1991 **managed float** (RBI can still step in as a big player) |

**Pegging.** Smaller neighbours tie their currency to the rupee — Nepal around **1.6**, Bhutan at **1:1** — so when the rupee moves against the dollar, their currencies move with it.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Fixed = a promise you must fund"]
A fixed rate is only as strong as your dollar reserves. Run out of dollars and the promise breaks — exactly what snapped India in 1991.
:::

## Why the Twin Deficit Is India's Problem, Not Everyone's

The professor was blunt: the twin deficit is a real worry for India, but **not for China, the US, or Singapore.** Those are surplus economies — trade surpluses give them a pile of foreign savings *looking* for somewhere to invest. India's job is the reverse: **prove** it has the right business environment, a credible growth path, and political stability so that foreign savings choose to come here.

:::note[Good to Know — The "Made in Italy" GDP Puzzle]
A student opened class with a sharp question: Italy can stamp a product "made in Italy" if the **last substantial transformation** happens there — so who gets the GDP credit when the parts were built elsewhere? The professor admitted this is an **unresolved flaw in GDP itself**. A smartphone crosses many countries before it reaches your hand, and GDP's clean definition — "the value of all final goods and services produced *within* a country" — quietly assumes that away.

> **The professor:** "That is the problem with GDP — you don't know how much of the value of a good should be credited to which country."

The professor also flagged healthy skepticism about India's own numbers: a forecast that jumped from **6.4% to 7.5–7.8%** in two months is worth questioning — real growth does not turn on a dime.
:::

## The Pivot: Aggregate Demand Is the Economy's Steering Wheel

Now the session turns to its core new topic. Output equals income equals aggregate demand at equilibrium:

**Y = C + I + G + (X − M)**

**Why obsess over aggregate demand?** Because **swings in aggregate demand trigger the business cycle** — the booms and busts. If you can nudge aggregate demand with policy, you can smooth those shocks into a **soft landing** instead of a hard crash. The economist who built this toolkit is **John Maynard Keynes.**

| Component | Symbol | Driven by | Short-run flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumption | C | Disposable income (via MPC) | Largest (~50–60%); fairly stable |
| Investment | I | Interest rates + business expectations | **Volatile** |
| Government spending | G | Policy choice (deficit / borrow / print) | **Fully autonomous, fast** |
| Net exports | X − M | External sector, tariffs, bilateral ties | Outside government control |

---

## Live Within Your Means — The Micawber Principle

Before the maths, the professor reached for Dickens. In *David Copperfield*, Mr Micawber lays out the whole of macro-prudence in two lines (one pound = 20 shillings; one shilling = 12 pence):

> **The professor:** "Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure nineteen nineteen six — result, happiness. Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure twenty pounds ought and six — result, misery."

**The lesson.** Live within your means and you save; live beyond them and you sink. And saving is not idle — through financial markets, **savings becomes investment (S = I)**, which is what makes a nation grow.

- **Save, invest, grow:** 19th-century US, 20th-century Japan, East Asia.
- **Consume more, save less:** many African and Latin American economies that stayed stuck at low development.

The identity also splits the saving: **S = private saving + (T − G) government saving + (M − X) foreign saving.**

---

## The Consumption Function — Keynes's Starting Block

**Concept.** Since consumption is the single largest slice of demand and a **leading indicator** of where GDP is heading, Keynes asked: what *is* consumption a function of? His answer:

**C = A + cY**

- **A = autonomous consumption** — the intercept. The **subsistence minimum** you consume *regardless* of income. As the professor put it, you cover it "by beg, borrow, or steal" if you must.
- **c = the slope = MPC** — the **marginal propensity to consume**, ΔC/ΔY: how much of each extra rupee of income you spend.

**The class pattern (US table, mirrored in India):** services take the biggest bite, durables a small one, and non-durables the rest.

| Consumption slice | Rough share | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Services | ~60% | Largest and rising with income |
| Non-durables (food, clothing, energy) | ~29% | Food share stays flat as income rises |
| Durable goods | ~11% | Lumpy, income-sensitive |

As income climbs, the **relative** spend on food stays flat while housing, transport, medical care and luxuries rise — and personal saving (around **6%** of disposable income) does the balancing.

**The 45° line.** Along a 45° line, consumption exactly equals income (MPC = 1). The real consumption function cuts across it: **above the line is dissaving** (you spend more than you earn — borrow, beg, or steal), **below it is saving.**

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**C = A + cY.** A is autonomous consumption (does not move with income); small **c is the MPC = ΔC/ΔY**, and it is **always between 0 and 1 (never quite 1)** — you cannot spend every extra rupee or you would sit on the 45° line. MPC is **higher in poorer / lower-income groups** and **lower in richer / developed ones.** This single equation is the seed of the whole Keynesian model.
:::

:::tip[Important — MPC is the slope, not a region]
A common slip: pointing at the gap between the curve and the 45° line and calling it "MPC." It is not. **MPC is the slope of the consumption line.** The gaps above and below the 45° line are **dissaving** and **saving**.
:::

---

## Savings Is Just the Leftover

Saving is simply whatever consumption does not take:

**S = Y − C = Y − (A + cY) = −A + (1 − c)Y**

So the slope of the savings line, **(1 − c)**, is the **marginal propensity to save (MPS)**. Because every extra rupee is either spent or saved:

**MPC + MPS = 1** (that is, c + s = 1)

Keep the two "averages vs margins" apart: **APC = C/Y** (average), **MPC = ΔC/ΔY** (marginal). If people consume a lot (high c), they save little, invest little, and — via S = I — the economy grows slowly.

---

## Investment — Interest Rates and Animal Spirits

**Concept.** Investment (I) is the second, and most temperamental, component. The professor pulled two drivers out of the class:

1. **Interest rates.** A firm compares a project's **return on investment** against the **cost of capital**, anchored to the interest rate the RBI effectively sets. When rates fall, projects that were unviable become viable — so the **investment-demand schedule slopes down** (interest rate on the vertical axis, investment on the horizontal).
2. **Business expectations.** Pure sentiment about an uncertain future — and it can swamp interest rates entirely.

> **The professor:** "Even if you give them money free of cost, they'll hold it. They'll keep the money. They will not invest."

Because expectations rest on "flimsy foundations," they can flip dramatically on a rumour of political change. That makes **investment demand unstable and volatile** — yet investment decisions still *must* be made. This is the fog in which Keynes operates.

:::tip[Important — Why "free money" can fail]
In a deep slump, low interest rates alone do **not** revive private investment, because expectations are too grim. That failure is exactly why Keynes turns to the one actor who can still spend: the government.
:::

---

## Government Spending — The Only Spender Who Designs the Coat First

**Concept.** Government spending (G) is **fully autonomous in the short run.** Unlike you and me, the government is not boxed in by its income — it can run a **deficit**, borrow, or, failing everything, **print money.**

> **The professor:** "An individual cuts the coat according to the cloth. The government designs the coat first — then buys cloth to fit it. If nothing else works, it prints the money."

That is why Keynes **leans on the government**: when no private individual will inject demand, the state can. The big-ticket items are **defense** (wars — Indo-Pak, China, Bangladesh, the Gulf, the World Wars) and **infrastructure**. Net exports (X − M), by contrast, depend on the external sector — tariffs, barriers and bilateral ties, "like India is facing now."

> **The professor:** "No country has gone into a deep depression since — simply because we learned from Keynes."

## The Keynesian Multiplier — One Rupee Becomes Many

**Concept.** Here is the session's payoff. Take a closed economy and substitute the consumption function, using disposable income Y_D = Y − T:

```text
Y = C + I + G
C = A + c(Y − T)

Y = A + c(Y − T) + I + G
Y − cY       = A − cT + I + G
Y(1 − c)     = A − cT + I + G
Y            = (A − cT + I + G) × [ 1 / (1 − c) ]
```

That last bracket, **1 / (1 − c)**, is the **multiplier.** The autonomous pieces (A, I, G, and T) get multiplied up to the new equilibrium income. **Trigger just one of them — say G — and income rises by a multiple.**

| MPC (c) | Multiplier = 1 / (1 − c) | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 2 | ₹1 of extra G lifts income by ₹2 |
| 0.8 | 5 | ₹1 of extra G lifts income by ₹5 |

Notice the twist: the **higher the MPC, the bigger the multiplier.** Since poorer economies have higher MPCs, a spending push works *harder* there.

> **The professor:** "The poorer the economy, the more successful an injection through G or I in lifting Y."

This is Keynes's great contribution: in a recession Y is stuck low, private investment is frozen, and taxes can't be raised — so **increase G**, let the multiplier do the rest, and lift the economy out. It feels counterintuitive (how can ₹100 of spending create more than ₹100 of income?), but the *mechanism* — round after round of re-spending — is next session's story.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC).** New income = autonomous spending × multiplier. With MPC = 0.5 the multiplier is 2; with MPC = 0.8 it is 5. In a slump, **G is the lever** (I is frozen, T can't rise), and a high MPC makes the push more powerful. Be ready to derive it from Y = C + I + G.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "1 over 1 minus c"]
Whenever MPC appears, reach for **1 / (1 − c).** The more a country *spends* out of each extra rupee, the smaller the denominator — and the larger the bang per rupee of government spending.
:::
