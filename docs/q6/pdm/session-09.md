# Session 9: Crossing the Chasm — Why Great Products Stall Between the Early Adopters and Everyone Else

*Launching a product is the easy part. Getting one person to adopt it, and then a whole market to diffuse it, is the work that decides whether the product lives — and the frameworks that tell you where you are on that journey*

> **Professor Banerjee:** "Adoption is an individual phenomenon. Diffusion is a group phenomenon."

Launching a product is not enough. It has to be *used* — to become "an artifact of regular use" — by one person (adoption), and then by enough people within the target market (diffusion). ## The Product Life Cycle

A successful new product does not last forever. Sales and profits vary over its life with market demand, which itself shifts with technology. The **PLC** is the concept that captures this pattern.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "It implies that products have a limited life. The product sales pass through distinct stages, with each stage posing different challenges, opportunities and problems."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The **four PLC stages** and how the marketing job changes at each — expect a question asking you to explain a stage and apply it to a case.

| Stage | Sales | Profits | Customers | Marketing Objective |
|-------|-------|---------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Introduction** | Low | Negative/low (high promo cost) | Innovators | Create product awareness, induce trial |
| **Growth** | Rapidly rising | Increasing | Early adopters | Maximise market share |
| **Maturity** | Peak, then flat | High then stabilising | Early & late majority | Maximise profit, defend share |
| **Decline** | Falling | Falling | Laggards | Cut costs, milk the brand |
:::

### Introduction: The Price × Promotion Matrix

In the introduction stage, product and place are largely fixed; the flexibility is in **price and promotion**, giving four launch strategies:

| | High Promotion | Low Promotion |
|---|----------------|---------------|
| **High Price** | **Rapid skimming** (new mobile handsets — price high, drop as each competitor arrives) | **Slow skimming** (luxury cars, niche products) |
| **Low Price** | **Rapid penetration** (Reliance Jio in telecom) | **Slow penetration** (Balaji wafers — priced low, no ads, more grams per pack) |

The professor's mobile-handset example nails skimming: launch high, drop the price a little when the first competitor appears at ~3 months, drop again at ~6 months — "taking the topmost layer off the market." Balaji wafers illustrate slow penetration for **variety-seeking** categories (biscuits, wafers, snacks): unknown brand, no advertising, lower price per gram, riding on the category awareness the big players pay to build.

### Growth

Marked by rapid sales increases and incoming competition (which arrives the moment someone is seen making money). Strategies: add features, improve quality and style, add models/flankers, enter new segments, increase distribution, shift advertising from *awareness* to *preference*, and cut price to reach the next layer of price-sensitive buyers. The core trade-off: **high market share vs. high current profit** — firms often forego current profit to dominate, betting on loyalty and a longer, more profitable life later.

### Maturity

Most products today sit here, so most product strategy is about mature products. It has three sub-stages: **growth maturity** (still growing, slower), **stable maturity** (flat, saturated), and **decaying maturity** (absolute sales beginning to fall). Three modification levers:

- **Market modification** — get the *same* customers to consume *more*. The professor's signature example: **Colgate widened the tube nozzle by 4 mm** so more paste is squeezed per brushing — "that increases sales by maybe about 20%; in maturity stage, that's awesome growth." Also **Clinic shampoo** advertising "rinse twice," doubling consumption per wash.
- **Product modification** — Lifebuoy → Lifebuoy handwash; smartphone **versioning** (better battery, storage, camera to drive upgrades).
- **Marketing mix modification** — change price, channel, or promotion.

A key clarification from the class discussion: **geographic expansion is a *growth*-stage move, not maturity.** He illustrated with **Chitale Bandhu** (Pune) and **Pahalwan** (Jammu) sweet shops — mature in their home city's heart but still growing by opening outlets in newer areas, sustaining 12–25 years of "growth" on the same beloved products. Contrast this with genuine maturity, where geography is already covered and you must squeeze more from existing users.

### Decline

Sales fall due to technology change, shifting taste, and over-capacity leading to price-cutting and profit erosion. Unless there is a strong reason to hold a weak product, it is best to let it go. Strategies: **increase** investment to dominate, **maintain** until uncertainties resolve, **decrease** selectively (drop weak segments, focus on niches), **harvest** (stop investing, milk the brand), or **divest** (sell off).

### The 4 P's at Each Stage

The professor's two-page revision summary maps the marketing mix onto the four stages — a compact table worth memorising for the exam:

| | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
|---|--------------|--------|----------|---------|
| **Product** | Basic product | Extensions, services, warranties | Diversify brands & models (versioning) | Phase out weak items |
| **Price** | Cost-plus (marginal cost is the floor) | Price to penetrate | Match or better competition | Cut price |
| **Place** | Selective distribution | Intensive distribution | More intensive distribution | Selective — phase out unprofitable outlets |
| **Promotion** | Heavy sales promotion to induce trial; build awareness among early adopters | Reduce promotion as demand builds; avoid discounts | Stress brand differences; sales promotion to encourage switching | Cut to retain hardcore loyals |

Note the pricing floor: you cannot charge below **marginal cost**, or you lose money on every extra unit sold.

## OKRs: Objectives and Key Results

To plan a roadmap, you first need goals. **OKRs** — a goal-setting and execution tool introduced by **Andy Grove (Intel)** and popularised by **John Doerr** — force hard choices.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "OKRs basically set up the MVP and then keep on adding to it. We cannot have everything on the plate in one shot."

- **Objectives** define *what* we want to achieve — qualitative, aspirational.
- **Key Results** define *how* we'll know we're on track — 3–5 quantitative, measurable, time-bound outcomes ("focus less on output, more on the outcome").

His food-delivery example: Objective — *dominate food ordering and delivery*; 90-day Key Results — grow new customers 30%, expand partner restaurants 40%, increase delivery personnel 25%. Then each department cascades its own OKRs (marketing: SEO/SEM to 500k visitors/month, three social campaigns, 10 micro-influencers/week). He compared it to a **CAT coaching class** breaking the exam into modules, testing each, then testing combinations. The five benefits: **alignment, focus, engagement, accountability, transparency.**

## Jobs-to-be-Done, Reinforced

The professor revisited JTBD (from Session 3) because the roadmap must be built on it. Customers "hire" products to make progress, across **functional, emotional, and social** dimensions.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "People don't want a drill, they want the hole which the drill will make. A streaming service is not just a streaming service — it helps a person relax after work."

His example: fruit juice is a crowded functional market (Tropicana), but **Paper Boat** won by adding the **emotional (nostalgia)** and **social** dimensions with flavours like aam panna and coconut water. JTBD maps the market's **pains → pain relievers** and **gains → gain creators** — the same product-market-fit logic that will structure the Nexent roadmap.

## Diffusion of Innovation and the Chasm

The heart of the session. First, the individual **adoption stages**: awareness → comprehension → attitude → legitimation → trial → adoption. A crucial nuance:

> **Professor Banerjee:** "People who buy a product twice have not yet adopted — they are just trying it a second time. We should not celebrate immediately when somebody repurchases."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovation** distributes adopters over time into five groups:

| Adopter Group | Share of Market | Character |
|---------------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Innovators** | 2.5% | Want the latest; adopt fast, move on fast |
| **Early Adopters** | 13.5% | Make the product part of their routine |
| **Early Majority** | 34% | The tipping point into the mainstream |
| **Late Majority** | 34% | Adopt once it is established |
| **Laggards** | 16% | Adopt last, if at all |

The **first 50%** = innovators + early adopters + early majority.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "We should feel good about a product only when the early adopters have adopted and the early majority has *started* using it. Till then we cannot say whether our new product is successful. We have to handhold our product until the early majority starts buying."

**The Chasm (Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*):** many products get stuck in the gap between the **early adopters** and the **early majority**. Prompted by a student (Manish), the professor confirmed: "This is Jeffrey Moore's crossing the chasm." The product roadmap is precisely the plan for carrying a product *across* that chasm.
:::

### Factors Influencing the Rate of Diffusion (ACCORD)

How fast a product diffuses depends on six attributes, which Rogers packages as **ACCORD**:

| Factor | Question |
|--------|----------|
| **Advantage** (relative advantage) | Is it clearly superior to existing/competing products? |
| **Compatibility** | Is it consistent with consumers' values, lifestyle, past experience? |
| **Complexity** | Is it easy to understand and use? (complexity slows diffusion) |
| **Observability** (communicability) | Are the results visible and describable to others? |
| **Riskiness** | Are there foreseeable negative economic/social/physical consequences? |
| **Divisibility** (trialability) | Can it be tried on a limited basis? (e.g., a premium version, SaaS trials) |

High **cost** slows diffusion — which is exactly why SaaS models, spreading cost over time, diffuse well.

## Eager Sellers and Stony Buyers

Customers do not leap at new products; they **resist**. The reading *Eager Sellers and Stony Buyers* gives a 2×2 that predicts the fate of a launch based on two axes: how much the **product changes** (demanded of the producer) and how much **behavior change** it demands of the customer.

:::tip[Important]
**The customer-resistance matrix:**

| | Low Behavior Change | High Behavior Change |
|---|---------------------|----------------------|
| **High Product Change** | **Smash hits** | **Long haul** (takes a lot of time) |
| **Low Product Change** | **Easy sales** | **Sure failures** |

> **Professor Banerjee:** "The seller may be eager but the buyer is stony — the buyer is resisting."

To reduce resistance: make **behaviorally compatible** products; target consumers not yet locked into entrenched products; **find believers**; brace for slow adoption; and — as a golden rule — **add on**, offering benefits much greater than existing products.
:::

## The Technology Readiness Index (TRI)

Product teams often over-estimate how tech-ready their customers are. The **TRI** (from the reading in the course material) is a **validated multi-item scale** that measures a customer group's readiness to embrace technology across four psychological dimensions:

:::tip[Important]
**TRI's four dimensions** split into drivers and inhibitors:

| Dimension | Items | Role |
|-----------|-------|------|
| **Optimism** | 10 | **Driver** |
| **Innovativeness** | 5 | **Driver** |
| **Discomfort** | 7 | **Inhibitor** |
| **Insecurity** | 6 | **Inhibitor** |

It is measured at the **individual** level (not the company's Python-library competence), because if you ask directly ("are you optimistic/innovative?") everyone says yes. Items are grouped by **factor analysis** (exploratory / confirmatory / principal component analysis), and scale reliability is checked with **Cronbach's alpha** (a value **> 0.7** is accepted as reliable). Some innovativeness items are **reverse-coded**.
:::

Uses: understand the customer base's overall readiness, benchmark it against the public, find distinct **segments**, and decide the pace of technology rollout and support needed. It even helps in hiring **tech-support / customer-service** staff — people who rate high on *both* interpersonal skills and technology readiness are far more effective.

The professor closed the TRI module (in the session's Q&A tail) by walking through **Table 4** of the reading — mean TRI scores segmented by whether people currently own, plan to buy, or have no plans to buy eight technology services. Using **ANOVA** and superscript notation (means sharing a superscript are *not* significantly different at p < 0.05), he showed the TRI **discriminates well for complex/futuristic technology** (computer at home: 3.04 vs. 2.74 vs. 2.50 — all significantly different) but **not for simple, ubiquitous ones** (cable TV: 2.91 / 2.82 / 2.80 — statistically the same). The lesson: TRI predicts adoption best exactly where discomfort and insecurity matter most.

## Decline Is Real: The IBM and Kalyani-Sharp Stories

A rich Q&A on exit strategy underscored that **not every firm survives decline**. When a whole industry is overtaken by technology, your immediate competitors may decline *with* you — pagers were killed by mobile handsets; typewriters vanished though everyone still types.

The professor's centrepiece was **IBM**. Until 1992 IBM was a hardware company (RISC/CISC machines, "Big Blue") and then, for the first time, made losses. Under **Lou Gerstner** (who came from Nabisco, a biscuit company) it transformed from hardware into end-to-end software and consulting — selling its PC business to Lenovo — but it was an eight-year, painful transformation that cost jobs and forced "**tin cupping**" (going around begging for project funding). Contrast **Kalyani Sharp**, which geared up to make VCPs/VCRs just as CDs, then USB drives, then streaming made the technology obsolete — a modern factory rendered nearly valueless. Modern echoes: **Unacademy** sold for ~$200M against a ~$3B peak; **Byju's** collapse.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "It's not easy — these transformations, if the company is big and in decline. We have to be able to come to terms with failure. Resilience is very important."

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **Adoption vs. Diffusion** | Adoption is an individual using a product; diffusion is the group's collective uptake |
| **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** | Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline; each stage needs different strategy |
| **Rapid/Slow Skimming & Penetration** | The four launch strategies from the price × promotion matrix |
| **Diffusion of Innovation** | Rogers' five adopter groups: innovators 2.5%, early adopters 13.5%, early majority 34%, late majority 34%, laggards 16% |
| **The Chasm** | Geoffrey Moore's gap between early adopters and early majority where products stall |
| **ACCORD** | Advantage, Compatibility, Complexity, Observability, Riskiness, Divisibility — factors in diffusion rate |
| **Eager Sellers & Stony Buyers** | Resistance matrix: product change × behavior change → easy sales, smash hits, sure failures, long haul |
| **Technology Readiness Index (TRI)** | Validated scale of optimism, innovativeness (drivers) and discomfort, insecurity (inhibitors) |
| **Cronbach's Alpha** | Reliability measure of a scale; > 0.7 is accepted |
| **OKRs** | Objectives (qualitative goal) + Key Results (3–5 quantitative, time-bound outcomes) |
| **Market Modification** | Growing sales from the *same* customers by increasing usage per occasion |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Dr. Anaya Rao had built something genuinely good. Her startup's app let rural diagnostic centres upload an X-ray and receive an AI-assisted reading within minutes. The pilot with fourteen tech-forward clinics had gone beautifully — those doctors loved the tool, tweaked its settings, and evangelised it at conferences. So Anaya raised a round, hired a sales team, and braced for the hockey-stick. It never came. Six months after launch, adoption had flatlined at roughly fifteen percent of her target market.

She assumed the product was broken and pushed her engineers to add features. Then a mentor drew two curves on a napkin. "You've won your innovators and early adopters," he said, tapping the left of the diffusion curve. "That first fifteen percent were always going to love you. The next thirty-four percent — the early majority — are different people. They don't want the newest thing. They want the *safe* thing. You're stuck in the chasm."

Anaya ran the diagnosis properly. On the resistance matrix, her product demanded a *high behavior change* from the average small-town radiologist — new workflow, new trust in a machine's reading — while offering a high product change. That put her squarely in the "long haul" quadrant, not the smash-hit one. Worse, when she quietly scored her prospects on a technology-readiness scale, the early-majority clinics rated far higher on **discomfort and insecurity** than her pilot users ever had. They were not lazy; they were anxious. The tool that delighted an innovator terrified a careful practitioner who had never let software second-guess her.

So Anaya stopped adding features and started reducing resistance. She made the AI a **co-pilot that suggested, never overruled**, keeping the doctor's behavior almost unchanged. She found *believers* — respected senior radiologists in mid-tier towns — and let them, not her sales team, carry the message. She offered a free, low-risk trial on a handful of scans a day so the product was **divisible**. And she braced her board for slow adoption instead of promising a curve that physics would not allow.

The early majority came — not in a leap, but in a long, patient climb. Anaya had learned the lesson the professor kept returning to: a launch is a single event, but diffusion is a journey with a chasm in the middle, and the frameworks exist so you know exactly where on the journey you are stuck — and why.

**A product does not fail because the early adopters didn't love it. It fails because nobody planned the crossing to everyone else.**
