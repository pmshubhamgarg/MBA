# Session 3: Positioning and Value Proposition — From Differentiation to the Six-Line Promise

*Why 72% of new products fail to deliver on expectations — and how a six-line template created by a guy who wrote "Crossing the Chasm" can save yours from becoming one of them*

## The Central Question of This Session

You have built (or are building) a product. You believe it solves a real problem. But here is the uncomfortable truth: your customer does not care about your product. They care about *their* problem. Your job as a product manager is to carve out a space in the customer's mind — a clear, distinct, defensible mental slot — that says: *this product is the best answer to my specific problem.*

That mental slot is called **positioning**. And the document that captures it is called the **value proposition statement**. This session builds the entire journey from differentiation to that statement.

---

## What Is Positioning, Really?

> **Professor:** "Positioning is the act of designing a company's offering and image so that they occupy a meaningful, distinctive competitive position in the target customer's mind."

Notice three words doing heavy lifting there: **meaningful** (it matters to the customer), **distinctive** (it is different from alternatives), and **competitive** (it holds up against what others offer).

Positioning is not just advertising. It has to be backed up by the actual product characteristics, the pricing, the distribution channel, and the promotion. Say you position your soap as "premium." If it is sold in a bin next to the cheapest detergent in a discount store, your positioning is dead on arrival.

:::tip[Important]
**Positioning** = designing your offering and image to occupy a *meaningful, distinctive, competitive* slot in the target customer's mind. Two steps: first **differentiate** (find a meaningful difference), then **communicate** it across every touchpoint — product, price, channel, promotion. Those three adjectives and the two steps are exam-ready.
:::

### Two Steps to Get There

| Step | What You Do |
|------|------------|
| **Step 1: Differentiate** | Identify how your product is meaningfully different from competitors |
| **Step 2: Communicate** | Develop and convey a positioning strategy built on those differences |

Simple in theory. The rest of this session is about making it work in practice.

## The Five Dimensions of Differentiation

:::tip[Important]
The **five dimensions of differentiation** — Product, Services, Personnel, Channel, Image — are a likely exam list. Pair it with the professor's distinction: **identity** is how a company aims to project itself; **image** is how customers actually perceive it. You control identity and only influence image.
:::

Before you can position, you need to know *where* your product can be different. Differentiation can happen along five dimensions:

| Dimension | What It Means | Example |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| **Product** | Physical characteristics, features, quality of the product itself | Lifebuoy soap vs. Lifebuoy handwash (same formulation, different *form*) |
| **Services** | Everything that wraps around the product | ICICI Bank going to the customer's table to offer loans |
| **Personnel** | The people who deliver the product or service | A well-trained service team that shows up on time, communicates clearly |
| **Channel** | How the product reaches the customer | Dell selling computers directly online; Amway using multi-level marketing |
| **Image** | How the brand is perceived | Apple's image of premium design and simplicity |

An important distinction the professor drew: **identity** is how a company *aims* to project itself, while **image** is how the target audience *actually perceives* it. You control identity. You influence image. They are not always the same thing.

## The Nine Product Differentiators

When you zoom into the product dimension specifically, there are nine ways a product can stand apart:

| Differentiator | What It Is | Example |
|---------------|-----------|---------|
| **Form** | The physical shape, size, or format | Lifebuoy soap becoming Lifebuoy handwash — same formula, liquid form, higher margin |
| **Features** | What the product can do | Surf Excel removing stubborn stains vs. Wheel which only cleans |
| **Performance Quality** | How well it performs its core function | Surf Excel using LABS (a chemical that binds with grease and dissolves in water) for superior stain removal |
| **Conformance Quality** | Consistency across every unit produced | The Yamaha RX100 — every unit had nearly identical pickup, maneuverability, and feel |
| **Durability** | How long the product lasts | Godrej Storwel steel almirahs — 15-20% more expensive but outlast competitors |
| **Reliability** | Probability the product will not malfunction | HP laptops known for consistent reliability |
| **Repairability** | Ease of fixing when things go wrong | Products with widely available spare parts and simple repair procedures |
| **Style** | Visual appearance and feel | Perfume bottles with distinctive shapes — you would rather buy a funkily shaped bottle than a plain one |
| **Design** | Usability combined with style | The total user experience — how something works *and* looks |

## Seven Service Differentiators

Beyond the product itself, the service wrapper can be a powerful differentiator:

| Service Differentiator | Example |
|-----------------------|---------|
| **Ordering ease** | Banks offering ATMs — without them, people would not do business with them |
| **Delivery** | FedEx enabling Dell's direct-to-customer model, which people initially doubted |
| **Installation** | Godrej refrigerator engineers carefully leveling every unit during setup |
| **Customer training** | Teaching customers how to get the most from the product |
| **Customer consulting** | Advising customers on best use cases |
| **Maintenance and repair** | Proactive servicing and responsive repair |
| **Miscellaneous services** | Value-added services beyond the core offering |

## The Seven Tests for a Worthwhile Differentiator

Not every difference is worth promoting. A differentiator earns its place only if it passes seven criteria. The professor shared his personal mnemonic: **IDSCPAP** (he pronounces it "IDS-CAP-AP" — the kind of mental shortcut consultants use when they are in front of a client and cannot look at their notes).

| Criterion | What It Means |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Important** | Delivers a highly valued benefit to a sufficient number of buyers |
| **Distinctive** | Not offered by competition, or not offered as clearly |
| **Superior** | Better than other ways the customer could get the same benefit |
| **Communicable** | Visible and easy to explain to buyers |
| **Pre-emptive** | Not easily copied by competitors |
| **Affordable** | Buyers can actually pay for the difference |
| **Profitable** | The company finds it profitable to introduce |

If your differentiator fails on any of these, reconsider whether it belongs in your positioning.

## Why Technical Jargon in Positioning Sometimes Works

:::note[Good to Know]
The "PUF" and LG "Golden Eye" trick — a technical name customers do not understand yet that signals distinctiveness, educates, and sparks curiosity ("killing multiple birds with one stone") — is a memorable nuance. Worth recognizing, but lower priority than the core positioning frameworks.
:::

A student asked: when Godrej says "refrigerator with PUF" (polyurethane foam), most customers have no idea what PUF is. So why use a technical term?

The professor's answer was sharp — it serves three purposes simultaneously:

1. **Distinctiveness** — Others cannot easily say "our product also has PUF." The term sets you apart.
2. **Customer education** — It signals that there is a desirable benefit worth learning about.
3. **Curiosity** — The customer thinks, "I don't know what PUF is, let me find out." And then the benefit of uniform cooling is explained.

> **Professor:** "It's killing multiple birds with one stone."

The same logic applies to LG's "Golden Eye" technology — a feature that reduces eye strain during long TV watching sessions. The technical name makes it sound proprietary even though the underlying benefit is simple: "soft on eyes."

## A Critical Distinction: Product Positioning vs. Company Positioning

A student asked about premium pricing and whether a company controls its positioning. The professor's response reframed the question entirely.

> **Professor:** "We are here in product management. So whatever we are talking of in terms of positioning is that of positioning a product, or at best a product brand."

He used a buffet analogy to make this vivid. Imagine you are planning a buffet:

- First you decide: South Indian or North Indian or Chinese? (This is the **business strategy** level.)
- Then within South Indian, you are the product manager of *sambar*. The sambar made in Tamil Nadu is different from the one in Andhra, which is different from Karnataka and Kerala. Your job is to position *your specific sambar*.

Unilever is a company. But Lux is a value-for-money soap, Lifebuoy is economy, and Dove is premium. Three completely different product positioning statements under one company umbrella. Product positioning operates at the **product or brand level**, not the company level.

## Why Cost Is NOT a Product-Level Differentiator

This generated significant class discussion. A student argued that lower production cost could be a differentiator. The professor disagreed — firmly but constructively.

His reasoning, illustrated with the Tata Motors example:

- Tata Motors makes hatchbacks (Indica, Bolt, Vista, Punch), sedans, SUVs, and EVs.
- If Tata is a cheaper producer, that cost advantage affects *all* products — it is an **SBU-level** capability, not a product-level one.
- Cost leadership is one of Porter's three generic strategies (cost leadership, differentiation, focus). It is a **business strategy** choice.
- At the product level, the job is to create *value* for the customer. Value creation is about what you *give* the customer, not what it costs you to make it.

> **Professor:** "I will not necessarily advertise the fact that I'm a cost leader. I may signal it to my competition, but I will not convey this to my customer — because then that would lead to negotiation to bring down my surplus."

The professor also introduced the concept of **value-based pricing** — pricing based on the value you create for the customer, not on your production cost. Positioning is about creating that value perception.

## How Many Differentiators Should You Promote?

Less is more. The class covered a clear hierarchy:

| Number of Benefits | Recommendation |
|-------------------|---------------|
| **One benefit** | Ideal. Single-minded positioning is the clearest and most memorable |
| **Two benefits** | Acceptable under competitive pressure |
| **Three or more** | Likely to confuse the customer. Avoid. |

The objective is to be **number one on some dimension that is important to the customer**. Not number one on everything — that is impossible. Pick one hill and own it.

## Seven Positioning Strategies

Once you have chosen your differentiator, you need a strategy for how to frame it. The professor walked through seven approaches:

| Strategy | What It Does | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Attribute positioning** | Positions based on a product attribute | LG TVs with "Golden Eye" technology (soft on eyes) |
| **Benefit positioning** | Claims leadership in a key benefit | Fair & Lovely — "makes you fairer in 3 weeks" |
| **Use/application positioning** | Best product for a specific use case | Move (pain relief cream) for backaches |
| **User positioning** | Best for a specific user group | Sugarfree — positioned for diabetics |
| **Competitor positioning** | Better than a named or implied competitor | Sprite's "Seedhi baat, no bakwaas" (straight talk, no nonsense) |
| **Product category positioning** | Leader of an entire category | Amul — "The Taste of India" |
| **Quality/price positioning** | Best value for money | Tata Indica V2 — "More car per car" |

This list is not exhaustive but covers the most common approaches.

## Communicating the Positioning

Once the positioning strategy is developed, it needs to reach customers through *every* touchpoint, not just advertising:

- Product features and characteristics (including packaging)
- Pricing strategy
- Distribution strategy
- Promotion strategy
- Development and promotion of company image

Positioning is a **holistic commitment**, not a tagline. If your positioning says "premium" but your distribution says "available in every corner shop," you have a contradiction.

## The Buyer Utility Map — A Tool for Spotting Differentiation

The professor introduced a tool from Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne (the Blue Ocean Strategy thinkers): the **Buyer Utility Map**. It answers a deceptively simple question: *Is my product actually different in a way that matters?*

It is a 6x6 matrix that maps **six stages of the buyer experience** against **six utility levers**:

:::tip[Important]
The **Buyer Utility Map** (Blue Ocean Strategy) is a 6×6 grid: six buyer-experience stages (Purchase, Delivery, Use, Supplements, Maintenance, Disposal) against six utility levers (Customer Productivity, Simplicity, Convenience, Risk, Fun & Image, Environmental Friendliness). Plot your concept across its 36 cells; genuine differentiation lives in cells competitors have not occupied.
:::

| | Purchase | Delivery | Use | Supplements | Maintenance | Disposal |
|---|---------|----------|-----|-------------|-------------|----------|
| **Customer Productivity** | | | | | | |
| **Simplicity** | | | | | | |
| **Convenience** | | | | | | |
| **Risk** | | | | | | |
| **Fun & Image** | | | | | | |
| **Environmental Friendliness** | | | | | | |

**How to use it:** Locate your new product concept on one or more of these 36 cells. If your product creates a different utility proposition from existing products in cells that competitors have not occupied, you have found genuine differentiation.

For example, most companies in a given industry might cluster around "Use + Customer Productivity." But what if you could differentiate at "Purchase + Simplicity" (making the buying process radically easier) or "Disposal + Environmental Friendliness" (making it easy to recycle)?

> **Professor:** "We plot this in product management. It is not the only way, but a good way to see how our product is going to be different from competition."

## From Business Model Canvas to Value Proposition Canvas

The professor screened video clips to walk through two connected frameworks. Most students already knew the Business Model Canvas from their entrepreneurship course. The new piece was the **Value Proposition Canvas** — a zoom-in on the two most critical blocks of the Business Model Canvas: *Customer Segments* and *Value Proposition*.

### The Business Model Canvas (Quick Recap)

Nine building blocks that describe an entire business:

| Block | What It Covers |
|-------|---------------|
| Customer Segments | Who you create value for |
| Value Propositions | What bundles of products/services create value |
| Channels | How you reach and interact with customers |
| Customer Relationships | Type of relationship with each segment |
| Revenue Streams | How you capture value (pricing mechanisms) |
| Key Resources | Assets indispensable to the model |
| Key Activities | Things you must do well |
| Key Partners | Who helps you leverage the model |
| Cost Structure | Major costs inherent in the model |

### The Value Proposition Canvas (The Product-Level Framework)

This is where product managers live. It is composed of two halves that must fit together:

**The Customer Profile (right side — the circle):**

| Component | What It Captures |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Jobs to be done** | What the customer is trying to accomplish — functional (get from A to B), social (impress friends), or emotional (gain peace of mind) |
| **Pains** | What annoys customers while trying to get the job done — frustrations, risks, obstacles, dissatisfactions with current solutions |
| **Gains** | How customers measure success — positive outcomes, benefits, aspirations |

**The Value Map (left side — the square):**

| Component | What It Captures |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Products and services** | What you offer |
| **Pain relievers** | How your offering eliminates, reduces, or minimizes customer pains |
| **Gain creators** | How your offering produces, increases, or maximizes customer gains |

**The fit:** You achieve it when there is a clear connect between what matters to customers (the profile) and how your products ease pains and create gains (the map).

> **Professor:** "The jobs to be done is what comes in your MRD — the Market Requirement Document. The gains and pains that your product relieves and creates are part of your PRD — the Product Requirement Document. Your PRD has to fit the MRD."

This connects the Value Proposition Canvas directly to the product management process covered in earlier sessions: the problem space (MRD) feeds into the solution space (PRD), and the value proposition canvas is where you check whether the two actually align.

## Geoffrey Moore's Six-Line Value Proposition Statement

This is the culmination of the entire session. Once you have identified your differentiators, mapped your buyer utility, and built your value proposition canvas, you need to distill everything into a crisp, shareable statement. The template comes from **Geoffrey Moore**, author of *Crossing the Chasm*.

The professor played a video clip of Moore himself explaining the framework. Here it is:

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Geoffrey Moore's **six-line value proposition statement** is the session's culmination and the most likely thing you will be asked to *write*. Memorize the six openers in order: **FOR** (target customer), **WHO** (their problem), **OUR PRODUCT IS** (generic category), **THAT PROVIDES** (primary benefit), **UNLIKE** (a real competitor), **OUR PRODUCT** (your key differentiation). It is created *during* development to align the team — expect to draft one for WattMan II and III.
:::

| Line | Starts With | What You Write |
|------|------------|----------------|
| 1 | **FOR** | Your target customer (segment or persona) |
| 2 | **WHO** | Their specific problem or need (the state they are in) |
| 3 | **OUR PRODUCT IS** | A generic description of your product or service (think "which aisle in the supermarket" — you are not differentiating yet) |
| 4 | **THAT PROVIDES** | Your primary benefit — the breakthrough capability, the answer to their problem |
| 5 | **UNLIKE** | Your closest *real* competitor (a legitimate alternative the customer should be thinking about — not a strawman) |
| 6 | **OUR PRODUCT** | Your key point of competitive differentiation (not "we are better" — rather, "they are good for X, we are good for Y") |

> **Professor:** "It is not just one statement. It is actually these six lines that we use in today's world."

### Why Each Line Matters

- **Line 1 (FOR)** forces you to pick a target. If you say "everyone," you have said nothing.
- **Line 2 (WHO)** grounds you in the customer's pain. If you cannot articulate the problem, you do not understand your customer.
- **Line 3 (OUR PRODUCT IS)** prevents premature differentiation. First, tell people what category you are in so they know where to mentally file you.
- **Line 4 (THAT PROVIDES)** is your value promise. This is what the customer actually buys.
- **Line 5 (UNLIKE)** shows you understand the competitive landscape. Picking a weak competitor here undermines your credibility.
- **Line 6 (OUR PRODUCT)** is your final punch — the reason to choose you over that legitimate alternative.

This six-line document is shared internally with the product team *during* development — not after. It keeps everyone aligned on who you are building for, what problem you are solving, and why your approach is different.

> **Professor:** "When we are in the problem space and creating the product vision, one of the first outcomes of that solution space has to be the value proposition statement. There needs to be clarity within the team as well as how we are going to convey to the end consumer at this stage of the product development process."

## The Zenatix Case — A Preview

The professor introduced the Zenatix case that will be discussed fully in Session 4. Here is the essential background:

**The Company:** Zenatix, an energy data analytics startup founded in 2013 by three IIT Delhi batchmates — Amarjit Singh, Vishal Bansal, and Rahul Bhalla.

**The Product:** WattMan — an IoT-based hardware product that monitors usage and performance of electrical assets (air conditioners, refrigerators, generators, lights). It uses off-the-shelf sensors connected to a proprietary gateway device (the zSmart Controller), feeds data to a cloud server, and applies AI/ML to generate analytics. Those analytics enable corrective actions to reduce energy consumption and costs.

**The Journey of Pivots:**

| Version | Target Market | What Happened |
|---------|--------------|---------------|
| **WattMan I** | Industrial clients (factories, manufacturing units) | Each industrial setup was unique — different machines, different environments, different needs. No standardized, repeatable product possible. Difficult to scale. |
| **WattMan II** | Commercial buildings (hotels, hospitals, office complexes, malls) | Repeatable product — energy patterns were similar across commercial buildings. But sales were painful: each building's facility management team had to be convinced separately, and they were defensive about their efficiency being questioned. |
| **WattMan III** | Retail chains (banks, fashion brands, restaurants with many outlets) | Game changer. One sale to the central operations head covered hundreds of outlets. Added automated remote control (not just monitoring) and predictive maintenance. Savings estimated at 8-30% of energy costs. |

The professor asked students to come prepared for Session 4 with positioning statements for WattMan 2 and WattMan 3 using the six-line template. The Zenatix case is a perfect vehicle for practicing everything learned in this session — because each pivot required a completely different positioning statement for a different target market.

## Differentiating Products That Are Nearly Identical

A student from the banking industry raised an important question: how do you differentiate financial products when the core benefit (the interest rate) is nearly identical across providers?

The professor's response illustrated how differentiation works at multiple product levels:

1. **Core benefit level** — Nearly impossible to differentiate (all banks offer similar IRRs)
2. **Product framing level** — Same loan can be packaged differently: 80% financing with 20% down payment vs. 100% financing with 4 advance EMIs vs. 100% financing at lower interest with 40% security deposit
3. **Augmentation level** — Services become the differentiator: ICICI Bank was among the first to bring loan processing to the customer's desk instead of making them come to the branch

He also mentioned Swiggy and Zomato: two platforms delivering the same core benefit (food delivery) but Swiggy differentiated through ease, flexibility, and portfolio — proving that even near-commodity products can find meaningful differentiation.

> **Professor:** "Differentiating a product which is close to commoditization is not easy. It is definitely a challenge. But we have to look for ways, and it varies from industry to industry."

## Connecting It All: The Product Management Process So Far

The professor paused to tie together the journey across sessions:

1. **Problem Space** — Customer insight, identifying pains and jobs to be done (Session 1-2)
2. **MRD** — Market Requirement Document capturing the problem (Session 2)
3. **Solution Space** — Converting the MRD into a product vision and PRD
4. **Value Proposition Statement** — One of the *first* outcomes of entering the solution space (Session 3)
5. **Buyer Utility Map** — A tool to validate that your differentiation is real
6. **Value Proposition Canvas** — Ensuring your PRD (pain relievers + gain creators) fits your MRD (jobs + pains + gains)

The value proposition statement is not something you write after the product is built. It is created *during* the product development process and shared with the internal team to ensure everyone is building toward the same positioning.

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **Positioning** | Designing your product's offering and image to occupy a distinct, meaningful slot in the customer's mind |
| **Competitive Differentiation** | Designing meaningful differences to distinguish your offering from competitors across five dimensions |
| **IDSCPAP** | Mnemonic for the seven tests a differentiator must pass: Important, Distinctive, Superior, Communicable, Pre-emptive, Affordable, Profitable |
| **Buyer Utility Map** | A 6x6 matrix (buyer experience stages vs. utility levers) to spot where your product creates unique value |
| **Value Proposition Canvas** | A framework matching customer jobs/pains/gains with your product's pain relievers and gain creators |
| **Value Proposition Statement** | Geoffrey Moore's six-line template: FOR, WHO, OUR PRODUCT IS, THAT PROVIDES, UNLIKE, OUR PRODUCT |
| **Value-Based Pricing** | Pricing based on the value you create for the customer, not on your cost of production |
| **Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)** | What the customer is trying to accomplish — functional, social, or emotional |
| **MRD** | Market Requirement Document — captures the problem space (customer needs) |
| **PRD** | Product Requirement Document — captures the solution space (how the product addresses needs) |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Meera had been staring at the same slide for forty minutes. The slide said "NutriTrack: AI-Powered Nutrition for Everyone" and she hated every word of it. Her startup had built a genuinely clever product — a wearable patch that tracked blood glucose, hydration, and micronutrient levels in real time, feeding data to an app that suggested dietary changes. The technology worked. The pilot users loved it. But every investor pitch ended the same way: polite nodding, no cheque.

Her co-founder Ravi walked in with two coffees and said what he always said: "The product is great. We just need better marketing." Meera was starting to suspect the problem was not marketing. The problem was that she could not explain, in one clean breath, who this product was for and why it was better than alternatives.

She pulled out a blank sheet and wrote six lines.

FOR busy professionals managing early-stage Type 2 diabetes WHO need to understand how daily food choices affect their blood sugar in real time, OUR PRODUCT IS a continuous health-monitoring wearable and companion app THAT PROVIDES personalized, data-driven dietary guidance based on the user's actual metabolic response to meals, UNLIKE traditional glucose monitors that only show a number without context, OUR PRODUCT correlates glucose readings with food intake, hydration, and activity to recommend specific, actionable meal adjustments.

She read it back to herself. Then she crossed out "AI-Powered Nutrition for Everyone" from the old slide. "Everyone" was the problem. She was not building for everyone. She was building for a specific person with a specific frustration — the forty-five-year-old office worker whose doctor had said "watch your diet" but never explained what that actually meant at lunch on a Tuesday.

The next pitch went differently. Instead of opening with the technology stack, Meera opened with the customer: "Imagine you have just been told you are pre-diabetic. Your doctor says eat better. You Google it and get fourteen contradictory articles. Our product tells you — based on your own body's data, not a generic chart — that the rice you had for lunch spiked your glucose for three hours but the dal did not. Over time, it builds a picture of what works for you specifically."

The investor leaned forward. Not because the technology had changed — it had not. But because for the first time, Meera was not describing a product. She was describing a *position*: a clear space in the customer's mind where NutriTrack lived and no competitor currently sat.

She got the term sheet two days later. Ravi, to his credit, stopped saying "we just need better marketing." The marketing had always been fine. What they had needed was positioning — the discipline of deciding, before a single ad runs, exactly who you are for, what problem you solve, and why you and not someone else.

**A product without positioning is a solution still looking for its sentence.**
