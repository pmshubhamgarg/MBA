# Session 5: The Eight Gates — How New Products Are Actually Built (and Why 72% Still Fail)

*From a stroke-of-genius idea to a shelf full of product — the disciplined, humbling, failure-ridden process of building something that did not exist yesterday*

---

## Green-Field vs. Iterating: Two Ways to Make Something New

Session 5 switches gears from positioning to *building*. The professor drew the fork in the road at the very start. With an **existing product**, new development is driven by the **funnel** we saw in the redBus case: wherever the funnel suddenly drops — wherever users fall away — you improve the product or deepen engagement to lift that stage. That is **iteration**.

But what about **green-fielding** — starting from scratch, with nothing? A student (Chirag) sketched the intuitive answer: gather market requirements, scope the development, estimate effort, decide what existing technology to reuse versus build new, allocate resources, plan the program. Another (Sentil, in chat) mapped it to the classic SDLC — requirement analysis with market-gap analysis, then development, testing, implementation, maintenance, and feedback. Both were right, and the professor built on them to give an all-encompassing framework that works for online *and* offline products.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "Many of us are working in online space, digital space, as well as offline space. So I've tried to give you an all-encompassing framework, and you may work in subsets of it."

## Six Kinds of "New" — and Why They Are NOT Strategies

Not every new product is an earth-shattering breakthrough. A product can be new to the *market*, or merely new to the *company* — and if it is new to the company, that company still has to gear up to make it. On that basis, new products fall into **six categories**:

| Category | What It Means | Example from Class |
|----------|--------------|--------------------|
| **New-to-the-world** | Creates a market that did not exist | ChatGPT, BlackBerry's email phone, Sony Walkman/Discman, Apple iPod and iPhone |
| **New product lines** | Lets a company enter an established market for the first time | Unilever's Close-Up gel toothpaste (they only made soaps/detergents before); Telco/Tata entering passenger cars; Tata into retail and jewellery via Titan/Tanishq |
| **Additions to existing lines** | Supplements an established line | Maruti adding hatchbacks; Tata Motors adding Punch, Nexon, Tigor, Tiago |
| **Improvements / revisions** | Better performance and higher CDV, replacing the old version | Any software product improving through iterations |
| **Repositioning** | Aiming an existing product at a new market or segment | Hamam soap; Bajaj Caliber → Caliber Croma → "Hoodibaba"; ChatGPT from conversational to logical LLM |
| **Cost reductions** | Similar performance at lower cost | Maruti 800 holding its price point 1995–2001; Parle-G; Cadbury Dairy Milk |

The professor was emphatic — twice, firmly — about one thing when students started calling these "six strategies":

> **Professor Banerjee:** "No, no, no. They are not strategies. I'm just saying new products could fall into one of these categories. A strategy is a *method to fulfil an objective*. The objective here is that we have identified a gap in the market — an unfulfilled need — and we are trying to plug it. That need could be filled in various ways."

**CDV** in the table stands for **Customer Delivered Value** (also called customer perceived value) — the value the customer actually receives, which a genuine improvement must raise.

### The Hamam Repositioning — Why Repositioning Still Needs Product Change

A student pressed on repositioning: if you only change the market, not the product, is it really new product development? The professor used **Hamam soap** to show that repositioning almost always drags *some* product change with it.

Until 1996, Hamam was marketed as a greenish **beauty bar** by Tata Oil Mills (TOMCO). When **Unilever acquired the brand**, it hit a conflict: Unilever already had **Lux** positioned as a beauty bar, and Lux was its own, better-selling product. Rather than cannibalize Lux, Unilever **repositioned Hamam as a herbal soap** — a "sudata sabun" built on the mother-and-child, gentle-and-safe platform, carefully kept clear of Rexona's territory too. The colour stayed roughly green, the name stayed Hamam, but the ingredients were genuinely **herbalized**. New market, new positioning — and real product development underneath.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "While targeting a new market, why should that new market purchase our product? We have to give them a reason to buy. That reason comes through the modification in the product."

## The Spine of the Session: The Stage-Gate Model

To reduce the risk of new-product failure, NPD follows a structured, gated process. This is the framework to know cold.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The **Stage-Gate model** has **eight stages**: (1) Idea Generation → (2) Idea Screening → (3) Concept Development & Testing → (4) Marketing Strategy Development → (5) Business Analysis → (6) Product Development → (7) Market Testing → (8) Commercialization. The whole point is to **drop poor ideas as early and as cheaply as possible**, because cost rises steeply at each later stage. A framework is needed precisely because teams work together and everyone must know *which gate a product idea currently sits at*.
:::

> **Professor Banerjee:** "To reduce the risk of new-product failure, new product development follows a structured process."

Let us walk each gate.

---

## Stage 1: Idea Generation

New products are not just a stroke of genius. Ideas are hunted systematically from many **sources** — customers, employees and staff, R&D, engineering, competition, marketing channels and their staff, distribution channels, senior management, and **lead users**. The team scans these groups with survey forms, then focus-group discussions (FGDs), then a few expert or depth interviews to probe further, and compiles a list of ideas.

But *how* do you squeeze ideas out of those people? Six techniques:

| Technique | What It Does | Class Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| **Attribute listing** | List a product's attributes, then try to improve each | Refrigerator "uniform cooling" → **polyurethane foam (PUF)** in the walls; Godrej introduced "refrigerators with PUF" in India. For an app: improving screen-to-screen responsiveness |
| **Forced relationships** | Combine separate objects into one product | The smartphone — call + SMS, then camera, then email, then Wi-Fi and internet, all forced onto one motherboard; embedding IoT into a platform |
| **Morphological analysis** | Examine structural dimensions of a problem and their relationships | The **moped** = a motorized bicycle (Kinetic **Luna**, Luna Plus, Luna TFR) — convenient, economical, effortless |
| **Customer need / problem identification** | Research where customers are dissatisfied; the dissatisfaction points to a new product | **Dettol stings** when applied → the idea for a non-stinging antiseptic → **Savlon** |
| **Brainstorming** | 6–10 people, no criticism, free flow, quantity over quality, combine and improve | The non-transferable lipstick (below) |
| **Lead user studies** | Experts passionate about a category who help design and test | Enthusiasts in automobiles, skincare, food — often the alpha/beta testers |

A telling twist on the Dettol case: a segment of customers actually *believe* that unless an antiseptic burns, it is not healing. So even though Savlon "solved" the sting, **Dettol kept its dominance** — because for some buyers the burn *is* the benefit. A perfect reminder that customer psychology, not logic, decides adoption.

:::note[Good to Know]
**The non-transferable lipstick.** The professor once ran a brainstorm with women who use lipstick daily. One elegantly dressed participant mentioned she avoided drinking tea or coffee in company — because her lip mark transferred to the cup and she felt self-conscious about others noticing it. That single confession, surfaced only because a brainstorm digs past the obvious, gave birth to the **non-transferable lipstick** — a product that does not leave a mark on the cup. Great ideas often hide inside small human embarrassments.
:::

### The Ritual and the Ritual Artifact

A student (Shivangi) shared a real nugget: after talking to six or seven critical customers who all described their problems *differently*, her team realized one solution could serve all of them. The professor gave this its technical name. When customers repeatedly use a product to do a job, that repeated behaviour is a **ritual**, and the product becomes a **ritual artifact**. A single product can be an artifact for *multiple* rituals.

His example was **Maggi noodles**:

- A **tasty snack** for school children (the primary target).
- A **quick breakfast** for a working professional rushing out the door.
- A **light dinner** for elderly couples living on their own.

Same product, three different rituals, three different target groups — which is exactly how a product widens its audience. He sharpened one word here: the **need** is the same (satisfy hunger), but the **wants** differ (children want many flavours and add-ins; the elderly stick to one; professionals want speed). Product development lives in that nuance between need and want, which is why variants exist.

---

## Stage 2: Idea Screening

Now the list gets brutal. Ideas are sorted — by a team, with some subjectivity — into **promising, marginal, and rejects**. Only promising ideas advance. The objective is to **drop poor ideas at the earliest**, and the danger is making one of two opposite mistakes.

:::tip[Important]
- A **drop error** is rejecting a *good* idea — you only discover your mistake later, when a competitor launches a product built on it.
- A **go error** is letting a *poor* idea proceed into development and commercialization — which burns real money, because product development is expensive.

Screening exists to minimize both. Surviving ideas run through a **product-idea rating process**: each idea is described in detail (the idea, target market, competition/reference frame, estimated market size, price, development time and cost, manufacturing cost, rate of return) and scored against criteria — does it meet a need, offer superior CDV and price-performance, can it be distinctly positioned, and does it fit company objectives, strategy, and resources?
:::

---

## Stage 3: Concept Development & Testing

This is the gate most people blur, so the professor slowed down. There is a difference between an idea, a concept, and an image.

:::tip[Important]
- A **product idea** is the raw starting point for a green-field product.
- A **product concept** is that idea elaborated into meaningful *consumer* terms — detailed enough that a customer can understand it before it is built.
- A **product image** is the picture the consumer actually forms of the product.

One student offered "idea is a soul, concept is a skeleton"; the professor gently reframed it: the idea is the **skeleton**, the concept is the **flesh and blood**, and the final product is fine-tuned flesh and blood in the real world.
:::

He made this unforgettable with the **"nutritious, tasty powder"** exercise. A food company wants to make such a powder. What could that become? The class rattled off dozens: Bournvita, Boost, Complan, Horlicks, whey protein, powdered milk, Cerelac; Knorr soup powder; Rasna and Tang soft-drink powders; powdered sugar (*pisi shakkar*); Gits gulab-jamun mix; idli, dosa, and vada mixes; besan; atta; MTR and Pillsbury ready-to-cook mixes. **One idea, an enormous range of possible products.** That is exactly why you must convert the idea into a *specific* concept.

**How to convert an idea into a concept — three questions:**

1. **Who** will use the product? (children, working professionals, elderly, sportspeople)
2. **What** primary benefit should it provide? (taste, nutrition, convenience, energy, recovery)
3. **When / for what ritual** will it be consumed? (breakfast, snack, dinner, rejuvenation)

Pick one answer from each and you get a concept. For instance: *Concept 1 — an instant breakfast for working professionals; Concept 2 — a tasty snack for children; Concept 3 — a light lunch/dinner for the elderly.* Each concept locates the idea inside a **category**, which fixes the **reference frame** and the **competition** (Concept 1 competes with cornflakes, parathas, idlis) and therefore the **price points**.

You then plot a **product positioning map**, taking two attributes at a time (say price vs. speed), to find the empty rectangle where a differentiated product can live. Finally you **test the concept** — still on paper or screen, not built — on dimensions such as communicability, believability, need level, gap, difference from existing products, perceived value, price reasonableness, and purchase intent. Consumer preferences across competing concepts are teased out with **conjoint analysis**, which surfaces the trade-offs: if a higher feature level costs more, would the customer pay, or would they rather sacrifice the feature for a lower price?

## Stages 4 & 5: Marketing Strategy and Business Analysis

For concepts that survive, you develop a **marketing strategy** in three parts:

- **Part A:** target market size, structure, and behaviour; planned positioning; sales, market-share, and profit objectives for the next 2–3 years.
- **Part B:** how you reach that market — the product itself, its form/shape/pack sizes, price, distribution, and marketing budgets. *This is the go-to-market strategy — the subject of next week's Dropbox case.*
- **Part C:** long-term sales and profit goals, and the marketing-mix strategy over time.

Then comes **business analysis**. New products need investment — angel funding, venture capital, a term loan from a bank or an NBFC (the professor named **LenDenClub**, Bajaj Finance). Loans carry a **tenure**, often with a one-year **moratorium** (a "1 + 5" repayment plan). You project sales, costs, and profits over about five years and match them to company objectives. Only if the business case holds does the concept move forward — **and note: from stages 4 and 5 onward, the concept itself is not changed.** Whatever came out of concept testing is what you carry ahead; stages 4 and 5 plan the money and the market around it, they do not re-open the concept.

---

## Stage 6: Product Development — Where the Product Gets Flesh and Blood

Now the concept goes to R&D, engineering, and the product-development team to become a physical (or digital) product — a **prototype** or an **MVP**. This is also where **Agile Scrum enters the process** (a student asked, and the professor confirmed: Scrum comes in at stage 6, *after* the PRD is written — everything before that is on paper or screen).

**Alpha testing** happens here: first for psychological aspects (colour, look, style, price, fitment), then for functionality and effectiveness (is it doing what it is supposed to?), then for safety. Only once management is satisfied with functional and psychological performance is the product ready for market testing.

### A Detour into Extreme Programming

A student (Manish) asked about **Extreme Programming (XP)** and Feature-Driven Development from the assigned reading, *Product Development Fundamentals* (Iansiti, Toffel, Herman & Kelley, HBS). The professor kept it practical and offered a memorable brick-and-mortar analogy.

Imagine your product works beautifully across 25 use cases, but during **user acceptance** six *new* use cases emerge. Do you restart the whole design, or quickly write code to slot those six in? That quick, targeted response is where XP lives — an accelerated form of Agile for when user acceptance throws up demands you must satisfy fast. It relies on people who can do **impact analysis** — the old "programmer analyst" who understands the ripple effect of a code change on the product's usability.

> **Professor Banerjee's analogy:** The Bajaj Pulsar is quick but can feel accident-prone at speed. So engineers added an **engine kill switch** on the right handlebar — a flick of the thumb kills the engine for a fast stop. It adds no new general functionality; it exists for one specific, previously unplanned use case. "The engine kill switch is extreme programming."

For accuracy, the reading defines these Agile sub-methods precisely:

| Method | Definition (from the reading) |
|--------|------------------------------|
| **Scrum** | A lightweight framework; a team plus a Scrum Master work in short **sprints**, with frequent meetings to review progress. Iterative and incremental |
| **Extreme Programming (XP)** | A "no-nonsense, code-first" approach built on four activities — **coding, testing, listening, designing** — that brought testing to the forefront |
| **Feature-Driven Development** | For *larger* teams; a five-step process (develop a model, build a features list, plan / design / build by feature) with "just enough process to ensure scalability" |

The professor was careful not to over-teach software engineering — "my mandate is strictly to stay on product management" — but the point stands: today's **technical product manager** needs enough XP exposure to enable fast responses during user acceptance, which he called "quite a challenge."

## Stage 7: Market Testing

Before a full launch, you test the product in a real market setting to learn actual market size and how consumers and dealers use, handle, and repurchase it. Four methods:

| Method | How It Works |
|--------|-------------|
| **Sales-wave research** | Offer free samples, then repeat 3–5 times with a small discount, noting how many buy again. **Amul used this** for milk in parts of Maharashtra to study whether it deteriorated — the origin of its long-life cartons |
| **Simulated test marketing** | Qualified shoppers see ads (including for the new product), get shopping coupons, and their buying reveals trial rates; non-buyers get free samples and are later surveyed on satisfaction and repurchase |
| **Controlled test marketing** | Run by a market-research firm, often without a full label, with only local advertising, to gauge who would consider the product |
| **Test markets (best method)** | Actually launch in a few cities or with a customer group — effectively **beta testing** — and monitor real sales and the marketing mix. Negative results mean redesign first, drop only rarely |

He also explained the **"bitten apple"** approach to launch timing: let someone else bite the apple first (test the market); if the apple is bad, they bear the brunt, and you follow only if it is good.

---

## Stage 8: Commercialization

After successful market testing, production ramps up rapidly and the product is formally launched. The four launch decisions: **when** to launch, **where** to launch, **to whom** to launch, and **how** to launch.

## Why 72% of New Products Still Fail

A student asked the question every founder fears: why do most startups fail in their first six months? The professor's honesty was the lesson.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "I wish I knew. 72% of new products fail, as per a study. It's not easy — customers are very demanding. We try to *increase the chances of success*, but guaranteeing success, I don't know. This process makes us more and more humble."

Two forces make it hard. First, **customers keep evolving**: once quick-commerce delivered milk in ten minutes, that became the new baseline — and one spoiled delivery now earns a complaint that the old, watery *doodhwala* never would have. Second, **products cannot change as fast as customers do** — it takes real time to build and respond.

His cautionary cases: **Tata Indica** took advance bookings, then cancellations, because early buyers were unhappy; the Indica V2 did reasonably well but the original's image still lingers. The **Tata Nano** was test-marketed to lukewarm feedback, and the redesign pushed its price past the promised one-lakh mark — which is exactly why the professor warns against **putting a price into your positioning**: it pins you to a number you may not be able to hold, and the price *will* rise over time.

The antidote is the **product pipeline**. A product manager should have several ideas maturing at *different* stages simultaneously — some at commercialization, some in development, some in concept testing, some in idea generation — so that, hopefully, one or two of six ideas bring in real revenue. A PM, he added, is "stretched and stressed," and must delegate to a strong team (a great database administrator, a strong UI person, a good data scientist) and personally plug whatever gap remains.

:::tip[Important]
The assigned HBR reading **"Six Myths of Product Development" (Thomke & Reinertsen, 2012)** formalizes exactly the humility the professor preached. Two of its six fallacies map directly onto class discussion: **"the more features we put in, the more customers will like it"** (why he pushed conjoint analysis and warned against over-featuring) and **"we will be more successful if we get it right the first time"** (why NPD is gated, iterative, and failure-tolerant). The other four: high resource utilization improves performance; large batches improve economics; a great plan just needs to be followed; and starting sooner finishes sooner. All six are traps that make the 72% failure rate worse, not better.
:::

## Where Should a Product Manager Spend Their Attention?

A student asked which of the eight stages a PM should focus on most. The professor refused to name one stage — and answered with a school analogy instead. In Class 10, where should you spend study time? Not on the subjects you already ace (97% in maths), but where the **scope for improvement is greatest** (60% in languages), because it is the *overall* percentage that matters, not the sectional marks.

The same logic applies to NPD: whatever stage a given product idea is *currently* at is where that idea needs the PM's attention. And because a PM runs not one idea but a **pipeline**, the honest answer is "all of them, at once."

> **Professor Banerjee:** "You build a pipeline — some ideas at test-marketing and commercialization, some at product development, some at business analysis, some at concept testing, some at idea generation. If you have six product ideas maturing at different stages, hopefully one or two will bring in decent revenue and profit."

Because the PM is "stretched and stressed," success also depends on **delegating to a strong team** — a solid database administrator here, a strong UI designer there, a capable data scientist for the analytics — and personally plugging whatever gap remains.

### Impact Analysis in the Real World: A Legacy Migration

A student (Pratik) shared a story that made the process concrete. His bank ran a credit-dispute-resolution system built in 2000 on Oracle and Forms 3.0, originally written as a single text tag. Fifteen-plus years later, migrating it to the cloud was not a simple port: it fractured into many smaller projects, the team grew from 20 to nearly 70 people, and specialized tools were bolted on for each sub-problem. Worse, a vendor tool the bank had trained developers on for six months was **red-flagged by the bank's own security and compliance servers** — and scrapped after eleven months, forcing another route entirely.

The professor's point: this is why **impact analysis** — understanding how a change in architecture or code ripples through the product's usability, effectiveness, and timelines — is the soul of product development, and why a migration estimated at 13 months can stretch to 27 and *still* leave gaps. "A lot of failure, a lot of trauma, a lot of anxiety. Not easy. But that doesn't mean we should not try."

## Recipe Chain

**Session 5 gives you the engine of New Product Development:**
New products fall into six categories (from new-to-the-world to cost reductions) but are all built through the eight-gate **Stage-Gate model** — idea generation, idea screening (avoiding drop and go errors), concept development and testing (idea → concept → image), marketing strategy, business analysis, product development (where Agile/Scrum and XP live), market testing, and commercialization. Ideas come from many sources via techniques like brainstorming and forced relationships; concepts are tested on paper before a rupee is spent building; and even a perfect process only *reduces* the 72% failure rate, because customers keep evolving.

**Next up:** The afternoon session applies this to the **LenDenClub** peer-to-peer lending case — concept-testing the small-ticket products **InstaMoney** vs. **Kwik Money**, then running **A/B tests** (a "before-after with control" design, where A and B act as controls for each other) on the winning concept, analyzed with a **t-test in Excel**. **Next week** moves to Stage 4's go-to-market strategy through the **Dropbox** case.

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **Stage-Gate Model** | The eight-stage NPD process from idea generation to commercialization, gated to drop poor ideas early |
| **Product Idea** | The raw starting point for a green-field product (the "skeleton") |
| **Product Concept** | The idea elaborated into meaningful consumer terms, locating it in a category (the "flesh and blood") |

| **Go Error** | Letting a poor idea proceed into costly development and commercialization |
| **CDV** | Customer Delivered Value (customer perceived value) — the value the customer actually receives |
| **Conjoint Analysis** | A method to reveal customer trade-offs between feature levels and the price they will pay |

| **Market Testing** | Testing a product in a real market (sales-wave, simulated, controlled, or test markets) before full launch |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Ananya had a powder. Specifically, she had a jar of a protein-rich millet powder her grandmother used to mix into warm milk, and a conviction — bordering on religion — that it would sell. She had quit her consulting job, incorporated a company, printed pouches, and built a website, all in eleven weeks. Her pitch to friends was simple: "It's a nutritious, tasty powder. Everyone eats. Everyone wants to be healthy. The market is *everyone*."

Her first mentor, a grey-haired ex-FMCG hand named Mr. Rao, listened to all of this and asked one question: "Who will use it, what benefit do they want, and when will they eat it?" Ananya said, "Everyone, health, anytime." Mr. Rao smiled the way people smile at a puppy. "You have an idea," he said. "You do not have a concept."

He walked her through the powder that could be a hundred products. Her millet powder could be a baby food, a post-workout shake, a diabetic-friendly breakfast, an elderly-nutrition drink, a festive sweet mix, or a soup base — each a different customer, a different benefit, a different ritual, a different shelf, a different competitor, a different price. She had been building for all of them, which meant she had been building for none of them.

So Ananya did the unglamorous thing. She stopped printing pouches and went back to Stage 1. She ran a small brainstorm with new mothers and heard, buried in a complaint about mixing lumps, the real job: "I don't have ninety seconds in the morning." She screened her twelve ideas down to three, killing a "protein millet energy bar" she'd loved — a small drop error she could live with — because the economics were hopeless. She wrote three concepts and tested them on paper with forty people before spending anything on manufacturing. The winner was not the one she'd been in love with. It was an *instant, single-serve breakfast mix for time-starved young parents* — no lumps, ninety seconds, one benefit, one ritual.

She test-marketed it in two neighbourhoods with free samples repeated over a month. Repurchase held. Only then did she commercialize — one product, one customer, one clean sentence.

The company that eventually did well was almost unrecognizable from the one she'd incorporated in eleven weeks. The powder was nearly the same. Everything around it — the who, the what, the when — had gone through eight gates.

**A product idea is a lottery ticket; a product concept is a decision. The Stage-Gate model is just the discipline of deciding before you spend.**
