# Session 1: The Conductor Without a Baton — What Product Managers Actually Do

*Why the most influential person in a tech company often has zero formal authority -- and how they get things done anyway*

---

## Setting the Stage: What Is This Course About?

Product management is one of those roles that everyone talks about but few people can define crisply. This 10-session course, taught by Professor Pranto Banerjee, takes a practitioner's approach: conceptual clarity first, then contextual application through cases. The professor calls this the **CCCA method** -- Conceptual Clarity followed by Contextual Applicability.

The course arc looks like this:

| Sessions | Topic | Case |
|----------|-------|------|
| 1-2 | Introduction, PM roles, key concepts | redBus |
| 3 | Product strategy and positioning | Zenetics |
| 4-5 | New product development and A/B testing | Lane Dane Club |
| 6-7 | Go-to-market strategy | Dropbox |
| 8-10 | Product adoption and diffusion | Nexent Systems |

> **Professor Banerjee:** "I cannot offer you a sure-shot formula for success. Apart from saying that you need to iterate, you need to be resilient. Iteration is part of it. And you keep on iterating -- with patience."

The course leans toward **digital product management** -- apps, platforms, online services -- because that is where the growth opportunities and flatter organizational hierarchies are. But as the class discussion showed, the principles apply just as well to physical products, from automotive components to soap.

**Important:** No prior product management or technology experience is required. This is a managerial course, not a coding bootcamp.

## The Product Manager: Entrepreneur, Strategist, and Customer Advocate -- All Without the Title

At its core, a product manager (PM) is responsible for three things:

1. **Defining** the product
2. **Managing** its development, launch, and improvement
3. **Representing the customer** inside the company

That third one is the one that makes or breaks careers.

### The Customer Advocacy Problem

The PM is the voice of the customer within the firm. But the PM is also a human being with opinions. And that creates a dangerous trap: sometimes a PM gets so attached to their own vision of the product that they miss what customers actually need.

The professor illustrated this with **Zomato vs. Swiggy**. Zomato launched around 2007. Swiggy came much later, around 2014-2016. Yet many users report that Swiggy feels easier to use -- the UX is cleaner, the navigation more intuitive, and the range of services (food, Instamart, etc.) more convenient. Despite being the first mover, Zomato did not lock up the user experience advantage.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "You may get into an existing space, but if you understand the customer better and you're able to see the customer's point of view better, the visualization of the product gets enhanced. The product vision is much better, and that leads to bigger success in the marketplace."

The lesson: **first-mover advantage means nothing if a later entrant understands the customer more deeply than you do.**

## Responsibility Without Authority: The PM's Core Paradox

Here is the single most important thing to understand about being a product manager: **you have enormous responsibility but almost no formal authority.** The engineers do not report to you. The designers do not report to you. The marketing team does not report to you. Yet you are expected to coordinate all of them toward a single product vision.

### The Paint Shop Story

The professor shared a personal anecdote from his time working with a motorcycle manufacturer (likely Hero Motors). The paint shop was painting motorcycles in only **one color per shift** -- two shifts a day meant only two colors available. The reason? Switching colors required cleaning the spray nozzles, which wasted paint. The production head's metric was efficiency, so he optimized for paint utilization.

But from a product perspective, this was a disaster. Imagine a young buyer walking into a showroom and seeing only two color options. So the professor needed the paint shop to produce **eight colors per day**.

He had zero authority over the production head. When he raised the idea, the production head "threw him out" and took him on a tour of the paint shop to show him why it was impossible. But during that tour, the professor noticed the paint shop had **partitioned stalls**. He innocently asked: could they not run two colors simultaneously in different partitions?

Two days later, they were doing two colors per shift. A week later, he suggested another partition. Result: **eight colors per day** -- a solution so simple that the one-time cost of a partition wall solved it.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "As a product manager, we don't take any credit. It just doesn't matter whether the other person takes the credit. We are fine with it, so long as it happens."

They gave the production head a certificate for the achievement. The PM got nothing -- and that was exactly the right outcome.

**The takeaway:** A PM's job is to make the right thing happen, not to get credit for it. Influence without authority. Suggestion without command. Reward the people who execute.

## The PM as Orchestra Conductor

The professor used a vivid analogy: the product manager is like the **conductor of an orchestra**.

Does the conductor need to know how to play every instrument? Yes and no. They do not need to be a virtuoso on the violin or the cello. But they must understand enough about each instrument to know what role it should play, when it should come in, and how the whole thing fits together.

He pointed to **Ennio Morricone**, the legendary composer who scored "The Good, the Bad and the Ugly." Morricone created the film's iconic theme by simulating desert sounds -- wind, howling, whistling -- using a massive orchestra. If you watch him conduct in the studio, you see the artistry of coordination: dozens of musicians, each playing their part, producing something none of them could produce alone.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "The product manager is somewhat of that sort -- a conductor of an orchestra. The only thing is, in addition to that, the conductor has to ensure that the voice of the customer is reflected. And it has to be a business success. So there's additional pressure."

## Seven Core Responsibilities of a Product Manager

The professor walked through a comprehensive list that he asked students to screenshot for their phones:

| Responsibility | What It Involves |
|----------------|-----------------|
| **Product Strategy** | Defining long-term vision, translating business goals into a cohesive product strategy, creating a product roadmap -- all documented, not just in your head |
| **Customer & Market Understanding** | Conducting customer research, market analysis, competitive analysis; identifying opportunities for improvement |
| **Requirement Definition** | Translating Market Requirements Documents (MRDs) into Product Requirements Documents (PRDs) |
| **Cross-Functional Collaboration** | Working with engineering, design, marketing, sales, and support; aligning everyone on product goals |
| **Product Development Lifecycle** | Overseeing the product from conception to implementation; planning what goes into each launch and what gets held back |
| **Product Roadmapping** | Outlining future features and releases based on strategic goals and customer feedback |
| **Commercial Success** | Managing the business case, product profitability, and overall commercial success |

### The Document Chain: MRD to PRD

This is one of the trickiest handoffs in product management. The **Market Requirements Document (MRD)** captures what customers need -- it is written from the outside in. The **Product Requirements Document (PRD)** specifies what the engineering team should build -- it is written from the inside out.

The danger zone is the translation. What the MRD team means and what the development team understands can be two very different things. If the gap is not caught, the product that gets built may not match what the customer actually needed.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "The MRD is created by one team and the development team works on the PRD. In the translation, there may be gaps. You mean it in a particular way, but it is understood in a different way. So the product gets developed differently, and the result is not very good."

## Types of Product Managers

Product management is not one-size-fits-all. The professor outlined four types, plus a fifth that was contributed by a student:

| Type | Focus Area |
|------|-----------|
| **Digital Product Manager** | Manages entire digital product lifecycle and strategy |
| **Data Product Manager** | Concentrates on products that leverage data (e.g., AI models, analytics tools) |
| **UX/Product Design Manager** | Leads design teams to ensure products are user-friendly and intuitive |
| **Product Marketing Manager** | Defines value proposition, messaging, and go-to-market strategy |
| **Technical Product Manager** | Manages product engineering for development teams based on internal customer requirements |

### The Technical PM: A Student's Insight

A student named Sarang shared an important distinction. Take PhonePe as an example. The **digital PM** handles the user-facing workflow -- login, scan QR code, enter UPI PIN. But the moment you hit "enter," there is an entire backend infrastructure: cloud systems, banking connections, payment gateways, compliance requirements (PCI and others), data centers. The person managing that backend product is a **technical PM**.

Their customers are internal -- engineering teams and non-engineering teams alike. They deal with more technically complex problems and more opinionated stakeholders. The professor immediately added this to the slides.

> **Professor Banerjee compared it to the human body:** "We have arteries, veins, and capillaries. Where an arterial capillary becomes a venal capillary -- that interface is what the technical PM is managing. And it's not easy."

## Product Manager vs. Program Manager vs. Product Owner

This sparked a lively class discussion. Here is the simplification that emerged:

| Role | Scope | Analogy (FMCG) |
|------|-------|----------------|
| **Product Owner** | Owns a single product or component; defines features, prioritizes backlog | Brand Manager (manages Lux or Dove) |
| **Product Manager** | Owns a product category; multiple product owners may report to them | Product Category Manager (manages all soaps) |
| **Program Manager** | Manages a portfolio of products across categories or technologies; handles budgets, timelines, coordination | All-India Sales Manager (manages all products across geographies) |

The professor used a Unilever example to make it concrete. Unilever has brand managers for Lux, Lifeboy, Dove, and so on. They report to a product manager who oversees all soaps. Meanwhile, the area sales team sells everything -- soap, detergent, shampoo -- across a specific territory. The all-India sales manager is like the program manager: managing the entire portfolio.

An interesting trend Sarang noted: **many companies are collapsing the program manager role into the PM role**, aided by AI automation. In his current and previous organizations, there was no separate program manager -- every PM also handled program management responsibilities.

## Problem Space vs. Solution Space: The Key Mental Model

This was the concept the professor was most eager to convey in Session 1, because it directly sets up the **redBus case** in Session 2.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "As a product manager, I try to think: am I working in the problem space right now, or am I in the solution space? I try to keep these two thought processes distinct. Otherwise, I make a mess of everything."

### Problem Space (Understanding What to Solve)

| Step | What You Do |
|------|------------|
| **Customer Research** | Gather data on customer needs, pain points, and preferences (surveys, focus groups, analytics, user testing) |
| **Market Analysis** | Examine the competitive landscape, trends, and gaps to identify opportunities |
| **Product Ideation** | Generate ideas for products or features that could address customer needs |
| **Prioritization** | Evaluate ideas based on customer value, feasibility, and business impact |

### Solution Space (Building the Solution)

| Step | What You Do |
|------|------------|
| **Product Definition** | Create a product vision document, then translate it into a PRD with features, requirements, and specifications |
| **Development** | Design, build, test -- iterating based on feedback from stakeholders |
| **Launch** | Develop go-to-market strategy, pricing, and promotion plan |
| **Post-Launch Evaluation** | Gather feedback, measure performance, make improvements |

The critical discipline: **finish the problem space before jumping to the solution space.** When you try to solve problems while still identifying them, you end up with incremental fixes that are never good enough. Document the problem space thoroughly, then move to solutions with that document as your anchor.

## The Product Life Cycle: A PM Wears Different Hats at Each Stage

The PM's role changes dramatically depending on where the product sits in its life cycle:

| Stage | PM's Primary Focus |
|-------|-------------------|
| **Introduction** | Launching the product, creating awareness, working closely with marketing on GTM strategy, tracking initial sales and feedback |
| **Growth** | Scaling the product, adding new features, building partnerships, managing the budget to ensure profitability |
| **Maturity** | Maintaining market position, maximizing profitability, finding new customer segments (especially important in B2B where existing customers negotiate prices down) |
| **Decline** | Managing end-of-life, analyzing why popularity is declining, learning from the experience to improve future products |

> **Professor Banerjee:** "No product is perfect on day one of the launch. It doesn't happen. I mean, we are not so great that we can design the ideal product on the first day. So we need to have that little bit of humility to understand that we keep on learning."

## The Cross-Functional Tightrope

A PM interacts with **seven different functions**, each with its own priorities and pressures:

| Function | PM's Interaction |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Users & Customers** | Deep understanding of needs; customer research through surveys, focus groups, user testing |
| **Development/Engineering** | Ensuring the product is built to spec, on time, within budget; collaborating on design and testing |
| **Marketing** | Developing GTM strategy, identifying target markets, pricing and distribution decisions |
| **Sales** | Understanding customer feedback from the field; developing aligned sales strategies |
| **Customer Support** | Identifying issues and bugs; ensuring the product meets customer expectations |
| **Finance** | Budgets, cost monitoring, ensuring profitability throughout the lifecycle |
| **Executive Management** | Aligning the product with the company's overall strategy and goals |

## Quick Concepts Introduced (Detailed in Session 2)

The professor briefly mentioned several concepts that will be explored in depth alongside the redBus case:

- **Funnel Analysis** -- Breaking the user journey into stages and analyzing conversion rates at each stage. Where are users dropping off, and why?
- **RICE Framework** -- Feature prioritization using Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort
- **Hypothesis Testing** -- Making assumptions about users, designing experiments, analyzing results
- **Agile Scrum** -- Product backlog, sprint planning, daily standups, sprint reviews, retrospectives
- **A/B Testing** -- Controlled release of features to compare performance between variants

## The Parle-G Biscuit Detour: A Product Management Mini-Case

A student mentioned Parle-G reducing the size of its biscuits to adjust for inflation. The professor, who had worked with the company, filled in the story:

Wheat flour and sugar -- **55% of Parle-G's cost of goods** -- had spiked in price. Margins had fallen from 14% to 9%. The company faced a choice: raise prices (risky for a mass-market product) or reduce quantity.

Changing the biscuit mold would require massive plant and machinery changes. But changing the **number of biscuits per packet** was a packaging problem -- much simpler. They calculated price per gram, ran the analysis across different pack sizes (1 rupee, 2 rupee, 4 rupee, 5 rupee packs), and reduced the count from **17 biscuits to 16 per packet**.

There was some consumer backlash -- what is now called **shrinkflation** -- but it was manageable. The alternative was worse.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "There are always trade-offs. These are hard choices that we have to make."

## Design Thinking vs. Product Management

A student asked how this course relates to design thinking. The professor's answer was illuminating:

**Design thinking** helps you visualize new products and new ways of solving problems. It stops once you have an innovative solution.

**Product management** picks up from there and keeps going. You do not leave the product after giving one solution. You execute, you get customers to adopt it, you iterate, and you keep innovating on what you have already built.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "Design thinking is more romantic. Product management is less romantic. Very down to earth. Very hair-tearing. You'll see -- I don't have any hair left."

A student named Sarang put it perfectly: "Design thinking leaves at the point where you come up with a list of solutions. But product management is completely iterative. It keeps on going. You also have to execute it. You also have to make the customer adopt it."

## Attributes of a Strong Product Manager

| Attribute | Why It Matters |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Ability to Influence and Lead** | You have to get things done through people who do not report to you |
| **Handling Ambiguity** | Resilience and tolerance -- things will be unclear, changing, and contradictory |
| **Market Knowledge and Business Judgment** | Astute, meticulous understanding of the market and commercial realities |
| **Attention to Detail and Process Skills** | The work is intricate; sloppiness kills products |
| **Fluency with Technology** | You do not need to code, but you need to understand what is possible and what is not |
| **Design Instincts (UX)** | See it as a user. Better yet, see it as a first-time user who is not tech-savvy |

## The Overarching Thought

The professor closed with a single slide that captures the entire role:

> **The Product Manager's job is fundamentally about defining the customer experience and ensuring that vision is translated into a successful product through collaboration, prioritization, and iteration.**

Three words: **collaboration** (because you need everyone), **prioritization** (because you cannot do everything), and **iteration** (because you will not get it right the first time).

## The Real-World Takeaway

Meera had been a software engineer for six years when her company told her she was being "moved into product." Her first reaction was confusion. Her second was flattery. Her third, once she started the actual work, was panic.

The product was a B2B invoicing tool used by small businesses across three states. On her first day as PM, she opened a spreadsheet of feature requests from the sales team and found 127 items, each marked "urgent." She tried to tackle them in order. By Friday, she had committed to twelve new features in a single sprint, and the engineering lead, a quiet man named Vikram, pulled her aside after the standup. "Meera," he said, "I respect your enthusiasm, but if we build all twelve, none of them will work properly. Pick three."

She picked three based on gut feeling. Two flopped. The third -- an auto-reminder for overdue invoices -- turned out to be the most requested feature in the next customer survey. She had gotten lucky, and she knew it.

The following month, Meera tried a different approach. She spent two weeks in the problem space. She called fourteen customers, sat with three of them while they used the product, and read every support ticket filed in the last quarter. She discovered something nobody on the sales team had flagged: small business owners were not abandoning the product because it lacked features. They were abandoning it because the onboarding flow asked for eleven fields before you could send your first invoice. Most of them gave up at field seven.

She brought this finding to the team as a documented problem statement -- not a solution, just the problem. Vikram's team proposed cutting the onboarding to four fields and letting users fill in the rest later. The design lead disagreed; she wanted a guided walkthrough instead. The sales head wanted both, plus a chatbot. Meera had no authority to overrule any of them. What she had was data from fourteen phone calls and a screen recording of a shop owner in Jaipur closing the browser tab at field seven with a sigh.

She played the recording in the product council meeting. Nobody argued after that. They shipped the four-field onboarding in three weeks. Completion rates went from 38% to 71% in the first month.

Meera did not write a single line of code. She did not design a single screen. She did not close a single sale. What she did was listen to a customer, separate the problem from the solution, and then get a room full of people who did not report to her to agree on what mattered most. Nobody gave her a certificate for it. The engineering team got the credit in the company newsletter. She was fine with that.

**The best product managers do not build products. They build clarity -- and then make sure the right people act on it.**
