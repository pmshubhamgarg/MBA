# Session 1: Economic Value Added (EVA)

## The Story

Your friend asks you for 1 lakh rupees to start a small food stall. A month later he comes back beaming: "I made 20,000 rupees profit!" Sounds great, right?

But wait — what if you'd just put that 1 lakh in a fixed deposit instead, earning, say, 10,000 rupees for that month with zero effort and zero risk? Now look again: your friend "made" 20,000, but 10,000 of that was just the **rent your money was always going to earn anywhere**. He only *actually created* 10,000 rupees of new value by running the stall.

Now imagine he'd only made 8,000 rupees profit. He'd still say "I made profit!" — but compared to what your money could've earned safely, he actually **destroyed** 2,000 rupees of value. Profit was positive. Value creation was negative.

## The Concept

That's EVA — Economic Value Added. It's not "did we make profit," it's "did we make more than what our money could've earned doing nothing risky." A business, a product line, even a whole company can be growing and "profitable" every year and still be quietly destroying value, if it's not clearing that bar.

## Why This Matters as a Future Manager

Every big company has dozens of divisions, product lines, and projects all fighting for the same limited pool of money. Someone has to decide: does the "Elite" product line deserve more budget, or does the boring "Classic" line that nobody's excited about actually deserve it more? A manager who only looks at "which one made more profit" gets fooled constantly — the flashy, fast-growing line often eats up huge amounts of capital (inventory, receivables, new equipment) to get that profit. EVA is the tool that cuts through the noise.

This is exactly why real companies — Coca-Cola, GE, Godrej, plenty of Indian conglomerates — actually pay bonuses to division heads based on EVA, not just profit or revenue growth. If you ever sit in a budget meeting and hear "this division grew 40 percent this year," your job as the MBA-trained person in the room is to be the one who asks: "grew 40 percent — but did it actually earn back more than what its capital cost us?" That single question is worth the whole degree. Nobody teaches you to ask it unless you've been trained to.

## The One-Liner

*"Making money isn't the same as making it worth it — always ask what you gave up to get there."*
