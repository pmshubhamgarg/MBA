# Session 17: Spin-offs, Activist Shareholders, and the Kraft-Mondelez Saga

*The final case of the course. ## Part 1: Why Would a Company Shrink Its Own Scope?

The professor opens with a direct question to the class: when should a company sell off, divest, or exit a business -- the mirror image of everything covered so far about *expanding* scope. The brainstorm that follows becomes the raw material for the whole theory section.

**Student answers, collected one by one:**

- **Manish (financial return):** if a business isn't delivering the return shareholders expect -- in finance terms, if it isn't beating the **weighted average cost of capital** -- and other opportunities offer a better long-term return, shareholders may want to divest.
- **Goro (technology disruption):** if your business is in a technology being replaced by something newer and you lack the expertise to adapt, that is a reason to exit.
- **Manish (low synergy and simplicity):** low synergy between businesses, one growing fast while the other is mature, complexity in a conglomerate, and analyst coverage all push toward spin-offs. A single analyst can't be an expert in every business a conglomerate owns -- splitting lets each analyst specialize.
- **A student (saturation):** a business in a saturation stage, where both profit and competition pressure are rising, is a candidate for exit.
- **Ashoke (the HP example):** HP split into two divisions -- HP (printers/PCs) and the enterprise-focused HP -- because the PC division was too big to manage as part of the whole and was stagnating rather than growing. Splitting let the two entities grow "without killing each other's revenue."
- **Priyanka (country-specific exit):** if a country's policies change and the cost of doing business there no longer makes sense, a multinational may exit that country -- a **geographical** scope shrinkage rather than a horizontal or vertical one. A student adds: many companies fled Russia during the Russia-Ukraine war for exactly this reason.
- **Sham (mission misalignment):** when a business no longer serves the company's overall mission or long-term goals, it should be sold.

> "Typically... in a diversification or geographical-expansion scenario, sometimes what you are saying may be correct. For example, operating in Russia -- many companies would not have the corporate value of one country having aggression over others. Or companies may sell off a non-green business when they adopt a strongly green corporate value. That is when the corporate vision and mission has strong inconsistency, or there is strong possibility of backlash from one particular portfolio business."

- **Priyanka (new discoveries):** a new scientific discovery revealing that a product harms the environment can force a company to close that business.

> "Sometimes a technological development may also lead to spin-offs, because specific technologies or product types don't necessarily create synergies with the existing dominant business. When they are separate, more investment and more focused management attention can be provided."

- **A student ties it back to the P&G case:** the US/UK structural split discussed earlier in the course is also, at root, about achieving more focus on a specific product segment or market -- the spin-off strategy can be viable there too.

> "I'm happy that many of you could provide some very interesting reasons, and many of you have also read the material."

## Part 2: Activist Shareholders -- The Uncomfortable Push

### Why CEOs Resist Spin-offs in the First Place

Before listing the reasons *for* spin-offs, the professor explains why they don't happen more often on management's own initiative.

> "Spin-offs are not necessarily always the first priority of most CEOs. Most CEOs like building further empire, broadening the scope, managing higher revenue, or acquiring businesses -- they see that as strong CV enhancement or personal ego satisfaction. There is a bias for expanding scope, or managing the status quo, because when you sell off something, it creates pain."

Selling a legacy business is especially hard when top leaders **personally rose from that business** -- past pride and personal connections make it very difficult to sell off something that has "become a dog," even when it's no longer lucrative for the portfolio.

> "That Corning TV-screen business -- that was very close to Corning's original success, very near to Corning's own headquarters, and therefore it was a difficult decision to make."

Selling disrupts lives, causes layoffs, and makes long-tenured employees feel cheated. **In such situations, you need an external person to push you, to twist your arm.**

**Think of it like this:** a family patriarch who built the business from one small shop refuses to sell the original shop even though it now loses money every year -- it takes an outsider, someone with no emotional stake, to force the sale.

### Who Are Activist Shareholders, Really?

> "Typically they are wealthy individuals heading hedge funds. Very egotist persons. Not necessarily very pleasant people. Not necessarily ethical people. But sometimes they play their roles constructively -- not for the company, but for the shareholders."

**What they do, mechanically:**
- Build significant **minority stakes** (not majority) -- enough to create real pain.
- Acquire **proxy voting rights** from other shareholders who can't be present for votes, amplifying their voting power well beyond their actual shareholding percentage.
- Pressurize management to appoint preferred people to the board, alter business scope and strategy, increase innovation or cut costs, and return more cash via dividends or buybacks.

### Manish's Question: Are Mutual Funds and Pension Funds Also Activist Shareholders?

A sharp question: aren't large fund houses and pension funds, which also hold big stakes, activist shareholders too?

> "They typically don't have that kind of mandate. These kinds of activities require huge research, ethically ambiguous stands, publicizing views in media, sending open letters, questioning board members. Most regular organized investors -- mutual funds etc. -- don't have that mandate. It is possible they may allocate their vote in conjunction with other activist shareholders if they support those views, but would they typically take the lead? Unlikely."

**Three reasons the professor gives for why mutual funds rarely lead activism themselves:**
1. Their mandate is fundamentally different -- invest in good companies and let them perform.
2. It is a risky activity: sometimes it works, sometimes it backlashes.
3. They often have an unspoken **quid pro quo** with companies (informal information access in exchange for not harassing management) -- though he notes this is legally murky territory he isn't an expert on.

**Statutory limits example:** in India, a mutual fund cannot acquire more than roughly 10 percent stake in a particular company. Banks, similarly, mostly can only extend loans rather than take equity stakes, except in specified financial-services entities like a stock exchange.

**The BlackRock/ESG tangent:** just before Trump came to power, large index-fund houses like BlackRock were sending letters pressuring companies on ESG/green criteria, threatening exclusion from green ETFs -- even though these were mutual-fund-type entities, not hedge funds, effectively behaving like activist shareholders outside their usual mandate.

**A student pushes back:** if a mutual fund holds a significant stake, wouldn't activist-style questioning hurt the value of their own investment -- so wouldn't they just quietly exit instead of making noise?

> "That is true. I'm not saying no to that, and I completely agree that most of the time that is how they behave. But at least I don't think there is any legal barrier on a mutual fund that they cannot take an active shareholder view."

**The Zee-Sony example (institutional investors acting in coalition):** when Zee Group wanted to appoint Subhash Chandra's son to head the group, proxy shareholders and institutional investors voted against it in collaboration, because there was a widespread view the person was unsuitable. Sony insisted Zee not keep that person as head; Zee refused; **the entire Zee-Sony merger talk broke down** as a result.

> "Institutional shareholders can vote in combination with proxy firms or other activist shareholders, but would they typically take the lead? Unlikely."

**The timeline mismatch, in one memorable line:**

> "A company like Danaher may be looking at a 20-30 year timeline, whereas an activist shareholder is looking at a one-year timeline. There is a difference between the timelines, and therefore how you assess the value of a business may also be very different because of that."

> **Formula for the exam: activist shareholders' own incentive is short-term and self-protective -- if a suggestion fails, their own holding takes the hit too. That is why they push hard, fast, and sometimes recklessly.**

## Part 3: The Rationale for Spin-offs

### Reason 1 -- Sum of the Parts Greater Than the Whole

The classic case: a **young, fast-growing business trapped inside an otherwise mature, large corporate portfolio.** Its true valuation is hard to establish while buried inside the parent -- it may not get enough funding, corporate attention, freedom to build different processes, entrepreneurial spirit, or the right talent. Once separated, its **multiple** grows, which then lets it raise equity cheaper, raise debt more easily, or acquire other firms.

**This directly challenges the BCG matrix logic** (cash cows funding stars) taught earlier in the course. The professor is candid that both arguments have merit -- sometimes a fast-growth business does get starved of bandwidth inside a mature portfolio; other times it thrives precisely because it's backed by a cash-generating parent that can't otherwise use its own cash.

**The activist-shareholder counter-argument, in the professor's own analogy:**

> "It is like saying that if I want to invest only in oil and gas, and if Reliance has oil and gas, green energy, retail, and telecom together, then it is not giving me an opportunity to invest in a very good oil-and-gas company only. Give that choice to your shareholders -- let them decide whether they want your combined portfolio or a specific part of your business."

This "sum of parts greater than whole" logic is directly consistent with the **conglomerate discount** discussed earlier in the course -- if the discount is large, a few high-potential businesses inside the conglomerate are heavily undervalued, and simply spinning them off increases their own valuation.

### Reason 2 -- Analyst Sector Coverage

After a spin-off, specialized analysts can start tracking narrowly defined businesses properly.

**The Jio Financial Services example:** when Jio Financial Services was separated from the rest of the Reliance portfolio, it could now be tracked by an expert in financial services -- whereas previously an oil-and-gas-focused analyst covering all of Reliance would never have the know-how to properly track every separate part, including financial services. Investors relying on specific investment criteria (say, only ethical or only green investment) can now decide firm by firm rather than being forced to accept a bundled entity.

### Reason 3 -- Simplicity and Focus

Spinning off simplifies business-level strategy, raises innovation, entrepreneurial orientation, and managerial accountability, and lowers the need to deal with internal politics and power structures.

### Reason 4 -- A Standalone Business May Find a Better Suitor

**The Cadbury Schweppes example, directly foreshadowing the case:** Kraft was not interested in buying the Schweppes part of Cadbury Schweppes. Only once Cadbury demerged from Schweppes and became a standalone company did it become a **real acquisition target for Mondelez** -- and Cadbury shareholders got good value out of that deal.

### Reason 5 -- Lack of (or Lost) Synergies

If anticipated synergies never arrived, or original synergies dissipated, selling makes sense.

- **Google/Motorola:** Google acquired Motorola's handset business at roughly a 12 billion valuation, then sold it off after about a year or two for roughly 1 to 2 billion, once it realized making smartphones was "not its own cup of tea." Google still ships a small showcase phone to demonstrate its Android OS features, but is not a serious phone-maker.
- **ITC's hotel business:** probably originally kept as part of the portfolio to balance cash flows and diversify away from the core tobacco business, not for genuine synergy -- but over time ITC likely realized hotels need a sharply separate focus, with no real synergy with the rest of ITC, making it more of a true conglomerate/unrelated business.
- **GE's three-way split:** likely for the same reason -- the three resulting businesses were "way different" from each other, with no point in keeping them together.

### Reason 6 -- Avoiding Cross-Subsidization of an Underperformer

**The IBM PC business example:** IBM's PC business was making huge losses -- at least 300 to 400 million every year between roughly 1995 and 1998 -- but this was invisible because only the consolidated corporate balance sheet was shared with shareholders. Separating the business avoids this kind of unhealthy cross-subsidization from being hidden.

## Part 4: Arguments *Against* Spin-offs

The professor is equally balanced on the counter-case:

1. **Banking fees.** Real, and sometimes substantial -- but if the strategic rationale is strong, this shouldn't be the deciding factor.
2. **Hidden synergies and shared services.** Economies of scale (shared purchasing), economies of scope (producing/selling multiple products together), leveraging a corporate brand, and lower overheads from centralized IT, board, investor relations, and legal departments. If these benefits outweigh the case for splitting, don't spin off.
3. **Risk reduction from diversification, and benefits of scale.** If revenues across business units are negatively correlated, staying together reduces risk and cost of capital. Larger companies also get better access to capital markets, more liquid shares, index-fund inclusion, and easier debt access.

**The Titan "Made in India" documentary example:** although Titan was conceived as a Tata Group company, because it wasn't formally part of a larger Tata Group entity, banks refused to lend to it -- even though it carried the aura of a Tata company. **Larger, formally connected companies get access to capital that a spun-off standalone firm may not.**

## Part 5: The Strategist's Counter-Argument to Pure Corporate Finance

The professor pauses to reflect on his own dual role:

> "When we are advising CEOs, we can't wear a pure corporate-finance hat. We know there is life beyond corporate finance. I'm not criticizing corporate finance -- I'm saying there are other rationales beyond what theory-driven corporate finance would suggest, and we are giving some counter-arguments the CEO can use to convince activist shareholders, the board, or analysts that there is a rationale behind what they are doing."

**The corporate-finance purist's position, restated bluntly:** if unrelated diversification reduces the portfolio's aggregate risk (bringing it closer to the market portfolio), the firm can raise funds cheaply and survive major macro shocks (2008 financial crisis vs. the narrower dot-com bust). But activist shareholders will still argue:

> "Why are you diversifying? Let the customers -- sorry, let the investors -- diversify. Let them decide how much, and whether to include or exclude specific sectors or countries. Why are you deciding on behalf of shareholders?"

Investors can move in and out of sectors far more easily than a business itself can restructure -- which is the efficiency argument for letting shareholders, not management, do the diversifying.

### Manish's Question: Is the Vedanta Split a Spin-off?

A student asks whether Vedanta Group's recent break-up into three, five, or six companies counts as a spin-off.

> "It is technically called, I think, a demerger... that group -- I don't have a very good analytical opinion about the policies and decision-making of that group, so I don't know. But technically that may not be called a spin-off, because spin-off assumes there is a larger entity and you are separating one business. If you look at GE, they are probably called demergers -- splitting into multiple entities. I don't think that is a very usual occurrence; they may be very special occurrences, compared to spin-offs which happen much more regularly. Most of the logic behind that would be the same we are discussing -- more specialized businesses, better focus, separate valuation. Its label may be different than spin-off."

> **Formula for the exam: spin-off = one piece separates from an intact core. Demerger = the whole entity splits into several pieces at once. Same underlying logic (focus, valuation, specialization), different scale of the action.**

## Part 6: Entering the Case -- A Company That Did Everything

With theory settled, the professor turns to the case, calling it "the final fascinating case" of the course:

> "You can see how much corporate activity is going on in this one case... acquisition, spin-off, tender offers, mergers -- I think everything is going on in this one case."

The professor is explicit that this case is **not** meant to be read as a leadership-effectiveness story -- his purpose is to use it as an **integrative capstone** for the entire course, since (with the partial exception of strategic alliances, which shows up once via a coffee JV) it touches nearly every corporate-strategy tool taught this term, all inside one company across roughly 11 to 13 years.

**The sobering twist he flags upfront:** what looked like a very smart move at the time this case was written later turned problematic. The **grocery half** of the original Kraft business (spun off, then acquired by the 3G Group to become Kraft Heinz) was considered a success story when the case was written -- but it later ran into trouble and is now itself being split again, a process in which **Warren Buffett lost money**, one of his rare investing failures.

### The Major Transformations at a Glance

| Transformation | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| **Portfolio composition** | Kraft = grocery + snacks combined | Split into grocery business (merged elsewhere, became Kraft Heinz) and a snacks business that acquired Cadbury and became Mondelez |
| **Geographic footprint** | US-centric firm overall | Grocery side stayed US-centric; the snacks side (Mondelez) globalized dramatically |

### The Full List of Corporate Actions in This One Case

| Action Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Acquisitions** | LU biscuit acquisition (Europe-centric); Cadbury acquisition |
| **Spin-off** | The Kraft spin-off separating grocery from snacks |
| **Divestment** | A few brands divested |
| **Joint venture** | A coffee-business JV |
| **Internal initiative** | Zero-based budgeting (adopted more fully by the Kraft Heinz side) |
| **Acquisitions that did NOT happen** | Hershey (trust problem); Frito-Lay (activist-shareholder pressure resisted by the CEO) |

**The Frito-Lay story deserves its own note:** Frito-Lay was part of PepsiCo. The **same activist shareholder** who held a stake in PepsiCo also held enough stake in Mondelez, and pressured PepsiCo to spin off Frito-Lay so Mondelez could acquire it. Mondelez's CEO **pushed back and won** -- arguing the Cadbury integration was still incomplete, ROE targets hadn't been hit, and acquiring another huge brand right then risked overstretching the company.

## Part 7: Why Separate Grocery From Snacks? The Store-Shelf Logic

The professor asks directly: why not just keep investing in the fast-growing snacks business *while remaining* one combined company? Why exit the grocery business at all?

**Manish's initial answer:** grocery was stable/mature while snacking was fast-growing; by acquiring Cadbury, the company wanted to be present in the **"hot zone"** -- creating smaller package sizes even for biscuits to occupy that fast-growing, higher-margin space.

The professor pushes further -- why not stay together and just invest more in the fast-growing piece? A student suggests shareholder pressure; another suggests reducing complexity. The professor confirms it was **more an internally driven decision**, and lays out the real underlying logic using the three-part corporate-advantage lens (portfolio, resources and capabilities, organizational processes):

**1. Store-shelf-space synergy was always weak.** Grocery is a **"middle of the store"** business. Snacks, candy, and chocolate are **impulse-purchase** businesses, ideally positioned near checkout/"horizon" space. Brands that can't capture that space stay stuck in the back aisle -- meaning the two product types were never naturally co-located even on the same store shelf.

**2. Distribution, branding, and innovation needs diverged.** Snacks needed constant new product launches and adaptive branding; grocery, as a mature category, needed much less of that.

**3. Cost structure and margin logic were opposite.** Groceries are **high-volume, low-margin** -- success requires dramatic cost-cutting. Post-split, the Kraft Heinz side pursued **extreme efficiency**: no advertising, no new product launches, no significant brand building -- and drove margins up from roughly **14 percent to somewhere around 19 to 23 percent.** That is a completely different operating character from R&D-heavy, brand-building, geography-adapting snacks.

**4. Buyer power was rising in the US grocery aisle.** Consolidating retail buyers -- "the Walmarts of this world" -- were gaining bargaining power fast, squeezing grocery profitability further.

**5. Growth strategy and geography pointed opposite directions.** To grow the impulse-purchase business fast and diversify away from over-reliance on the US market, the company needed to go global -- but grocery, by definition, is a far more domestic business.

**Cannibalization avoidance in the split design:** the businesses were deliberately separated so that Kraft Heinz kept the efficiency-focused, low-margin, high-volume, mature, US-centric "middle of the store" business, while the spun-off entity kept the fast-growth, globally focused, impulse-purchase consumer packaged goods business.

**Think of it like separating a marathon runner from a sprinter who'd been forced to train together:** one needs steady, efficient pacing over familiar terrain; the other needs explosive, adaptive bursts across constantly changing courses. Training them under one coach with one plan holds both back.

## Part 8: The Cadbury Acquisition -- A Patient, Lucky Hunt

The professor walks through the mechanics of how Kraft (later Mondelez) acquired Cadbury -- a nice mirror image of the earlier Cadbury Schweppes/Adams case, where Cadbury had been the *acquirer*; here Cadbury is the *target*.

**Timeline:**
- **Around 2007:** Cadbury Schweppes identified as a target -- but Kraft had **no interest** in the Schweppes beverages part of the business, so it waited.
- **Around 2009:** Cadbury and Schweppes demerged into separate companies. Now Kraft was very interested in acquiring Cadbury on its own -- **but the British pound was overvalued** (likely a lingering effect of the 2008 financial crisis pushing the dollar weaker relative to the pound), making the deal too expensive.
- **Around 2011:** After the Euro bond crisis weakened the pound, the currency math finally worked. Kraft acquired Cadbury in a **hostile takeover** at a price of roughly **19 billion**.

**The lucky part:** despite being a hostile takeover, there happened to be internal acceptance within Cadbury toward being acquired by Kraft.

> "Had it not been the case, then the organizational cost would have been far higher. For some very strange reasons, some due-diligence process or cost-of-organizing assessment was really not well assessed in advance. It was just by sheer luck that the company did not face any significant issues post-acquisition."

Kraft also made several smaller acquisitions along the way, the major one being the more **Europe-centric LU biscuit** acquisition.

## Part 9: The Coffee Business -- Why a JV Instead of Full Ownership

Even though coffee is technically a grocery-type brand, it was sold more as a **differentiated, internationally-focused consumer packaged good** -- so it stayed with the international/branded side of the business rather than the domestic grocery side.

**Why convert the coffee business into a JV rather than keep it wholly owned?** To reduce risk and achieve the **scale available from a partner** for distribution in a particular market.

**A cautionary detail from the case:** in one particular European market, the JV partner **sold its own stake to a competitor** of the non-Cadbury part of Mondelez -- forcing Mondelez to exit its own stake in that specific JV. This is exactly the kind of forced, unwanted exit the professor flagged as a risk back in Session 16's discussion of JV partner reputational damage.

## Part 10: Zero-Based Budgeting -- A Detour Into a Case Term

The professor pauses to clarify a term used in the case, explicitly noting **this has nothing to do with corporate strategy per se** -- it's an internal efficiency tool, and it was adopted more religiously by the Kraft Heinz side.

**A student's real-world example:** in a meeting they attended, budgeting normally starts from the previous year's remaining budget, with incremental adjustments. **Zero-based budgeting instead requires fresh justification every period** -- you can't say "10 percent more than last year"; you must justify from scratch: expected sales, the marketing budget required to hit it, and the customer awareness/loyalty points that budget is expected to buy.

**The professor's own critique, based on how such processes really work:**

> "They would do that only when there were negative variances -- some target wasn't achieved, so tough questions get asked. But the next target-setting, they may not force zero-based budgeting from scratch, because they also understand the numbers, and it's not easy to deceive corporate management by relying on them. They would force very tight scrutiny of minute variances instead -- why were electricity charges higher, why were shipping costs higher -- because they understand at a micro level how these businesses are run."

**The real risk of zero-based budgeting:** dramatic cost reductions are possible, but it can also create significant stress, unnecessary chopping and churning, and waste enormous divisional time proving justification every single cycle -- especially damaging for **long-gestation investments that don't have short-term benchmarks** to point to.

> **Formula for the exam: zero-based budgeting = fresh justification every period, from zero, for every line item -- powerful for cost discipline, dangerous for long-horizon investments that can't show quick wins.**

## Part 12: Grading Irene Rosenfeld -- Was the Transformation a Success?

The professor puts the exam-style question directly: **has Rosenfeld been successful? Is the portfolio better or worse than the day she took over? What assumptions went right, and what went wrong?**

A student's first answer -- "they have very good, very respected brands" -- gets pushed back immediately:

> "They might have very good brands, but are they making money for their own shareholders? That is the question."

### The Broader Consumer-Packaged-Goods Headwind

Many "high quality, low volatility, safe stock" CPG names -- not just Mondelez -- have underperformed since roughly **2019**. During COVID, some valuations briefly rose as people bought staples while other sectors suffered; but once lockdowns ended and interest rates stayed extremely low, **growth and technology stocks took off** while CPG names struggled. Then higher input-price inflation forced dramatic price increases, demand fell, and on top of that, **digital-first, direct-to-consumer brands** began threatening the whole conventional branding-and-marketing model.

### Reading the Stock-Performance Chart

The case's chart compares Mondelez international (including the impact of the Kraft-Heinz -- "KC" -- merger) against peers:

- **The combined-entity line (including KC merger impact) is the highest** -- but this is misleading, because Kraft Heinz achieved this through **short-sighted value creation**: drastic cuts to brand building, sales/distribution expansion, and marketing budget. Short-term profitability, at the cost of long-term growth and profit potential -- and by the time of this discussion, that grocery entity "is not doing well."
- **Mondelez *excluding* the KC merger impact** still did better than international large-cap food peers, roughly matched North American large-cap food peers, and clearly beat the S&P 500 -- **through 2017.**

### The Deeper Numbers (Exhibit 8)

| Metric | Mondelez Trend | What It Reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Total sales** | Declining (2012 onward) | Shared by nearly all peers (Nestle, P&G, PepsiCo, Unilever) -- likely reflects divestitures, not distress on its own |
| **Organic net sales growth** | 4.4 (2012) down to 1.3 | Higher than P&G, but lower than Nestle (its closer comparable); PepsiCo isn't a fair comparison |
| **EBIT margin growth** | From 4.1 down to 3.4 -- lowest of the five peers | Under pressure, but *not* pursuing Kraft Heinz-style short-term cost cutting |
| **R&D as percent of sales** | Lowest (apart from non-comparable PepsiCo and non-R&D-intensive KC) | Not investing in new-product capability |
| **Advertising expense as percent of sales** | Lowest (apart from non-comparable peers) | Not investing in brand-building either |

> "If you enter a growth portfolio, are you investing into growth, or are you achieving growth? If your organic net sales is falling, that's a worry. If your EBIT margins haven't sharply increased, your branding or new-product development isn't giving you the required differentiation. Neither investing in R&D nor spending enough on advertising -- so how can you manage a high-growth portfolio, particularly when facing demand saturation in developing countries, if you are not investing in growth?"

### The Verdict on Rosenfeld

> "The biggest criticism of this CEO probably is that although she started with the right motivations and vision, part of that vision did not materialize -- the growth rate expected from large developing countries like India and China never arrived as anticipated. Partly because she was neither investing sufficiently in growth, nor sufficiently in improving efficiencies."

**What a high-potential, internationally focused portfolio actually requires** (and where the case leaves real doubt about whether Mondelez built these capabilities):
1. Understanding **local taste and preference**, not just US preferences.
2. Sophisticated product innovation, marketing, and packaging for "hot zone" products.
3. Balancing local adaptation with economies of scale and scope, including efficient global supply chains.
4. Go-to-market capability across geographies with very different infrastructure, and responsiveness to local disruptions (a real chocolate-business problem in India for a couple of years).
5. Efficiency and specialization through zero-based budgeting and arbitrage opportunities -- the professor explicitly **pardons** the company on this fifth point, since focusing on the first four already takes enormous management bandwidth.

**Possible reasons for the shortfall, offered without certainty:** the company may have been distracted by continuous activist-shareholder pressure; Rosenfeld may have been nearing retirement and avoiding disruptive decisions; she may simply have been "playing safe."

### The Organizational-Process Twist

A surprising detail the professor highlights: Rosenfeld **disaggregated the businesses very early** -- possibly sacrificing global scale efficiencies unnecessarily, *before* the eventual spin-off into Mondelez even made that appropriate. Once decentralized, **re-aggregating became much harder** once growth disappointed and cost pressure mounted -- exactly the moment when more aggregation, more functional specialization, and more efficient global supply chains would have helped most.

### The Three-Part Framework Conclusion (Portfolio / Resources / Organization)

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Were the scope changes an adequate response to the changing CPG landscape? | **Yes** -- the original grocery business needed extreme cost control and US focus; the other needed brand-building, R&D, and global reach. Splitting them made sense. |
| Were the resources and capabilities appropriate to make each business competitive? | **Unclear / probably not yet built** -- weak evidence in the case that the required new capabilities were fully developed by 2017. |
| Were the organizational structure and processes appropriate for coordination and control? | **Questionable** -- early disaggregation likely sacrificed efficiencies that became hard to recover later. |

> "The combination of all three -- businesses, resources, and organization -- is crucial in both broadening and narrowing your scope, and in undergoing corporate transformation. That is why this is a good case to conclude whatever we have discussed in this course."

**The meta-lesson of Session 17:** a single company can execute nearly every corporate-strategy tool taught this term -- acquisition, spin-off, divestment, JV, failed deals, internal efficiency programs -- and still leave a genuinely mixed verdict. Getting the **portfolio scope decision right** (splitting grocery from snacks) is necessary but not sufficient; without matching **resources/capabilities** and the right **organizational process** discipline (especially not disaggregating too early), even a sound strategic logic can under-deliver on its own promise.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Scope Shrinking** | The mirror image of scope expansion -- divesting, selling, or spinning off a business; drivers include poor returns vs. WACC, technology disruption, low synergy, saturation, mission misalignment, or geographic exit |
| **Activist Shareholder** | A wealthy individual or hedge fund building a minority stake plus proxy voting power to pressure management into board changes, scope/strategy changes, or higher shareholder payouts; typically short-term-oriented |
| **Proxy Voting Rights** | Voting power an activist shareholder accumulates from other shareholders who can't attend votes themselves, amplifying influence beyond actual ownership percentage |
| **Sum of Parts Greater Than the Whole** | The core spin-off rationale: a fast-growing business trapped in a mature portfolio is undervalued; separating it unlocks a higher standalone multiple |
| **Conglomerate Discount** | The market undervaluing a diversified firm's high-potential businesses when bundled together -- spinning off a business can directly correct this |
| **Analyst Sector Coverage** | Specialized analysts can properly track a narrowly defined spun-off business, versus a generalist analyst forced to cover an entire diversified conglomerate |
| **Cross-Subsidization (hidden underperformer)** | A loss-making business unit's poor results stay invisible when buried inside a consolidated corporate balance sheet -- spin-offs expose this |
| **Demerger vs Spin-off** | Spin-off = one business separates from an otherwise intact core entity (the norm); demerger = the whole entity splits into multiple pieces at once (rarer, e.g., GE, Vedanta) |
| **Zero-Based Budgeting** | Every budgeting cycle starts from zero, requiring fresh line-item justification rather than incremental adjustment from last year's baseline; strong for cost discipline, risky for long-gestation investments without short-term benchmarks |
| **Hot Zone (impulse-purchase retail space)** | Checkout/horizon-adjacent shelf space suited to snacks, candy, and chocolate -- structurally different retail real estate from "middle of the store" grocery products |
| **Organic Net Sales Growth** | Sales growth from a company's own new product development and distribution initiatives, excluding growth from acquisitions or divestitures -- a purer read on whether a "growth" portfolio is actually growing |
| **Portfolio / Resources / Organization Framework** | The three-part lens (introduced earlier in the course) used to grade any corporate transformation: was the scope change right, were matching capabilities built, and was the organizational process aligned? |
| **Early Disaggregation Risk** | Decentralizing a business before scale/efficiency needs are fully understood can sacrifice global efficiencies that become very hard to recover once growth disappoints and cost pressure mounts |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Renata Cole ran "Data Products" at a mid-size software company called Larkspur, and on paper it was one team. In practice it was two very different animals wearing the same badge. Half her people built Pulse, a real-time analytics tool that had gone from a scrappy side project to the fastest-growing thing in the company, the feature every new customer asked about in the sales demo. The other half kept Ledger alive, the old monthly-reporting tool that every existing customer still depended on, still profitable, still completely unglamorous, still needing careful, patient maintenance rather than any bold new idea.

Every planning cycle turned into a tug of war. The Pulse engineers wanted to ship weekly, chase every new integration, hire aggressively, and take swings that might not pay off for a year. The Ledger engineers wanted stability, fewer surprises, and enough headcount to keep quarterly compliance reports from breaking during tax season. Renata kept splitting one budget, one roadmap, and one performance review cycle between two teams that were, in every way that mattered, running at completely different speeds. Good people on both sides were starting to feel like they were losing.

Then Marcus Iyer joined the board as an observer for the company's largest outside investor, and he did not come to make friends. In his first offsite, he put up a slide with Larkspur's revenue growth next to a competitor who had spun their fast-growing analytics arm into its own standalone unit two years earlier. "Your Pulse team is growing three times faster than Ledger," he said, "and you're letting Ledger's slow, steady numbers drag down the story investors tell about this company. Split them. Give Pulse its own budget, its own hiring plan, its own leadership, and let Ledger run itself like the reliable cash machine it actually is. Right now you're asking one manager to sprint and jog at the same pace, and neither team is forgiving you for it."

Renata pushed back hard in the room, half out of instinct and half because she had personally built Ledger from nothing years earlier, back when it was Larkspur's only product. Cutting it loose felt like abandoning something she still had a soft spot for. But that night, reviewing the last four sprint retros, she noticed the same complaint from both sides in different words: Pulse felt held back, Ledger felt neglected, and everyone blamed her for not choosing. She realized Marcus wasn't wrong just because he was blunt about it. He was an outsider with no attachment to how things used to be, which was exactly why he could see what she, standing too close to it, could not.

Three weeks later Renata split Data Products into two real teams: Pulse got its own lead, its own aggressive quarterly targets, and license to move fast and occasionally break things; Ledger got a smaller, steadier team explicitly rewarded for uptime and cost discipline, not growth. Both groups stopped apologizing for what the other side needed. Pulse shipped four releases in the following quarter. Ledger's support tickets dropped because its team finally had bandwidth that wasn't being quietly redirected to firefight Pulse emergencies. Renata kept a small, quiet ache about no longer personally overseeing the product she'd built from scratch, but she also finally understood why the pressure from someone outside the family business is often the very thing that makes the split possible.

**Sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a fast-growing team is stop asking it to share a roof with the slow, steady one it's outgrown.**
