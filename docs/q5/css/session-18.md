## Group 1: Voltas vs. Havells -- Belonging to a Parent vs. Building One Engine

### The Setup

Both companies are diversified, both are in similar industries, but they create value in **completely different ways**. Voltas draws strength from being part of the **Tata ecosystem**; Havells built its advantage by integrating multiple businesses under **one unified brand philosophy** -- "One Havells."

**Voltas's four divisions:**

| Division | What it does |
|---|---|
| **UCP (Unitary Cooling Products)** | The B2C engine -- dominant market share in ACs and commercial refrigeration |
| **Voltbek** | Strategic joint venture driving expansion into home appliances (washing machines, refrigerators) -- crucial for year-end revenue |
| **Engineering Products** | Specialized industrial arm -- textile machinery, mining and construction equipment |
| **International Operations (IOBG)** | Global project execution and MEP (mechanical/electrical/plumbing) services, especially in the Middle East |

The B2B side (engineering, international projects) provides the stability and cash flow that funds the aggressive B2C battles fought by UCP.

### The Real Engine: Belonging to Tata

**Think of it like this:** Voltas doesn't have to build every capability from scratch -- it plugs into the Tata ecosystem and rents the group's reach.

Being a Tata company delivers four tangible benefits: it **reduces transaction costs and friction**, it **fosters trust and innovation** through shared data and reputation across the group, it opens **markets otherwise inaccessible**, and it creates **captive synergies** -- think of Voltas executing MEP projects for Tata Motors and Tata Steel. The group also gives Voltas what the presenters called a near-**monopoly on talent and social capital**.

### Havells's Engine: Six Capitals, Six Ps

Havells has no parent conglomerate safety net, so its survival depends on one internal engine running with total precision.

**Six capitals** -- not just money, but significant investment in human capital -- feed a central mechanism of **six Ps**: Product, Place, Promotion, Price, People, and Process. These aren't run in silos; they are **tightly interlocked gears forced to run in union**, producing rapid, shared value creation.

**Think of Havells as a temple with one foundation:** a single undivided brand (not a portfolio of different names), pillars representing its core categories (cables, lighting, appliances), and a red structural beam running through all of them -- **B2C distribution density**. Secure shelf space for one product and you've secured it for the entire catalog.

### Structure: Formal Hierarchy vs. Flat Matrix

| Dimension | Voltas | Havells |
|---|---|---|
| **Corporate advantage source** | External ecosystem, Tata belongingness | Internal brand consolidation |
| **Structure philosophy** | Formal -- Tata enterprise framework down through board/project/risk committees | Flat, distributed matrix connecting R&D, manufacturing, and sales directly, supported by daily morning coordination huddles |
| **Business model** | Heavy B2B plus B2C, plus international MEP work | Dominant domestic B2C scale play |
| **Growth engine** | Joint ventures (Voltbek) plus captive Tata demand | Organic growth across product categories |
| **Market stance** | Diversified conglomerate subsidiary | Focused, unified electrical giant |

> **Formula for the exam: structured ecosystem leverage (Voltas) versus boundaryless functional integration (Havells) -- two internally consistent playbooks, neither objectively better.**

### Who Wins Where?

Both face the same outside risks but handle them differently: Voltas follows strict rules and leans on the Tata group to guide it safely; Havells is fast and relies on its own strong internal processes.

**The presenters' final scorecard:**
- **Speed and everyday teamwork:** Havells wins -- breaking down internal walls and pushing a single "One Havells" spirit makes it faster, more flexible, more united on the shop floor.
- **Pure size and outside power:** Voltas wins -- Tata backing gives it access to wealth, land, and technology Havells cannot match alone.
- **Playing it safe / managing rules:** Voltas wins -- the parent's trusted rule book heavily blocks out market risk.
- **Adapting quickly to new problems:** Havells wins -- it relies on its own agility rather than waiting on orders from a large parent group.

**Conclusion:** there is no single right way to grow a large business. Havells shows the power of working closely together internally; Voltas shows the huge benefit of a parent company on the outside. Success comes down to whether your structure actually matches your goals.

### Q&A Highlights

- **Which company has the stronger corporate advantage?** No single correct answer -- Voltas benefits from ecosystem/parenting advantages, Havells from faster coordination and stronger internal integration. It depends on whether external synergies or internal agility matter more in a given context.
- **Does Voltas lag on B2C distribution since Havells pushes so hard through retail?** No -- Voltas doesn't weaken on that front, it simply has more options because of its parent, while Havells has to build internal capability to compensate.
- **What about Herfindahl index calculations, vertical integration comparisons, internationalization focus?** The group admitted these weren't formally calculated, but noted that on backward integration, Havells strongly owns in-house manufacturing (motor winding, molding), while forward integration isn't meaningful for either company -- both stay asset-light downstream and rely on market contracting.

## Group 9: Dabur vs. Marico -- The Wide Empire vs. the Profitable Focus

### The Setup

Dabur is a **health and wellness powerhouse** with 140 years of legacy, spanning healthcare, home and personal care, and food -- reaching over 90 percent of Indian households. Its edge is **deep backward integration**, including 13,000 acres of sustainable herb cultivation feeding trusted core brands.

Marico runs an "agility and India" strategy: dominate core categories (coconut and edible oils) while building a high-margin house of **digital-first brands**, sourcing copra directly from farmers and leveraging quick commerce.

### Recent Scope Moves

Both firms are actively **pruning low-skill assets** and reallocating capital:

| Move | Dabur | Marico |
|---|---|---|
| **Exits** | Exited its Vita brand and adult/baby diaper businesses | -- |
| **Horizontal expansion** | Acquired Badshah Masala to enter the high-margin culinary/spices space | Built a digital-first D2C portfolio via acquisitions (Beardo, Plix, Just Herbs, True Elements) -- this segment exited FY25 at a 750 crore annual run rate |
| **Geographic scaling** | Signed an MOU with Tamil Nadu to build a 400 crore southern manufacturing hub | Expanded non-hair-oil categories across MENA and South Africa, reaching 29 percent of international revenue |

### Diversification: Wider Empire, Smaller Profit Machine

Dabur runs seven distinct categories; Marico runs four, over half of which is still just edible oils. Putting that into a diversification score, Dabur scores roughly 8,300 against Marico's 6,596 -- a real gap.

**But the number isn't the whole story -- it's how each got there.** Dabur spread using its know-how in herbal and Ayurvedic science, applied again to healthcare, personal care, and foods. Marico spread using a completely different capability: brand-building and marketing, applied again and again to new categories. Two different strengths, same result -- a portfolio that actually makes sense rather than a random collection of businesses.

### The Better-Off Test: A Split Decision

Dabur wins on **shared know-how and safety** -- when one category slows, the others cushion the fall. Marico wins on **brand leverage** -- every acquired brand (Beardo, Just Herbs) plugs into the same marketing machine and comes out stronger and faster.

The numbers back it up: Marico spends **50 percent more of revenue on advertising** than Dabur (10.4 percent vs. 6.9 percent), and walks away with a **better profit margin** (19.7 percent vs. 18.4 percent).

> **Formula for the exam: Dabur built the wider empire; Marico is making more money out of a smaller one. The recommendation isn't to add more businesses -- it's to extract more value from the ones you already own.**

### Vertical Integration: The Holdup Problem in Action

Vertical integration is measured through the **value-added-to-sales ratio** -- a higher ratio means greater vertical integration. For FY24-25, Dabur and Marico's ratios are **nearly identical** -- both sit in the range normal for a strong FMCG company that actually processes and manufactures, not just trades. Financially, both are equally integrated. **The real difference is why.**

This is the **holdup problem** at work -- you integrate backward when the market for an input is unreliable, with few suppliers, high uncertainty, and suppliers who can control price or refuse to supply.

- **Dabur** faces this with herbs and honey -- few quality suppliers, seasonal availability, price sensitivity. So it integrates backward.
- **Marico** faces the same risk with copra, sourced from scattered farmers and subject to volatile pricing. So it built its **own copra collection centers**.

For finished-goods manufacturing, both rely on third-party contract manufacturers -- no holdup risk there, so both outsource.

> **Formula for the exam: integrate only where the market can fail you; outsource where the market works.**

### Internationalization: Breadth vs. Depth

Both companies generate roughly a quarter of revenue internationally, but the strategies diverge sharply.

| | Dabur | Marico |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | Breadth -- present in 120+ countries via acquisitions, greenfield manufacturing, wholly owned subsidiaries, and exports | Depth -- focused market leadership in a handful of countries: Bangladesh, Vietnam, Middle East |
| **AAA emphasis** | Strong Adaptation (herbal toothpaste in the Middle East, hair oils for African hair) plus Aggregation (global brands like Vatika, Amla across regions) plus Arbitrage (sourcing from India, low-cost manufacturing in Egypt, Nigeria, Bangladesh) | Adaptation to serve local demand; efficiencies through regional manufacturing, but not building global export hubs |

### Corporate Advantage Formula

> **Formula for the exam: Corporate Advantage = Portfolio times Capabilities times Organization.**

Applied to Dabur: **Portfolio** = health, personal care, foods, and spices, all linked through herbal domain knowledge. **Capabilities** = Ayurvedic R&D, master brands (Dabur Red, Amla), backward-integrated herbal sourcing. **Organization** = SBU structure with distribution reaching 90 percent of Indian households.

**Four ways a business is concretely better off inside Dabur than standing alone:**
1. Inherits Ayurvedic R&D credibility instantly.
2. Plugs into distribution to 90 percent of households on day one.
3. Gets access to backward-integrated herbal sourcing no single brand could sustain alone.
4. Gets the group's capital and parenting advantage.

Dabur owns what it should own (herbal sourcing, master brands) and buys what it needs to buy (acquisitions like Badshah Masala).

### Five Recommendations to the CEO

1. **Keep pruning horizontal scope** -- exit categories where herbal/distribution resources give no real advantage; apply the better-off and ownership test before any new category.
2. **Hold the vertical integration level** -- around 48 percent value-added-per-sales is exactly right for branded FMCG; don't over-integrate.
3. **Trade breadth for depth internationally** -- one country out of 120 is thin; concentrate on MENA, sub-Saharan Africa, and US ethnic markets where Ayurvedic positioning actually wins.
4. **Close the synergy gap** -- raise ad spend from 6.9 percent toward Marico's 10.4 percent, back fewer sharper power brands, push Ayurvedic brand equity into foods and beverages.
5. **On AAA** -- more arbitrage from the manufacturing network, sustained adaptation, selective aggregation of global platforms like Vatika and Amla.

### Q&A Highlights

- **Marico's Bangladesh exposure:** roughly 40 percent of Marico's international revenue took a dip because of the 2024 political upheaval in Bangladesh, which was a major contributor. This is a direct consequence of Marico's **depth (concentrated)** international strategy versus Dabur's broader spread across 120+ countries -- the recommendation is for Marico to spread out geographically more like Dabur.
- **Why hasn't Marico grown as much as Dabur despite its strategic focus?** Dabur's 140-year legacy and strong Ayurvedic R&D give it a moat Marico simply doesn't have yet. Marico runs a leaner, more focused structure and has found great new digital brands, but to grow like Dabur it needs its own equivalent moat -- essentially, **backward integration and R&D** of its own.

## Group 10: Tata Steel vs. JSW Steel -- Global Integration vs. Domestic Efficiency

### The Framing

> "Corporate advantage is never an accident -- it is engineered through deliberate choice."

Rather than comparing operational performance, the group asked: how does Tata Steel create corporate advantage, and is its current scope and structure sustainable? They analyzed it through three lenses -- **vertical integration, related diversification, internationalization** -- benchmarked against JSW Steel, a similar-scale competitor with a very different corporate strategy.

### Tata Steel's Three Pillars

Established in 1970, Tata Steel evolved from a domestic manufacturer into a global integrated platform connecting raw materials, steelmaking, downstream value creation, and customer-facing businesses.

1. **Vertical integration** secures critical raw materials and extends into value-added downstream businesses.
2. **Related diversification** expands within the steel value chain while leveraging common capabilities.
3. **Internationalization** provides market access, technology access, and global scale through acquisitions.

Together these create cost leadership, capability sharing, and resilience -- deliberately avoiding the pitfalls of **unrelated** diversification.

### Related Diversification, Not a Conglomerate

Unlike diversified conglomerates hedging risk across unrelated industries, Tata Steel stays focused on **one industry: steel**. Its portfolio (flat and long products, ferro alloys, tubes, wires, retail brands) shares technology, capabilities, procurement, R&D, and even customer relationships. Geographic diversification measured via the Herfindahl index comes to roughly 5,433 -- **moderate**. Value comes from **economies of scope**: shared R&D, centralized procurement, common digital platforms, byproduct recycling, and the strong Tata brand across the UK, India, and Netherlands.

### Vertical Integration: Tata Steel's Strongest Lens

The company controls critical parts of its value chain: nearly **100 percent captive iron ore in India**, captive coal blocks, and power generation. Downstream, it operates branded businesses like Tiscon, Tata Agrico, tubes, wires, and even a digital platform.

**Three key benefits:** cost leadership through raw-material security, consistent product quality, and reduced exposure to commodity price volatility. **The cost:** high capital intensity reduces flexibility during the transition to low-carbon steelmaking. The group's recommendation: shift focus from **owning every asset** to **controlling strategic bottlenecks** -- quality scrap, renewable energy, carbon traceability.

### Internationalization: Growth by Acquisition

Unlike many Indian steel companies, Tata Steel expanded globally through **acquisitions rather than organic expansion** -- major deals including Corus, NatSteel, and Millennium Steel gave it access to advanced markets, technology, and global customers.

**Three benefits:** diversified revenue across economic cycles, accelerated technology transfer, strengthened global brand. **The costs:** regulatory complexity, higher capital requirements, and significant decarbonization costs, particularly in Europe.

> **Formula for the exam: international expansion creates value only when the benefits of ownership exceed the cost of managing geographically dispersed businesses.**

### Tata Steel vs. JSW Steel, Head to Head

| Metric | Tata Steel | JSW Steel |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Roughly 26 billion (bigger) | Roughly 20 billion |
| **Margin** | 12 percent | 13.6 percent (more profitable per rupee -- the "cost of complexity" for Tata) |
| **Herfindahl index (geographic)** | Roughly 543 (diversified across geography) | Roughly 1,600 (India-concentrated) |
| **Value-added / sales** | Roughly 58 percent (owns more of the value chain) | Roughly 43 percent |
| **Entry mode** | Large cross-border M&A -- Corus the defining deal | Greenfield and bolt-on acquisitions -- capital-light, domestic |

JSW remains predominantly India-focused, relying on domestic expansion, partnerships, and greenfield investments. **Tata's edge is integration and global capability; JSW competes through capital-efficient domestic scale.**

### The Present-Day Twist: Chosen Recalibration vs. Contested Recovery

Tata is repositioning globally on its own terms -- decommissioning UK blast furnaces, moving Port Talbot to electric arc furnace technology, signing a decarbonization pact with the Dutch government, and even entering passenger automotive manufacturing.

JSW's story is different: its **largest-ever acquisition, Bhushan Power and Steel (19,350 crore), was unwound by the Supreme Court in May 2025** -- the steel business is now inside a 50-50 joint venture with Japan's JFE Steel, while JSW still targets 62 million tons of domestic capacity by FY2032.

> **Formula for the exam: the real difference right now isn't who's bigger -- it's who's more exposed to control risk. Tata is executing a chosen recalibration; JSW is recovering from a contested one.**

### Six Recommendations

1. Broaden horizontal scope within India -- automotive steels, electrical steels, branded retail, digital services.
2. Deepen future-oriented vertical integration -- quality scrap, renewable power, low-carbon logistics.
3. Treat international ownership as **conditional, not permanent** -- future investment only with regulatory certainty, attractive economics, and positive risk-adjusted returns.
4. Formalize knowledge sharing across plants through centers of excellence.
5. Institutionalize "one Tata" synergy -- leverage Tata Power, Tata Motors, TCS, Tata Projects.
6. Strengthen the AAA framework by balancing adaptation, aggregation, and arbitrage across global operations.

### Q&A Highlights

- **Why recommend conditional ownership of Europe?** Corporate strategy is about creating value through ownership. If a business can't generate acceptable long-term secure returns despite restructuring, ownership should be reconsidered through joint ventures, minority partnerships, or phased exits -- ensuring capital goes where Tata has its strongest corporate advantage.
- **Professor's own question -- Tata Steel's Indian iron ore mine leases are expiring soon; how are Tata Steel and JSW managing this?** JSW is pursuing acquisitions and joint ventures for shared resources (a less capital-blocking strategy). The group admitted they didn't have a clear answer for Tata Steel's specific plan, but speculated Tata Steel may eventually follow a similar path.

## Group 12: ITC vs. HUL -- Internal Capital Market vs. Global Parenting

### The Setup

Both companies are Indian giants roughly 90 years old, built on completely opposite corporate structures. ITC runs **five very different businesses** selling to totally different buyers, generating almost 1 lakh crore of revenue -- and over half of ITC's capital (roughly 38,000 crore) sits **unallocated at the corporate center**, bigger than any single operating division. HUL is used not as a direct rival but as a **control/benchmark** -- similar scale, overlapping with ITC only in FMCG.

### The Claim vs. the Actual Actions

ITC's official story is that its edge comes from **synergy of enterprise strength** -- shared distribution, R&D, and branding across units. But the group's read of ITC's actual FY26 actions tells a different story: it spun off its hotel business, folded overseas subsidiaries back into the parent, and focused on managing tariff changes and protecting core cigarette revenue from tax hikes.

> "FY26 was about tidying up the portfolio and protecting cash flow, not pulling off big operational synergies. That's the gap we see between what ITC claims and what it actually does."

### Scope: Changing Industries vs. Changing Brands

**ITC changes the actual industry it's in. HUL simply changes the brand it owns inside a single industry.** HUL is highly related, focused on one main buyer channel. ITC operates across five completely unrelated buyer groups. Both firms recently demerged a business -- ITC spun off its hotel business, HUL is separating its ice cream business -- but **ITC made that decision itself in Kolkata**, while **HUL's decision was handed down by its parent company in London**.

### The Herfindahl Trap: A Number That Lies

Strictly by the math, for FY26, HUL scores 7,404 and ITC scores a lower 7,199 -- mathematically, **HUL looks more diversified**. But as the presenters put it: as a strategist, you have to look past the formula.

**The index measures the evenness of revenue, not how related the actual businesses are.** All five HUL segments -- home care, foods, and the rest -- go to the exact same endpoint: 9 million retail outlets. HUL's diversification is **highly related**. ITC, by contrast, sells to five completely separate endpoints: cigarettes to licensed trade, FMCG to retail, agri to institutional processors, paperboard to B2B converters, and IT services to CIOs in North America and Europe.

> **Formula for the exam: this is true conglomerate diversification -- these businesses share almost nothing on the shelf. The burden of proof is on ITC's corporate center to show these businesses are actually better off together.**

### Vertical Integration and the Holdup Problem, Again

Measured by value-added-to-sales, ITC is significantly more integrated at **58.7 percent and rising**; HUL sits at **50.9 percent and falling**. Why does ITC integrate backward all the way to small farmers? Not to save money by cutting out a supplier's markup -- because of **asset specificity**. ITC needs very specific leaf grades for its cigarette blends and agriculture inputs for its branded foods, and there's no reliable open spot market for that level of quality. ITC integrates to **guarantee security of supply and avoid holdup risk** -- a strategic choice of control requiring over 10,000 crore of internal trade every year.

### Internationalization: Mirror Images on the AAA Framework

- **ITC:** Arbitrage dominates -- exploiting *differences* between markets, not similarities. Indian factor costs, sold into world markets: agri and leaf exports, nicotine exports from Mysore, IT services on its own account.
- **HUL:** Adaptation dominates -- but HUL doesn't run its own internationalization strategy at all. **It is Unilever's adaptation node for India.** Its own exports are just 2.6 percent of turnover, mostly sourcing for other Unilever companies.

> "HUL does not run an internationalization strategy. It is the object of someone else's."

### The One Place They Actually Compete: FMCG

ITC's FMCG "others" business goes head-to-head with HUL. EBIT margin: **7.4 percent vs. 21.3 percent** -- roughly a 13.8 percentage-point gap, 25 years after ITC entered the category. Two important qualifications the group made themselves:

1. ITC's FMCG business is barely a third of HUL's size -- part of the gap is **scale, not capability**.
2. ITC's headline ROC of 77.5 percent **excludes the 38,000 crore sitting at the corporate center**. Add it back and the **firm-level return is 37.1 percent** -- segment ROC is not the firm's ROC.

Also worth noting: ITC's FMCG profit grew 14.1 percent while HUL's fell. **ITC is closing the gap -- it hasn't closed it yet.**

### Governance: Independent Control vs. Global Structure

In ITC, the board and corporate management committee independently decide which businesses to enter or exit, organized by division. In HUL, major strategic decisions -- like the ice cream demerger -- are made by parent Unilever in London and then implemented in India. **ITC has independent decision-making; HUL follows a global corporate structure.**

### The Real Advantage: Internal Capital Market, Not Synergy

**Does ITC have a corporate advantage? Yes -- but it's not the one ITC claims.** If sourcing, packaging, and distribution synergies were the real driver, ITC's FMCG business should outperform HUL's -- but HUL still posts much higher operating margins.

> **Formula for the exam: ITC's real advantage is its internal capital market. Its highly profitable cigarette business generates large amounts of cash, which ITC uses to fund long-term businesses like FMCG. This patient funding built a 24,000-plus crore business over many years -- something difficult to achieve through external investors alone.**

### Recommendations: One Question, Four Decisions

Every recommendation reduces to a single question: **does this business still need patient capital, and is the return actually arriving?**

- **Paperboard:** don't sell, but put a **2-year ownership clock** -- it's an 8,000 crore business (a quarter of segment capital) earning only 8.7 percent profit, down 12.6 percent, with FY26 relief coming only from a minimum import price policy.
- **FMCG:** keep it, but replace the growth target with a **margin clock** -- the largest capital base in the group (roughly 10,000 crore) is still earning only 7.4 percent against HUL's 21.3 percent. Profit is rising, but there's a lot of catching up to do, and a deadline needs to be set.
- **Corporate center:** 37,000 crore, over half of ITC's capital, sits unallocated at a return no outsider can even compute -- contrast this with HUL, whose parenting is explicitly priced: HUL pays Unilever a **royalty of 3.45 percent of turnover**.
- **What they deliberately did *not* recommend:** don't demerge FMCG -- it's not yet ready to fund itself independently (the presentation ran out of time before this point was fully developed).

### Q&A Highlights

Unusually, this presentation drew **no questions at all** -- the professor said the group had "covered all the bases," and joked, "why are people not asking good questions today?"

## Group 13: Maruti Suzuki vs. Hyundai -- Cost and Scale vs. Technology and Global Platform

### The Opening Story

The group opened with a question: what are the top three inventions in human history? Their answer -- **the wheel** sits at the top, giving humanity its first taste of mobility, and ultimately founding the global automotive industry. In India alone, the auto sector drives roughly **7 percent of national GDP** and nearly half of manufacturing output -- **when the auto industry moves, the entire economy moves with it.**

### Why This Comparison Matters Now

The basis of competition in the auto industry is shifting. Historically, **scale, cost efficiency, localization, and distribution** were the sources of competitive advantage. Now, companies also need capability in **SUVs, electrification, software, and increasingly global manufacturing**. The group's question: who is better positioned for the future of the Indian automotive industry?

### Two Different Sources of Corporate Advantage

| | Maruti Suzuki | Hyundai Motor India |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantage source** | Cost, scale, distribution -- the traditional playbook | Technology transfer from parent, plus the Hyundai-Kia group ecosystem |
| **Market position** | Roughly 40 percent market share, high localization, largest dealer network, significant manufacturing scale | Stronger EV readiness, more premium/technology-oriented positioning |
| **Balance sheet** | Roughly 96,000 crore cash in hand, debt-free | -- |
| **Portfolio spread** | Wide and even across hatchback, sedan, SUV, MPV -- low model-concentration risk | Concentrated in SUVs (via Creta) -- premium realization today, concentration risk tomorrow |

Maruti's debt-free, cash-rich balance sheet isn't just an accounting fact -- it's a **corporate-strategy asset**: it lets Maruti take risks, invest in R&D, and fund EV/battery development without needing external permission.

### Vertical Integration

Maruti's supply base is mostly local (**roughly 92 percent localization**), while Hyundai depends on parts imported through its own group company, **Hyundai Mobis** -- a top global auto-parts manufacturer that also handles after-sales service (roughly 82 to 83 percent Hyundai localization). Value-added-to-sales: Maruti 37 percent vs. Hyundai 35 percent.

**The group's recommendation:** Maruti should pursue **strategic alliances** rather than build the same EV capability Hyundai already has from scratch -- for example, an alliance with Toyota or another automaker with similar existing capability.

### Industry Context

The Indian passenger vehicle market is growing rapidly -- roughly **4.3 million vehicles expected by 2026** -- driven by rising incomes and urbanization. Customers are moving from hatchbacks toward **compact and midsize SUVs**, where most competition and profit now sit. EV adoption is early-stage but growing steadily, supported by GST benefits on small cars and EVs and stricter emission norms.

### Diversification and the AAA Framework

At the India-entity level, both companies are effectively single-segment (Herfindahl near zero, not very useful). At the **parent** level: Suzuki scores roughly 2,100 (auto-focused); the Hyundai Motor Group scores roughly 5,150 (more diversified -- Mobis, steel, finance).

- **Maruti leans Adaptation plus Arbitrage:** small cars designed for India's cost structure, then exported to price-sensitive markets like Africa and Latin America. Maruti exported roughly 332,000 units in FY25 -- **43 percent of India's total passenger-vehicle exports, through just 14 percent of its own production.**
- **Hyundai leans Aggregation:** India is one node in a globally standardized Hyundai-Kia platform network -- which is exactly why the Creta Electric can arrive faster (it rides a shared global EV platform).

> **Formula for the exam: India is becoming central to Suzuki's global export strategy, while Hyundai India is compared internally against other Hyundai plants worldwide.**

### Competitive Positioning and SWOT

A radar-chart comparison: Maruti's biggest strengths are scale, distribution network, localization, and financial strength. Hyundai leads in technology, R&D, and EV readiness, backed by its global Hyundai-Kia ecosystem. Marketing and financial strength are the closest, most contested battlegrounds.

**The group's own bet:** Maruti has the stronger **long-term** advantage, because technology can be acquired faster than a nationwide distribution and supplier ecosystem can be built.

### Recommendations, in Three Time Horizons

| Horizon | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Immediate (0-12 months)** | Deepen the Toyota alliance into real EV core development; prioritize the Gujarat capacity expansion since Maruti is already near 100 percent capacity utilization |
| **Medium-term (1-3 years)** | Build a domestic battery-cell assembly venture with Suzuki, Toshiba, and Denso -- so Maruti isn't late again the way it was with hybrids |
| **Long-term** | Scale EV models further and grow exports beyond the current 14 percent of production |

The constant theme across all three: Maruti needs **real in-house EV and software engineering capability** to close the gap with Hyundai's head start, while still defending its balanced portfolio and its premium Nexa retail channel.

### Q&A Highlights (the liveliest session of the day)

- **Why is Maruti losing market share over time?** The group's answer: Maruti is still winning corporate advantage through traditional means (dealer network, local suppliers), but the industry is changing fast. Without innovation in design, engine capability, and especially EVs -- where Maruti's share is very low -- it will keep losing ground unless it adopts the recommendations above.
- **If Maruti moved into the premium segment, would it weaken its market share?** No, the group argued -- citing a personal example: a group member bought a Maruti Baleno at almost the same price as a friend's Hyundai Grand i10, with similar features, and felt Hyundai was actually **behind** Maruti technologically at that price point. Premiumization wouldn't necessarily hurt Maruti, but it does need to keep pace with where the industry is heading.
- **The ethanol-blending question:** with government-mandated ethanol blending (E20/E10) reducing fuel mileage, and Maruti's core edge being pricing plus mileage, how should it respond -- and does this hand Hyundai an edge? The group's honest answer: fuel efficiency has dropped for **every** manufacturer, since many existing engines (including BS4 engines) weren't designed for anything beyond roughly E5 blends. This is an industry-wide problem, not something companies have yet built a specific strategy around. A group member added a personal, relatable detail: driving a Baleno, they've noticed reduced peak performance since the blending change and find it genuinely frustrating.
- **The professor's closing line:** happy with both the presentation and the tough questions it drew, joking that "Maruti is actually facing a lot of questions anyway" -- a nod to how the class Q&A mirrored Maruti's real competitive pressure.

## Group 60: ABFRL vs. Trent -- The Diversified Empire vs. the Focused Execution Machine

### The Setup

Both companies compete in the same segment -- **Indian fashion retail** -- yet follow completely different corporate strategies. **Aditya Birla Fashion & Retail (ABFRL)** expanded aggressively by acquiring brands across multiple segments. **Trent** (Tata Group) stayed focused, building just a few strong formats: Westside and Zudio.

**The numbers at a glance:**

| Metric | ABFRL | Trent |
|---|---|---|
| **Herfindahl index (diversification)** | Roughly 6,162 -- high diversification | Near zero -- reports essentially one segment |
| **Value-added / sales** | Roughly 56.8 percent | Roughly 43.5 percent |
| **Profitability** | Revenue over 7,300 crore, but a **loss** of 624 crore | Roughly 1,534 crore of profit |

Trent's market value is estimated at roughly **20 to 25 times higher** than its scale alone would suggest -- a strong signal that investors reward disciplined execution and profitability, not just size.

### Why Compare These Two

Both are India's leading listed fashion retailers, and both belong to large business groups -- Aditya Birla Group and Tata Group respectively. ABFRL was formed in **2015** (merging Madura Fashion & Lifestyle with Pantaloons); Trent was founded in **1998** using proceeds from the sale of Lakme. ABFRL traditionally spans value, premium, ethnic, luxury, and digital formats; Trent deliberately concentrated on Westside and Zudio.

**The trigger for this comparison:** ABFRL's **May 2025 demerger of Madura Fashion & Lifestyle** -- which removed its biggest and most profitable business from the portfolio.

### Applying the Better-Off and Ownership Tests

Pantaloons and its related formats **pass both tests**, benefiting from ABFRL's capital, sourcing, and reputation. But **Madura only got a conditional pass** -- and the key insight is that Madura remained strong but **no longer needed** ABFRL's parenting support, meaning it **failed the ownership test**. That's exactly why it was demerged into a separate listed entity, Aditya Birla Lifestyle Brands Limited (ABLBL).

Trent shows much stronger alignment: **Westside, Zudio, the Zara joint venture, and Star Bazaar all sit "top right"** on the framework -- passing both tests cleanly. Tata remains the right parent, which feeds directly into Trent's stronger results.

### Diversification: Expanding Then Simplifying vs. Focused From Day One

ABFRL originally had three major segments (Madura, Pantaloons, ethnic/others), giving it a diversification score around 6,162. **After the Madura demerger, that score dropped significantly** -- more focused, but still operating across multiple formats. Trent, by contrast, has always reported essentially one operating segment.

> **Formula for the exam: ABFRL first expanded through acquisitions and is now simplifying its portfolio; Trent has remained focused from the beginning.**

### Vertical Integration: The Numbers Need Context

At first glance ABFRL looks more vertically integrated (56.8 percent vs. 43.5 percent), but this needs unpacking. ABFRL does have some in-house manufacturing (Indivinity clothing), but it's a small part of operations -- its higher ratio is really driven by premium products and licensed brands. Trent outsources manufacturing almost entirely, but **owns its key brands** (Westside, Zudio) -- giving it much stronger control over design, branding, and the customer experience.

> **Formula for the exam: both companies create value differently. ABFRL relies on a broader portfolio and premium positioning; Trent focuses on owning its brands and executing a simpler, more focused model.**

### Internationalization: Inbound Only vs. Inbound and Outbound

ABFRL mainly runs an **inbound strategy** -- bringing international brands (Ralph Lauren, Hackett, Ted Baker) into India via licensing or joint ventures. Trent runs **both inbound and outbound**: inbound through its long-standing Zara joint venture with Inditex, and outbound through Zudio, which opened its first international store in Dubai in 2024.

**A key 2025 development:** Inditex increased its ownership in the Zara joint venture from **65 percent to 80 percent**. Instead of expanding Zara further, Trent redirected capital toward growing its own Zudio business internationally. Meanwhile, after the Madura demerger, ABFRL's international brands (Reebok, American Eagle, Forever 21) moved with Madura into ABLBL -- leaving ABFRL with a **smaller** international portfolio.

### Recent Timeline

**ABFRL** (2019-2023): acquired brands to broaden its portfolio; in 2025, completed the Madura demerger and listed ABLBL separately. **Trent**: launched Zudio in 2016, expanded organically across India, opened international stores in Dubai, and kept strengthening its Zara partnership. **Overall: ABFRL relied heavily on acquisition and restructuring; Trent relied mainly on disciplined organic growth.**

### Recommendations

The core message: ABFRL should shift focus from **portfolio expansion** to **portfolio optimization**. Under horizontal scope, strengthen the existing portfolio before any new acquisitions -- current formats should focus on improving profitability, while newer, smaller value-fashion formats have the potential to become strong brands similar to Zudio. Under vertical scope, increase focus on private labels, expand in-house manufacturing (Indivinity), and leverage the Aditya Birla Group's own fiber business (Grasim) to improve margins.

### Conclusion

Both ABFRL and Trent have created corporate advantage -- through very different strategies. ABFRL followed diversified growth; Trent focused on scaling a few successful formats, and that focus has resulted in **stronger profitability**.

> **Formula for the exam: diversification is not the problem -- execution is. Going forward, ABFRL should focus on integrating its businesses and extracting greater value from what it already owns, rather than expanding further.**

### Q&A and the Extended Retail Discussion (the richest part of the whole session)

This Q&A ran long and turned into a genuine class-wide discussion about how Indian fashion retail actually works -- gold for anyone who shops at these stores and wants vivid, memorable exam examples.

**The opening question:** Pantaloons gives strong loyalty discounts (a flat percentage off, stacked on top of any other sale), while Trent's formats mostly run occasional sales or birthday offers. How does each survive with such different discount approaches? Also -- the same Aditya Birla brand (say, Allen Solly or Van Heusen) seems to have inconsistent quality between its own standalone store and when sold inside a Pantaloons store.

**A student with 10 years of retail industry experience** (who has worked with both Aditya Birla and Tata) answered: it comes down to how each company chooses to manage its **P&L**. Pantaloons may give fewer direct discounts but more loyalty points; Tata prefers to discount directly at the store level rather than build up a loyalty program. Different approaches, same underlying goal -- balancing the P&L.

**The professor's own read, layered on top:**

> Trent believes in a **fast-fashion, quick-launch** model, typically staying under a price ceiling (around 999 rupees), aiming for high volume within an affordable band. Store inventory rotates quickly, so a returning customer always finds something new to choose from -- while staying affordable. Trent has also made a very deliberate choice about **where** its stores sit and **who** its target segment is, encouraging repeat visits and habit formation.

**On discount-heavy strategies specifically:** giving a lot of discounts isn't necessarily a good thing -- it can mean pushing inventory out at a lower margin, or even at a loss.

**On quality vs. price as a trade-off:** the professor's sharpest observation of the day.

> Some of the older branded stores (like Pantaloons-owned brands) have, over the last few years, started compromising on cloth quality or styling at the lower price points -- operating under what he called a **false belief** that only products priced above roughly 2,000 rupees deserve better sourcing and quality. Zudio, by contrast, **has not treated quality and price as a trade-off at all** -- it provides good quality even at its lower price segment, achieved through **ruthless backward integration and scale**. That is precisely why Zudio keeps bringing customers back to physical stores -- "not a small challenge."

**A student's own 12-13-year experience as a loyal Pantaloons customer** confirmed this pattern directly: the first five years (pre-COVID) genuinely had good fashion and cloth quality; from around COVID onward, quality and pricing value fell even though customer service stayed good. Pantaloons is now trying to catch up -- targeting Gen Z fashion and affordable, good-quality formal wear, and relaunching a value-fashion-format brand at a sub-1,200-rupee price point in a Zudio-like format. The student's own verdict: it might be "a lost battle," since Westside and Zudio have already established their position.

**The professor's synthesis on switching a brand's core motive:**

> "Bringing back the customer and creating that as a habit will be very difficult" once a loyal customer base has formed around one specific reason for shopping somewhere, and you then change that motive altogether.

**The Zara tangent:** a student pointed out Zara's ability to manufacture **fashion urgency/scarcity** -- once a design leaves the shelf, it's rarely restocked, and Zara's supply chain is tightly integrated enough that the design-to-store cycle can be as short as roughly two weeks.

**The professor's response:** Trent has **not** "100 percent copied" Zara's fashion-urgency model -- it's becoming stronger over time, but was never as intensely fashion-forward as Zara. What they do share: strong backward integration and scale, plus quick inventory turnover with minimal repetition. Where Trent differs from Zara: Trent built a **much stronger, more scaled B2C physical retail front-end**, with a very deliberate choice of store locations -- deliberately avoiding dependence on big malls, instead locating where ordinary shoppers naturally go, and even creating a **comfortable, safe shopping space specifically for women.**

The professor flagged a genuine risk ahead: Trent will likely face **format-level saturation and competition** (including from ABFRL's own catch-up efforts), and its growth rate could slow even as it stays ahead of rivals for now.

**The Zara-positioning tangent:** in India, Zara is seen as a **premium** store, whereas in the West it's a normal, mass-market retailer -- the professor compared this to how Coca-Cola was once treated as a status symbol in India. Zara has also faced recent quality backlash in India, tied to **where** specific garments are manufactured -- items made in India or the UK are seen as fine, while some made in countries like Indonesia are seen as lower quality.

**The sourcing-economics tangent** (built collaboratively by several students and the professor):

- Garments manufactured in **Turkey** tend to carry higher prices than the same item made in **Bangladesh**, reflecting different input costs.
- **Turkish and Egyptian cotton** are considered among the best in the world -- premium brands sourcing from there can't offer very low prices because input costs are high. Turkey has also faced heavy inflation and broader economic mismanagement in recent years, which likely feeds into its higher manufacturing costs.
- **Bangladesh**, despite a period of political instability, still offers relatively lower-cost, good-quality production capability. An interesting detail the professor added: a significant share of that manufacturing capacity is actually financially owned by **Indian investors**, even though the factories are legally Bangladeshi companies.
- For **fashion apparel**, sourcing tends to come from Bangladesh; for **sports apparel** (more synthetic, R&D-heavy materials rather than pure cotton), sourcing tends to come from the Far East or Vietnam and nearby countries instead -- likely reflecting more machine-driven than labor-driven production. The professor was careful to flag this last point as informed conjecture, not certain fact.

The professor closed by naming all six comparisons of the day -- Voltas vs. Havells, Dabur vs. Marico, Tata Steel vs. JSW Steel, ITC vs. HUL, Maruti Suzuki vs. Hyundai, and ABFRL vs. Trent -- noting that everyone in the room has some personal stake in buying clothes, which is exactly why the last discussion ran so long. He signed off hoping the momentum would carry into the next presentation slot later that day.

## The Big Pattern Across All Six Presentations

Look at the six pairings side by side, and **one single storyline repeats itself six times** -- which is exactly the kind of pattern-recognition an exam question loves to test.

| Pairing | The "leans on a parent/ecosystem" side | The "builds its own internal engine" side |
|---|---|---|
| Voltas vs. Havells | Voltas -- Tata ecosystem belongingness | Havells -- one unified brand, internal matrix |
| Dabur vs. Marico | Dabur -- 140-year legacy, herbal R&D moat | Marico -- lean, agile, brand-building machine |
| Tata Steel vs. JSW Steel | Tata Steel -- global integration and acquisitions | JSW Steel -- capital-efficient domestic scale |
| ITC vs. HUL | HUL -- Unilever's global adaptation node | ITC -- independently controlled internal capital market |
| Maruti vs. Hyundai | Hyundai -- Hyundai-Kia global platform and tech transfer | Maruti -- self-built dealer network, localization, cash reserves |
| ABFRL vs. Trent | ABFRL -- Aditya Birla Group's capital and brand acquisitions | Trent -- Tata-backed, but built its own focused execution model |

**Think of it like two different survival strategies in nature:** one organism thrives by living in symbiosis with a much larger host (the parent-ecosystem strategy); the other survives by evolving an unusually efficient, self-contained metabolism (the focused-internal-integration strategy). Neither strategy is universally superior -- what matters, across every single case this session, is whether the **structure actually matches the goal.**

This is the same lesson the course opened with in the Business Groups sessions and the Tata-parenting-advantage discussions -- it has simply come back, fully tested, through six real, current Indian companies.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Better-Off Test** | Whether a business unit is genuinely stronger for being part of the parent company (versus standing alone) -- used to justify the Madura/Pantaloons vs. ABFRL analysis |
| **Ownership Test** | Whether the parent still needs to *own* a business to deliver that advantage, or whether the business could get the same benefit without full ownership -- Madura failing this test triggered its demerger |
| **Herfindahl Index** | A diversification score measuring the evenness of revenue across a company's segments -- a high score can mean either genuine relatedness (HUL) or true unrelated conglomerate spread (ITC); the number alone doesn't tell you which |
| **Value-Added-to-Sales Ratio** | The standard metric for vertical integration -- the higher the ratio, the more of the product's value the company creates itself rather than buying externally |
| **Holdup Problem** | The risk of relying on a market with few, high-uncertainty suppliers who can control price or refuse to supply -- the classic justification for backward integration (herbs/honey for Dabur, copra for Marico, leaf tobacco for ITC) |
| **Asset Specificity** | Why some inputs can't simply be bought on an open market -- e.g., ITC's very specific leaf grades for cigarette blends have no reliable spot market, forcing backward integration |
| **AAA Framework** | Adaptation (responding to local needs), Aggregation (leveraging global scale/standardization), Arbitrage (exploiting cost/market differences) -- used to classify each company's internationalization style |
| **Breadth vs. Depth Internationalization** | Breadth = spreading across many countries (Dabur, ABFRL inbound licensing); Depth = concentrated leadership in a few markets (Marico) -- each carries a different risk profile (e.g., Marico's Bangladesh political-risk exposure) |
| **Internal Capital Market** | A conglomerate's ability to fund long-gestation businesses using cash generated by a highly profitable core business (ITC's cigarette cash funding its FMCG build-out) rather than relying on external investors |
| **Corporate Advantage Formula** | Portfolio times Capabilities times Organization -- the three-part lens used to judge whether a diversified company's structure genuinely creates value |
| **Parent-Ecosystem Leverage** | Growth strategy that relies on belonging to a larger group (Tata, Aditya Birla, Hyundai-Kia) for capital, trust, distribution, or technology, instead of building every capability independently |
| **Focused Internal Integration** | The alternative growth strategy: building one tightly coordinated internal engine (single brand, dense distribution, matrix structure) rather than relying on an external parent group |
| **Segment ROC vs. Firm ROC** | A segment's return on capital can look artificially high if it excludes unallocated corporate-center capital -- always check whether center capital has been added back before comparing across companies |
| **Fashion Urgency / Scarcity Model** | Zara's retail tactic of deliberately not restocking a design once it leaves the shelf, creating perceived scarcity and repeat store visits -- distinct from a discount-driven retail strategy |
| **Quality-Price Trade-off (or its absence)** | The assumption that lower price must mean lower quality -- Zudio's model deliberately breaks this assumption through backward integration and scale, unlike some legacy branded-store formats |
