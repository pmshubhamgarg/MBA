# Session 4: Long-Term Financing — Balance Sheet vs. Project Finance

*How do you pay for a power plant versus an expressway? Same country, same decade, very different answers.*

---

## The Setup: Two Projects, One Big Question

Imagine you are a CFO. Two deals land on your desk the same week.

**Deal 1:** Buy a running coal-fired power plant from L&T. It has been humming along for 12 years, customers are locked in with a long-term Power Purchase Agreement (PPA), and the technology is proven. You need about 4,000 crore of debt.

**Deal 2:** Build a brand-new 130 km expressway between Meerut and Badaun. No guaranteed traffic. No PPA. Revenue depends on how many trucks and cars show up. You need about 6,500 crore.

Both are infrastructure. Both need thousands of crores. But the financing routes are completely different. This session answers why -- and teaches you how to choose the right route for any project.

> **Professor's framing:** "You have a target structure, but what will be your actual structure? It depends on how you finance your long-term investment requirements."

---

## The Two Worlds: Balance Sheet Financing vs. Project Financing

Think of it like two ways to borrow money for a house.

**Balance sheet financing** is like a regular home loan where the bank looks at your total salary, all your savings, your other properties -- your entire financial picture. If this one investment goes bad, the bank can come after everything you own.

**Project financing** is like saying: "I am building a rental property. Judge me only on the rent this building will earn. Don't look at my other stuff. If this building fails, you cannot touch my other assets."

| Feature | Balance Sheet Financing | Project Financing |
|---------|------------------------|-------------------|
| **Lender's recourse** | Entire balance sheet of borrower | Only the project's cash flows |
| **Legal structure** | Loan sits on borrower's books | Separate SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle) created |
| **Impact on leverage** | Directly affects company's debt-to-equity | Off the parent's balance sheet |
| **Interest rate** | Lower (lender has cushion) | Higher (100-200 basis points more -- lender carries more risk) |
| **When to use** | Known business, stable cash flows, manageable risk | Unknown territory, high risk, need to "ring-fence" |

> **Professor's key distinction:** "In balance sheet financing, the entire balance sheet of the borrower is available to the lender. In project financing, the lender can recover their dues only from the cash flow of the project. If the project fails, they cannot go back to the borrower."

---

## What is an SPV?

A **Special Purpose Vehicle** is a legal entity -- typically structured as a trust or partnership, not a traditional company -- created specifically for one project.

Think of it as a "firewall." If you run 10 expressways, you create 10 SPVs. If expressway number 7 collapses financially, the lender of that SPV cannot reach into expressway number 3's cash box. That is **legal ring-fencing**.

A student asked: "Is an SPV a separate entity inside a company?"

> **Professor's response:** "How can a separate entity be inside a company? It is a legal entity. If the holding company is the main sponsor, then it becomes a subsidiary. And that's why the legal structure is not a company -- they are formed like a trust or a partnership."

The SPV itself is typically tiny -- maybe 1 crore of paid-up capital -- but it can handle a 6,500 crore project. It is a **shell** by design. And that is precisely the problem: with no assets of its own, who would lend it money? The answer requires transferring every risk to someone who can handle it.

## Rating Downgrades: Why Debt Capacity Matters

Before diving into the cases, a crucial concept from tradeoff theory that shows up in real life: **your credit rating is not forever**.

Imagine you have an existing loan at 7.5% fixed rate. Two years later, your rating drops from AAA to A. Not only does your future borrowing become expensive -- your **existing loan can reprice too**.

> **Professor's warning:** "I have seen many loan agreements where the lender says the loan will be repriced if there is a downgrade -- say 25 basis points for a one-notch downgrade. So suppose you got the loan two years ago at 7.5% fixed rate, and then your rating goes down. Not only does it affect your future borrowing capacity and cost, it also affects your past borrowing."

This is why companies carefully guard their debt capacity. Overleveraging triggers a downgrade spiral: more debt leads to a lower rating leads to higher interest on both new and old loans leads to distress.

## The Three Instruments of Long-Term Financing

Every company raising long-term capital has three broad choices:

| Instrument | What it is | Best for |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **Debt** | Loans and bonds -- you borrow and repay with interest | Mature companies with predictable cash flows |
| **Equity** | Issue shares -- give up ownership for capital | When debt capacity is maxed or risk is too high |
| **Hybrids** | Part debt, part equity (e.g., convertible bonds) | Startups and early-stage firms facing a valuation squeeze |

The choice depends on two things: your **business model and sector**, and your **stage of maturity**. A contracted utility and a tech startup both need thousands of crores, but the instruments available to them are completely different.

### The Startup Funding Trap

A quick detour the professor took to illustrate why instrument choice depends on life cycle stage:

Imagine your startup is worth 20 lakh. You need 2 crore to scale. Where do you go?

- **Bank?** Will not lend -- no collateral, no track record, no history.
- **VC?** Will only give equity. For 2 crore into a 20-lakh company, the VC says: "Give me 90% ownership." You just became a minority shareholder in your own company.
- **Bond market?** Forget it -- you need a prospectus, a track record, a rating.

That is why startups use **hybrid instruments** (convertible notes, SAFE agreements) -- they defer the valuation problem to a later date when the company's worth is clearer.

> **Professor's framing:** "For mature companies, you will see more traditional instruments being used. For startups, it's a different ballgame. What you see more commonly are hybrids. Long-term financing depends on the life cycle stage at which you operate."

## Case Study 1: Torrent Power -- Acquiring a Brownfield Power Plant

### The Facts

- **Company:** Torrent Power (Gujarat-based, mature power company)
- **Rating:** AA+ (one notch below AAA -- solid)
- **Objective:** Acquire 100% of Nava Power from L&T (a supercritical coal-fired power plant, two units of 700 MW each, operating since 2014)
- **Plant age:** 12 years old (typical life: 25-30 years, so roughly half-life remaining)
- **Key advantage 1:** Supercritical technology -- more efficient, lower operating cost
- **Key advantage 2:** Fully contracted long-tenure PPA -- revenue is assured
- **Key advantage 3:** Already operating -- no construction risk, no technology risk
- **Opex:** Fixed at approximately 6% of capex
- **Strategy:** Entry into north India as part of a 60,000 crore capex pipeline

### Back-of-the-Envelope Valuation

The professor walked the class through a quick calculation:

- A **new** supercritical plant costs 9-12 crore per MW
- This plant is 12 years old (roughly 45% of its life is gone)
- A **used** plant of this type: 4.5-6.5 crore per MW
- Taking the midpoint of about 5 crore per MW:

> **5 crore x 1,400 MW = 7,000 crore** (total project value)

Torrent wants to borrow 4,000 crore. That gives us:

> **4,000 / 7,000 = roughly 57%, rounded to 60% debt**

Capital structure for this acquisition: approximately **60% debt, 40% equity**. This is standard practice in India (can go up to 70:30).

> **Professor's observation:** "You can imagine that if 60% to 70% is loan, who bears the risk? The lender bears the risk. If the project goes bad, the equity provider has only put 30-40% of the money."

### Existing Leverage Check

Since this is balance sheet financing, the new debt goes on Torrent's existing books. So we must check whether the company can handle it.

| Metric | Current (pre-acquisition) |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Enterprise Value | ~7,000 crore |
| Equity | ~3,700 crore |
| Promoter Loan | ~495 crore |
| Net Debt | ~2,733 crore |
| Net Debt / EBITDA | **1.4x** |

Current split: about 40% debt, 60% equity. Adding 4,000 crore of new debt will push leverage up significantly, but with Net Debt / EBITDA at only 1.4x (recall from the Tata Steel capital structure discussion -- theirs was 3.1x), there is room within manageable guardrails.

The lender takes comfort from two safeguards: (1) the asset is already generating cash flows for 12 years, and (2) the long-term PPA eliminates revenue uncertainty going forward.

### Decision 1: Balance Sheet or Project Finance?

The class was divided -- some students argued for project financing, citing the PPA as a way to ring-fence risk. The professor was emphatic.

**Answer: Balance Sheet Financing. No doubt about it.**

Three clear reasons:

**Reason 1 -- Minimal project risk, so ring-fencing adds cost without benefit:**
- Revenue is assured through a long-term PPA
- Opex is fixed (~6% of capex)
- The plant has been running for 12 years -- no technology risk

> **Professor:** "SPV is used when you cannot handle the risk. You want to allocate it to people who can handle it better. Here, because of contracted PPA, there is minimum project risk. Your revenue is assured. Opex is also fixed."

**Reason 2 -- Same business, no expertise gap:**
- Torrent is already a power company buying a power asset
- They know how to run a power plant

> **Professor:** "If I know everything, I don't need an expert to run that business. Why should I create an SPV? I know it. There would be operating cost of 10 people I have to hire. There is compliance expenditure. Why?"

**Reason 3 -- Cost difference:**
Project financing would charge 100-200 basis points more in interest because the lender loses recourse to the broader balance sheet. For a low-risk acquisition, this premium buys you nothing.

A student pressed: "What if Torrent's other projects were loss-making? Wouldn't project finance protect this good asset?" The professor's response was sharp:

> "If Torrent Power is a loss-making company, L&T will not sell their power plant to them in the first place. They want a good price. It is only a profit-making company that can offer a good price."

Another student worried about balance sheet exposure. The professor clarified a common misconception:

> "Balance sheet financing does not mean I take from others, not from the project. It says if the project fails, I have recourse on others. If the project is generating cash flow, the lender collects from the project first."

### Decision 2: Bank Loan or Bond Issuance?

Now that we have chosen balance sheet financing, we need 4,000 crore of **debt**. Two channels are available:

| Channel | How it works |
|---------|-------------|
| **Bank Loan** | Borrow directly from a bank |
| **Bond Issuance** | Issue debt instruments that investors buy |

Students debated. One argued bank loans are faster. The professor countered: "Bank will take its own sweet time. 1 month, 45 days. It's not a fintech company that in 36 seconds you get your loan."

**Important context about India's bond market:**

- **90% of corporate bonds are privately placed** (not publicly listed or traded)
- The private bond market is actually **more liquid** than the public bond market -- the reverse of equity
- **Banks are the majority issuers** of bonds in India

This creates an interesting economic chain:

> **Professor's logic:** "If a bank is issuing bonds to raise money, and then giving you a loan -- you are indirectly raising from the bond market through the bank. They will charge their own sweet spread. So a bank loan can become costly, not in terms of interest alone but in terms of covenants, governance -- they want a seat, they'll have their own say."

Banks also have **sectoral exposure limits**. If Torrent's existing bank already has heavy power sector exposure, they might refuse: "I already have enough power loans. Go somewhere else."

### Decision 3: Fixed Rate or Floating Rate?

This is where the professor introduced real numbers and the discussion got very practical.

| Parameter | Bond (Fixed Rate) | Bank Loan (Floating Rate) |
|-----------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Interest rate** | 7.97% | 7.57% |
| **Rate type** | Fixed for 10 years | Floating (changes with market) |
| **Spread** | -- | 40 basis points below fixed |
| **Principal** | Bullet (repaid at end) | Assume bullet for simplicity |

**The savings math:**

A student (Gaurav) quickly calculated:

> **40 basis points x 4,000 crore = 16 crore per year**
>
> **16 crore x 10 years = 160 crore total savings** (absolute, not time-value adjusted)

Sounds like floating wins, right? Not so fast.

The professor asked a provocative question: "If floating is always lower than fixed at any point in time, why not take a series of one-year floating rate loans and refinance every year?"

A student (Kumar Pratap) immediately sensed the trap: "For long-term needs, we should not be taking short-term loans."

The professor confirmed: there is a **term premium** -- long-term rates are always higher than short-term rates. And if the yield curve is upward-sloping, each refinancing costs more. Plus you face the risk that a lender simply refuses to refinance.

**The Real Answer: Go Fixed Rate (Bond)**

> **Professor's decisive principle:** "My cash flow from operations -- selling electricity -- is at a fixed rate. I have a PPA agreement, long-tenure. So when my cash flow from operation is less volatile, why do you want to invite volatility on your financing cost?"

The logic is beautifully simple: **match the nature of your cash flows to the nature of your financing costs**.

- Fixed revenue (from PPA) + fixed interest (from bond) = **predictable, manageable**
- Fixed revenue + floating interest = **unnecessary uncertainty**

The breakeven is only 40 basis points. If floating rates rise by even 41 basis points above today's fixed rate, the bank loan becomes more expensive. Over a 10-year horizon, that is a very real risk you cannot control.

> **Professor:** "I do not know my future obligation of interest with floating. But in a fixed, even if it is high, I know my obligation. And that is good enough because my assets which are giving me revenue are also under long-term contract."

**The Signaling Bonus:**

A student worried that issuing bonds publicly might signal financial distress. The professor flipped this using capital structure theory:

> "If I say I'm taking a 10-year loan at a fixed rate of interest, the market gets a signal: I'm very confident of repayment. I am confident about my cash flow generation capacity in future. Just opposite."

Remember from Sessions 2-3: issuing equity signals overvaluation (management thinks shares are expensive, so they sell). Issuing long-term fixed-rate debt signals confidence.

### Decision 4: Convertible or Non-Convertible Bond?

The final choice. A **convertible bond (CD)** gives the lender an option to convert debt into equity shares. A **non-convertible debenture (NCD)** is pure debt -- pay interest, return principal, end of story.

**Answer: Non-Convertible Debenture (NCD)**

Several students got this right. The reasoning:

> **Professor:** "If you are doubtful about your future, then you distribute the risk -- why should you only suffer, let the other guy also suffer. But if you're confident that my cash flow is stable, it's a fixed rate interest rate, my project is going to give me cash flow for the next 12-13 years, my loan is for 10 years -- why give the upside benefit to your lender?"

With a convertible bond, if the company does well, the lender converts to equity and captures the upside through dividends and capital appreciation. That is value leaking from existing shareholders to bondholders.

**A sharp student observation (Raava):** Torrent has a 60,000 crore capex pipeline ahead. Future greenfield projects carry higher risk and might genuinely need equity. Issuing a convertible bond now would dilute equity prematurely -- better to preserve that option for later.

**Note on convertible bond types:**

> **Professor:** "Typically these are called optionally convertible bonds. You give a right or option to both parties -- the issuer and the investor. If I'm doing really well, I don't want to share it with equity. I will repay and not exercise the right of conversion."

If bonds are **compulsorily convertible**, there is no repayment option -- only conversion to equity. Optional conversion gives both sides flexibility.

### Torrent Power: Final Answer Summary

| Decision | Choice | Reasoning |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| **Structure** | Balance Sheet Financing | Same business, low risk, PPA assurance, no SPV overhead needed |
| **Instrument** | Bond Issuance (not bank loan) | Banks only offer floating for 10-year loans; need fixed rate |
| **Rate Type** | Fixed (7.97%) | Match stable operating cash flows with stable financing costs |
| **Bond Type** | Non-Convertible Debenture (NCD) | Cash flows are stable; no reason to share equity upside with lender |

## Case Study 2: IRB Meerut-Badaun Expressway -- The Risky Build

### The Facts

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Project** | 129.7 km, 6-lane greenfield expressway (Meerut to Badaun) |
| **Total cost** | ~6,500 crore |
| **Sponsor** | IRB Infrastructure Trust |
| **SPV created** | Meerut Badaun Express Limited (separate legal entity) |
| **Concession** | 30 years (extendable to 36 if recovery is insufficient -- 20% extension) |
| **Revenue source** | Toll collection -- depends entirely on traffic volume |

### Why Project Finance is the Only Option Here

This is the **exact opposite** of Torrent Power. Three critical risk factors make balance sheet financing impractical:

**1. No Revenue Guarantee**

There is no PPA equivalent. Revenue depends on how many trucks and cars use the expressway. Your customers are retail -- individuals who choose whether to take the road or not. Politicians might block toll booths. A new railway line could divert freight traffic.

> **Professor:** "Here there is no revenue guarantee. It depends on the footfall, meaning traffic. There is no PPA with the customer."

**2. Asset Specificity Risk**

Even though IRB has been building expressways for years, **each expressway is unique**. A Mumbai expressway and a Meerut expressway face completely different traffic patterns, regional economics, and competitive pressures.

> **Professor's comparison:** "Whether you have a 1,000 megawatt supercritical power plant in Chennai or in Meerut, economics is same. But two expressways in two different regions -- economics is not same. They are not comparable. They are not fungible."

**3. Balance Sheet Financing Would Be Ruinous**

Without ring-fencing, the bank would "charge through the moon" because it would see no reliable fallback. If this expressway fails, IRB's other expressways (each facing their own unique risks) cannot serve as reliable collateral.

Ring-fencing protects both ways: it protects IRB's parent balance sheet if the project fails, and it protects this project from being dragged down by problems elsewhere.

## How Project Finance Works: The PPP Structure

### The DBFO Model

**DBFO = Design, Build, Finance, and Operate**

In project finance for infrastructure, the developer (SPV) must handle four phases:

| Phase | What happens |
|-------|-------------|
| **Design** | Outsourced to architects/engineers with relevant track record |
| **Build** | Contracted to builders with construction experience |
| **Finance** | Developer arranges the debt and equity mix |
| **Operate** | Developer maintains and runs the asset for the entire concession period |

The developer does not own the underlying land. They have access rights for the concession period. After 30 years, the asset goes back to the government or sponsor.

### The IIM Udaipur Hostel Example -- A Real PPP Story

To make project finance tangible, the professor shared his own experience leading the **first-ever PPP project by an IIM** in India.

**The Problem:**
- IIM Udaipur needed new hostels. Cost: 100 crore.
- Pecking order check: Retained earnings? Almost zero. Debt? Already 70 crore on the books. Banks and HEFA are options but have their own limitations.
- Solution: Create an SPV and use a PPP structure.

**The Developer's Challenge:**

The developer selected through competitive bidding was an experienced infrastructure firm -- but they had built roads, defense quarters, and malls. They had **zero track record in academic hostels**.

No lender would fund them on experience alone. So the developer had to show how every risk was transferred to competent parties:

- **Design risk** -- transferred to an architect with hostel design experience
- **Construction risk** -- transferred to a builder who had done campus residential projects
- **Revenue risk** -- this is the hardest one

### Making the Project Bankable: The Annuity Model

Even with design and construction risks transferred, the lender asked the critical question: **"Who will pay you?"**

The institute signed an **annuity agreement**: pay the developer 7 crore per year for the concession period, with inflation adjustments every two years.

**How the tender worked:**

The institute's bid document specified:
- Project cost: 100 crore
- Concession period: 30 years
- Annual annuity: 7 crore (fixed, inflation-adjusted)

The developer calculates: 7 crore per year x 30 years = 210 crore inflow on a 100 crore investment. IRR comes out to roughly **7%**.

But a private developer needs at least **12%** to justify the risk. There is a **5% viability gap**.

### Viability Gap Funding (VGF) -- The "Public" in PPP

This is where the government steps in. The gap between the project's natural return (7%) and the developer's required return (12%) is bridged by a **capital grant from the government** -- paid upfront during construction.

**How the competitive bid works:**

The developer bids on one number: **how much VGF they need**.

- Maximum permissible VGF for this pilot project: 60 crore (60% of cost -- normally 30% in India)
- The developer who asks for the **least VGF wins**
- The winning developer asked for only **18 crore** (instead of the maximum 60)

> **Professor:** "In the bid document I mentioned: 100 crore project, 30-year concession, 7 crore is what I'm going to pay every year. They have to bid for the viability gap funding. The guy who bids with the least VGF wins the project."

With the institute guaranteeing 7 crore per year AND the government guaranteeing 18 crore upfront, the revenue risk was covered. **Only then** would the lender sign off.

### Why 30 Years, Not 10? -- The Rod Diameter Argument

The board challenged the professor: "It's only 100 crore. Pay them off in 10 years."

> **Professor's brilliant answer:** "If I go for a 10-year concession period, the developer -- when they build the columns, pillars -- the rod diameter will be 6 mm. If I go for a 30-year concession, the diameter will be 12 mm. Because this fellow knows he has to maintain it for 30 years. I'm not going to pay anything extra. So I went for 30 years."

**Memory trick:** A longer concession is not just about spreading payments -- it is an **incentive alignment device**. The builder builds better because they eat their own cooking for 30 years.

There is also a disincentive for construction delays: if the developer takes 36 months instead of 24 to build, they lose 12 months of their revenue-earning window. The concession period does not extend for delays (except in specific cases).

### Why 13% is Not Expensive -- The Effective Cost Lesson

The board's other objection: "A bank offers 8%. HEFA offers 5.5%. You are paying 13% IRR to this developer. Are you crazy?"

> **Professor's answer:** "Don't go by nominal percentages -- these are misleading. If I take a bank loan at 8%, who will maintain the hostels for 30 years? I have to maintain them, and that has a cost. Add that maintenance cost to the 8%, and the effective cost comes to the same -- around 13%."

**Takeaway: Always compare effective cost of financing, not nominal interest rates.**

## The Expressway Funding Structure

Back to the Meerut-Badaun Expressway. Here is how the 6,500 crore was actually funded:

| Source | Amount (Crore) | Share | Details |
|--------|---------------|-------|---------|
| **VGF** (Government) | ~1,746 | ~27% | UP Industrial Development Authority |
| **Equity** (IRB + GIC Singapore) | ~2,133 | ~33% | Risk capital from sponsors |
| **Senior Secured Debt** (Bank consortium) | ~2,659 | ~41% | Secured against project cash flows; senior; amortizing |
| **Total** | **~6,538** | **100%** | |

**Overall capital structure:** roughly 55% debt, 45% equity. Of the 45% equity, about half comes from GIC, so IRB's net equity contribution is around 22%.

### Why GIC (Singapore Sovereign Wealth Fund) Matters

Having a sovereign wealth fund as an equity partner gives two enormous advantages:

**1. Certification Effect:**

> **Professor:** "If GIC is putting money, lender will think okay they must have done due diligence. It must be a good project."

**2. Patient Capital:**

Unlike private equity funds that want returns in 3-5 years, sovereign wealth funds can wait 10-15 years.

> **Professor:** "For an expressway of such a huge length, 6,000 crore investment, you cannot get back money in 5 years. Impossible. So you need players who have patience, who can stay with the sponsors."

### The Senior Debt Details

The bank loan under project finance is structured carefully to protect the lender:

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---------|---------------|
| **Secured** | Against the SPV's cash flows (not land -- the SPV does not own the land) |
| **Senior** | Gets repaid before other obligations |
| **Amortizing** | Principal is repaid throughout the loan life, so effective interest decreases over time (unlike Torrent's bullet bond) |
| **7-10 year tenor** | Much shorter than the 30-year concession -- lender exits early |

### Who Really Bears the Risk If It Fails?

If the expressway fails (traffic dries up, competing routes emerge):

1. **Lender** bears the most pain -- 41% exposure, non-recourse, cannot touch IRB's other assets
2. **GIC** is next -- their equity is stuck with a dead project
3. **Government** does not lose much -- they created a social asset (the road) and gave a grant, not a loan
4. **IRB (sponsor)** is hit the least -- net equity is only about 22%

The professor noted a common practice: sponsors often have equity stakes in the construction companies that build the expressway. They extract value through construction contracts **during the building phase**, long before the concession period generates toll revenue.

> **Professor's candid observation:** "The sponsor gets money out of the project much faster. They don't wait for the residual period. It is GIC who will be stuck, and the lender. And that's why they need proper ring-fencing and allocation of all risks."

## The Evolution of PPP in India: A Quick History

A student (Harsh) asked how effective the PPP model has really been in India. The professor traced the painful evolution:

**Phase 1 -- BOT (Build Operate Transfer):**
Developer builds the road, collects toll, eventually transfers the asset back. **Problem:** Developers would not come. Too risky. Politicians break toll booths. No guaranteed revenue. Banks refused to fund.

**Phase 2 -- Annuity Model (AM):**
Government (NHAI) says: "Forget toll collection. I will pay you a guaranteed annuity every six months." Developers came back. **New problem:** Construction delays from land acquisition issues, environmental clearances. Every month of delay ate into their recovery period.

**Phase 3 -- Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM):**
Government provides **40% capital grant** during construction to cover delay risk PLUS annuity for operations. Net developer equity needed: about 18%. **New problem:** NHAI started borrowing heavily to fund those 40% grants. NHAI's debt exploded from 40,000 crore to **2,31,000 crore** in six years. Government stopped the scheme.

**Current state:** In most sectors, annuities are limited. Developers are expected to raise their own revenue and run the asset. PPP still exists but with far fewer sweeteners.

### Real-World Examples of Infrastructure Risk

- **GMR Infra** was awarded the Kishangarh-Udaipur-Ahmedabad road project. Mid-execution, the government announced the **Dedicated Freight Corridor (DFC)** for railways. GMR surrendered the project because freight traffic would divert to rail, making the road unviable.

- **Delhi Airport T3** was built with certain traffic projections. After T3 opened, IndiGo started operating from T1, diverting passenger traffic. The concession period was extended from **30 to 60 years** -- doubled -- so the developer could recover their investment.

> **Professor:** "Infrastructure projects are risky projects. That's why SPV."

## External Commercial Borrowings: The Currency Trap

A student asked about borrowing from abroad, where nominal rates are lower (say 5% from the UK vs. 8% in India).

> **Professor's warning:** "Don't compare 5% and 8% and say 5% is cheaper. You cannot compare. With the 5% you have to add the currency swap rate. Given the vulnerability of our currency, external loans are costlier because of the swap rate, not because of nominal interest rate difference."

**Memory trick:** Nominal rates across currencies are like apples and oranges. You must add the **currency hedge cost** (swap rate) to make them comparable. The professor used the same IIM hostel example: "Like my PPP project -- don't compare 5.5% from HEFA and 8% from a bank and 13% IRR to a developer. Add the hidden costs."

## Refinancing: When Does It Make Sense?

A student asked about refinancing costs. The professor clarified:

- **Refinance when rates are going down** -- you replace expensive old debt with cheaper new debt
- **Do not refinance when the yield curve is upward-sloping** -- each successive loan will cost more because of the term premium
- Transaction costs are real -- legal fees, documentation, and the risk that a new lender says no

> **Professor's rule:** "Normally people do refinancing when the interest rate is going down, not when interest rate is going up."

## The Decision Framework: Generalizing Beyond These Cases

The professor stressed that the real learning is not about Torrent or IRB -- it is about building a transferable framework.

### When to Use Balance Sheet Financing

- You know the business (same sector as the new asset)
- Cash flows are predictable (long-term contracts, PPA)
- You have a good credit rating with borrowing capacity remaining
- Ring-fencing would add cost with little benefit (100-200 bps premium for no real risk reduction)

### When to Use Project Financing

- New territory (different sector, no track record)
- Revenue is uncertain (depends on market conditions, footfall, traffic)
- Asset specificity -- each project is unique and non-fungible across locations
- You want to protect the parent's balance sheet from project failure
- Risk needs to be allocated to multiple stakeholders (PPP, VGF, specialized contractors)

### The Generalization Test

The professor ended with a prompt to verify understanding:

**Scenario A:** A developer plans a 5,000 crore **solar park** with a 25-year PPA.
- Contracted cash flows, long-term PPA -- very close to Torrent Power
- **Answer: Balance sheet financing, fixed-rate NCD**

**Scenario B:** An early-stage **EV charging startup** needs capital.
- No track record, bank will not lend, no bond market access, no prospectus
- **Answer: Hybrid instruments (convertible notes, venture capital structures)**

### Key Formulae

| Calculation | Expression |
|------------|-----------|
| **Debt share in project** | Debt amount / Total project value |
| **Interest saving (fixed vs floating)** | Basis point spread x Loan amount x Tenure |
| **Breakeven basis points** | Fixed rate - Current floating rate |
| **VGF percentage** | VGF amount / Total project cost |
| **Effective cost of financing** | Nominal interest + Maintenance/operating cost (if self-managed) |

## Memory Tricks

- **Balance sheet = "Open house"** -- lender can see everything, lower rate
- **Project finance = "Ring-fenced room"** -- lender sees only one project, higher rate
- **SPV = Firewall** -- protects parent if project burns, protects project from parent's fires
- **PPA = Sleep well at night** -- contracted revenue means go fixed-rate, non-convertible, balance sheet
- **No PPA = SPV + PPP** -- allocate every risk to the party best equipped to handle it
- **Match your financing to your cash flows** -- fixed revenue deserves fixed cost; volatile revenue needs risk-sharing structures
- **30-year rod test** -- longer concessions mean the developer builds stronger because they eat their own cooking
- **Don't compare nominal rates across currencies** -- add the swap rate first
- **Don't compare nominal rates across structures** -- add the hidden maintenance cost
