## Part 1: Share Buyback -- Completing the Payout Policy Story

### Dividend vs. Buyback: What Drives the Choice?

Imagine you are the CEO. The board says: "We have 100 crore surplus cash. Distribute it to shareholders. You choose the mode -- dividend or buyback." Here are the factors that tilt the decision:

#### Factor 1: Undervaluation Signal

If the firm is **currently undervalued** and you want to send a positive signal, **buyback wins**.

Two reasons:
1. **Mechanical effect** -- buying back shares reduces supply, pushing price up
2. **Signaling effect** (the stronger reason) -- by paying out a lump sum today, you are telling the market: "I am so confident about future cash generation that I can afford to give away this cash now without hurting future dividends"

> **Professor's point:** "You are sending a signal that you are confident about the future. I am offering a large cash outflow because I know I can generate enough cash to service equity going forward."

#### Factor 2: Management Compensation (ESOPs)

If the CEO has **share-based compensation** (ESOPs), they will **always prefer buyback** over dividend. Why?

- **Buyback** reduces share supply, price goes up, ESOP value goes up
- **Dividend** reduces share price (ex-dividend drop), ESOP value goes **down**

But the deeper reason is this: as an ESOP holder, you **cannot receive dividends** until you exercise and actually hold the shares. So every rupee paid as dividend is a **lost opportunity** for the ESOP holder. More dividend = more loss for the manager.

> **Professor's exact words:** "If management compensation is a factor, the CEO will never recommend dividend over buyback. They will rather recommend go for buyback. Clear."

#### Factor 3: Capital Structure Adjustment

This is subtle. Consider:

| Scenario | Impact on D/E Ratio |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Pay dividend** | No change -- cash goes out, retained earnings drop equally on both sides, but debt and equity proportions stay the same |
| **Buy back shares** | D/E ratio **goes up** -- equity shrinks (fewer shares outstanding, capital goes down), debt stays the same |

Think of it like this: you have 300 crore of debt. You use 100 crore of surplus cash to buy back shares. Debt stays at 300. But equity base shrinks. So debt-to-equity rises -- **even though you did not borrow a single rupee**.

Why does this matter? If your company has a **low D/E ratio** and wants to gravitate toward a **target capital structure** with more leverage, buyback is a tool to get there. Dividend does not do this.

**India-specific note:** In India, bought-back shares must be **cancelled** (removed from the balance sheet entirely). There is no "treasury stock" concept like in the US. So the equity reduction is permanent.

#### Factor 4: Prospect Theory and Regret Aversion

This is a behavioral finance explanation. **Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory** says investors want to **avoid regret**.

**The Regret Story -- Selling Winners Too Early:**

You buy a stock at 100. It goes to 200. You sell. 100% return -- you should be happy. But the next day, the price hits 230. Now you regret missing those extra 30 rupees. This is **irrational** -- you are regretting a *notional* gain while forgetting an *actual* 100% return.

**The Portfolio Story -- Holding Losers Too Long:**

You have two stocks. One is up 10%, one is down 20%. What do most people do? They **sell the winner** (to "book profit") and **hold the loser** (hoping it will "turn around").

> **Professor's insight:** "In 90 out of 100 cases, people will sell the winning stock and hold the losing stock. Why? Because if they sell the losing stock, they cannot tell their friend 'I made profit.' They have to say 'I made a loss.' But that would have been the better decision."

**How this connects to buyback:** Prospect theory says investors prefer **dividend over buyback** because buyback means they have to sell shares. And once they sell, if the price goes up further, they will regret. So they say: "Give me dividend instead. I keep my shares. No regret."

**The antidote?** Have a **reference point** (a target price). When it hits that price, sell -- do not look back. Like a stop-loss, but for profit-booking too.

### Why Companies Buy Back Shares (Beyond Signaling)

| Reason | How it works |
|--------|-------------|
| **Signaling** | "Our shares are undervalued" -- confidence message to market |
| **Excess cash distribution** | Jensen's free cash flow theory -- don't let managers overinvest |
| **Takeover defense** | Buy back from dissatisfied shareholders so they don't sell to a hostile bidder |
| **Consolidate control** | Promoter holding goes up proportionately (promoters do not participate in buyback) |
| **Capital market allocation** | Money flows from cash-rich companies to cash-starved ones via the investor |
| **Pre-equity-issuance jugglery** | Buy back first (price goes up), then issue equity (price falls, but stays above original) |

#### The Takeover Defense Story

Imagine a company whose book value is 1,000 crore but market cap is only 800 crore. A potential acquirer thinks: "Why negotiate with the promoters at 1,000 when I can buy from the market at 800?"

The acquirer approaches **dissatisfied shareholders** (who are already unhappy that the stock is undervalued) and offers 900 -- a 12% premium over market. Those shareholders are the **first to desert** the company.

**Buyback as defense:** The company itself offers to buy shares at, say, 900. This does three things simultaneously:
1. Gets rid of dissatisfied shareholders (they sell to the company, not the bidder)
2. Increases promoter holding proportionately (consolidates control)
3. Sends a signal that management believes the stock is worth more

> **Real example from class:** "Aditya group bought 10% stake in L&T from Reliance Industries and threatened a takeover. L&T's cement business eventually became UltraTech Cement because of this."

#### Capital Market Allocation

This is a **macroeconomic** explanation, not a company-level one. The logic:
- Company A has excess cash -- that is why it is buying back
- Investor receives cash from buyback
- Investor reinvests in Company B (maybe an IPO) that **needs** cash

So capital flows from **excess-cash firms** to **cash-starved firms**. This is why the Government of India allowed buybacks in 1999 -- as a capital market allocation mechanism.

### How Companies Execute Buybacks

#### Method 1: Through the Stock Market (Open Market Operations)

- Company informs the exchange: "I will buy back up to X% over the next 12 months"
- Buys whenever price dips low
- **No signaling effect** -- nobody knows exactly when or how much is bought
- **Primary objective:** Minimize cost of repurchase by buying at the bottom

> **Professor:** "If Reliance has done three buybacks through the open market, do you know which date they bought and how much? Nobody knows. Only the exchange knows. So where is the signal?"

#### Method 2: Fixed Price Tender Offer (Off-Market)

- Company advertises in newspapers: "We will buy back X shares at Rs Y per share" (at a premium over market price)
- **Strong signaling effect** -- paying a premium says "we have positive information you do not have"

**The Bajaj Auto Story (India's First Buyback):**

In January 2000, Bajaj Auto became the first Indian company to do a buyback after the 1999 law. Market price was around 375. They offered **400 per share**.

> **Professor:** "By offering a higher price, you are sending a signal -- there is some good news coming. The management has complete information and is saying your shares are undervalued."

**The risk:** What if shareholders expect even more? If Bajaj offers 400 but shareholders want 450, the buyback may fail -- people will not tender their shares.

#### Method 3: Dutch Auction (Reverse Book Building)

To solve the fixed-price problem, companies use **reverse auction:**

1. Announce: "We want to distribute 100 crore through buyback" (total pot, not price or quantity)
2. Shareholders bid: "I will sell X shares at Rs Y"
3. Company sorts bids in **ascending order of price**
4. Keeps accepting lowest-priced bids until the 100 crore pot is exhausted
5. **Cut-off price** becomes the buyback price -- everyone at or below gets paid at this price

**Example:** Suppose the 100 crore pot fills up at 400 per share. Anyone who bid 380, 385, 390, 400 -- all get 400. Anyone who bid 405 -- gets nothing. No one can complain because they got at least what they asked for (or more).

**SEBI restriction:** If you buy back shares, you must wait **at least 3 months** before issuing new equity. This prevents the "buyback-then-issue" jugglery from being done instantly.

## Part 2: Introduction to Financial Risk Management

### The Foundational Principle

> **"Risk management is costly. If you can manage the risk organically -- as part of your business model -- do it. It will be the least costly. Only if you cannot manage it internally should you enter into costly external contracts."**

This is the overriding principle for the entire risk management block. Think of it like health: eating well and exercising (organic risk management) is cheaper and better than going to the hospital (derivatives contracts).

### Three Steps of Risk Management

Think of it as a sequence: **IQM**

| Step | What it means | Key insight |
|------|--------------|------------|
| **Identify** | Find the source of risk | Not easy -- requires deep understanding of your business model |
| **Quantify** | Measure the impact | "What gets measured gets managed" -- if you cannot put a number on it, you cannot manage it |
| **Mitigate** | Manage it yourself or transfer it to someone who can do it better | Like buying insurance for your apartment -- you transfer risk to someone better equipped to handle it |

### Why Does Risk Management Matter? (The Modigliani-Miller Argument)

In a **perfect world** (no market imperfections), M&M says risk management is **superfluous** -- a hedged firm and an unhedged firm would have the **same value**.

But the real world has imperfections, and because of these imperfections, investors are willing to **pay a premium** for firms that manage risk better. Four key imperfections:

#### 1. Financial Distress Risk

If a company can **stabilize its cash flows** (through long-term contracts, PPAs, guaranteed offtake agreements), lenders feel more comfortable, suppliers extend better terms, and the cost of capital drops.

> **Professor:** "Can the individual investor go and talk to the buyer of this company's product and say 'Hey, you should buy at a fixed price'? It will not work. The company can do this. The individual cannot."

The key insight: companies can manage financial distress risk **better than individual investors** by signing long-term supply contracts, fixing prices, and securing predictable cash flows.

#### 2. Imperfect Investment Decisions (Debt Overhang)

When bad news hits, companies often cut **R&D spending** first -- even though R&D drives future value. If the company had managed its risk beforehand (alternative revenue sources, hedging), it would not need to cut productive investments during a downturn.

#### 3. Other Imperfections (from the reading)

The course note mentions additional imperfections including tax benefits of hedging and agency cost reduction -- these make risk management value-additive in the real world.

### The Three Types of Financial Risk

| Risk Type | Definition | How it shows up | How BP manages it |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Market Risk** | Change in market variables (FX, interest rates, commodity prices) adversely affects assets, liabilities, or earnings | Crude oil price fluctuation for a refinery; rupee-dollar rate for an exporter | Uses **derivative contracts** (external transfer) |
| **Credit Risk** | Non-performance by counterparty -- default, delayed payment, partial payment, or credit downgrade | Customer does not pay on time; rating drops mid-contract | Manages **internally** (credit scoring, approval systems, credit limits) |
| **Liquidity Risk** | Not having enough cash when you need it -- to run operations or fund near-term capex | Running out of working capital; missing an investment window | Manages **internally** (undrawn credit lines -- BP had 4 billion USD available) |

**Key takeaway from BP:** Out of three financial risks, BP transfers risk **externally** only for market risk (using derivatives). Credit and liquidity risk are managed **organically** -- internally, without external contracts.

### The BP Case Study: Market Risk

BP's annual report reveals two uses of derivative contracts:

1. **Speculation** (first sentence in the report) -- trading derivatives to "optimize portfolio value" even without an underlying exposure. They take directional bets on crude price movements.
2. **Hedging** (second sentence) -- using commodity and financial derivatives to manage actual exposure to crude oil and gas price fluctuations.

**India note:** In India, **speculation by operating companies is not allowed**. An Indian refiner can hedge its crude oil purchase (the second use) but **cannot speculate** on crude price direction without an underlying exposure (the first use).

### Credit Risk: Non-Performance is Broader Than Default

"Non-performance" encompasses:
- **Default** -- does not pay at all (the extreme case)
- **Delayed payment** -- pays on day 45 instead of the contractual 30 days
- **Partial payment** -- pays only a portion
- **Credit downgrade** -- even if payment is not yet due, a rating drop from AAA to A changes the risk profile

### Operational Risk: The Silent Killer

Unlike financial risk (which causes **losses** you can recover from), operational risk can cause **existential crises** -- the entire company can collapse.

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| **People-related** | Fraud (Satyam), mis-selling (reputational damage), incompetent hires |
| **Process-related** | Cloud outages (Azure/AWS), supply chain disruption |
| **Regulatory** | ITC diversifying away from tobacco because of regulatory ban risk; liquor prohibition |

> **Professor:** "Enron collapsed not because of business risk, not because of financial risk -- purely operational risk. Satyam collapsed in India. Did they have bad software developers? No. Did they not have customers? No. Why did they collapse? Fraud."

## The Hindalco Case: Same Company, Different Risks

**Hindalco** has two business segments -- aluminium and copper. The professor (who wrote a case on this) showed that **you cannot use the same prescription to manage risk for both**.

| Feature | Aluminium Business | Copper Business |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Model** | Vertically integrated (bauxite to finished aluminium) | Conversion (copper concentrate to copper products) |
| **Input price driver** | Multiple (bauxite, alumina, energy) -- no single exchange-linked price | LME copper price |
| **Output price driver** | LME aluminium price | LME copper price |
| **Type of risk** | **Revenue risk** (output price fluctuates; input has no derivative hedge) | **Spread/margin risk** (both input and output move with same LME price) |
| **Hedging approach** | Hedge revenue side only using LME aluminium futures; manage costs **organically** (long-term supply contracts, own bauxite mines) | Hedge the **margin** -- ensure the spread between buying and selling price is maintained |

> **Professor:** "For aluminium, it is better to own your bauxite mines to secure supply, because there is no derivative product to hedge the raw material cost. For copper, even if you do not own mines, it is fine -- both input and output are linked to the same LME price."
