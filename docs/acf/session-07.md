# Session 7: FPL Group Case — Dividend Cut Dilemma

*A 47-year dividend streak, a deregulating industry, and four theories that point in different directions -- welcome to the hardest payout decision in corporate finance*

---

## Setting the Stage: Kate Stark's Dilemma

Imagine you are **Kate Stark**, a buy-side analyst covering US electric utility companies. FPL Group -- Florida's largest electric utility -- is on your desk. Your job: recommend **buy, hold, or sell** to your investors.

The company has been increasing dividends for **47 consecutive years**. Not one cut, not one miss. But now the electricity industry is being deregulated, competition is coming, and there are whispers that FPL might break tradition.

The professor polled the class:
- **Sell:** 1 student (Abhishek)
- **Buy:** 25 students
- **Hold:** ~49 students

The session then systematically applied every payout theory from Session 6 to arrive at a structured answer.

---

## Why Dividend Matters Here: The "Bond Surrogate" Concept

Before diving into the theories, the professor asked a fundamental question: **How critical is dividend for this specific company?**

### The CAPM Calculation

Using data from the case exhibits:

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Risk-free rate (RF) | 7.3% | 30-year US Treasury bond yield (Exhibit 8) |
| Beta | 0.60 | Case fact |
| Market Risk Premium (MRP) | 5.5% | Professor's input (not in the case) |

> **Expected Return = RF + Beta x MRP = 7.3% + 0.6 x 5.5% = 10.6%**

### The Critical Ratio

FPL's dividend yield was approximately **7%+** (dividend per share of 2.47 USD divided by the share price of around 32-34 USD from the case exhibits).

Out of 10.6% total expected return, **roughly 7% comes from dividend** -- that is about **70% of total return**.

Only 30% comes from price appreciation. Compare this to a bond, where almost all return comes from coupon (interest) and very little from price changes.

> **This is why FPL shares are called "bond surrogates."** Most of the investor's return comes from the dividend, just like most of a bondholder's return comes from the coupon. If you cut the coupon on a bond, the price crashes. Same logic applies here.

This is fundamentally different from a company like Reliance, where dividend yield is 0.3-0.7% and almost all return comes from price appreciation. For Reliance, MM irrelevance might hold. For FPL, **dividend is the main event**.

## The Historical Pattern: Smooth Dividends, Volatile Earnings

The professor displayed Exhibit 4B data (1984-1993):

- **Dividend per share:** Continuously increasing, a smooth upward line for 10 years
- **Earnings per share:** Fluctuating, going up and down

This tells you the company has been maintaining a **stable payout policy** (not following earnings). But the payout ratio has crept up dangerously -- reaching **90% in 1993**, and over **100% if extraordinary items are excluded**.

The company is paying out more than its annual profit in some years, drawing on past reserves. This is unsustainable.

### Peer Context from Exhibit 9

When compared to other electric utility companies in the Southeast US:

- Other S and P industry groups: dividend yields range from less than 1% to 4%
- Electric utility companies: yields range from 5% to nearly 10%
- FPL's 7%+ yield places it in the **top tier but not the highest** among utility peers
- Five other utility companies also have 90%+ payout ratios -- so FPL is not alone in this squeeze

## Strength vs Weakness: Analyzing Deregulation Impact

The professor pushed the class to look at case exhibits rather than make general statements. Every claim had to be backed by specific exhibit data.

### Strengths (Why FPL Can Survive Deregulation)

**Strength 1 -- Locational advantage (Exhibit 2):**

FPL serves Florida -- the fourth-largest US state with **substantial population growth**. More people means more houses, more industry, more electricity consumption. This is not a shrinking market.

Looking at the map in Exhibit 2, FPL's service area is shielded from southern competition by the **ocean**. You cannot run transmission lines across the sea. Competition can only come from the **north side**, limiting the threat.

> **Professor's framing:** "They have a locational advantage -- not only are they in a large state, but a growing state. And from the south, there's sea, so transmission lines very difficult to get."

**Strength 2 -- Favorable customer mix (Exhibit 7):**

| Customer type | FPL percentage | Deregulation risk |
|--------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Residential** | **56%** (highest among peers) | Low -- small consumers are sticky, 1 cent difference does not matter |
| **Commercial** | 36% | Medium -- some may switch for savings |
| **Industrial** | 4% | High -- but tiny share, so minimal impact |

When deregulation hits, **bulk consumers leave first** -- even a 1-cent-per-kilowatt-hour difference matters when you consume megawatts. But residential customers consuming 100 units will not switch providers for marginal savings.

With 56% residential (highest among peers) and only 4% industrial, FPL's revenue base is **relatively sticky**.

### Weakness (What Could Hurt FPL)

**Weakness -- High O and M cost (Exhibit 7):**

FPL's operations and maintenance cost per kilowatt-hour was **0.75 cents** -- among the highest in its peer group. Most competitors were at 0.72 or lower (except Carolina Power).

This matters because in a deregulated market, competitors can undercut FPL on price. FPL's current high realization rate (revenue per kWh) is sustainable only because there is no competition. When competition arrives, prices will fall -- but if your costs are already high, your margins get squeezed.

**Symptom of high costs:** FPL purchases 30% of its power from outside -- the **highest** among peers. Sometimes it is cheaper to buy finished electricity from others than to generate it yourself. That tells you internal generation costs are too high.

The case mentions that the CEO was already working on reducing O and M costs, but this remains a vulnerability.

## Applying the Theories: Cut or Don't Cut?

The professor walked through each theory systematically, asking the class to justify their position with **specific case facts** -- not general knowledge.

### Theory 1: Signaling -- DON'T CUT

**Logic:** FPL has 47 years of continuous dividend increases. Breaking this streak sends a devastating signal: "We are not confident about our future."

The case mentions that other utility companies that cut dividends in the past are **still talked about years later** -- the memory lingers in the investor community. The negative signal is permanent.

Given that FPL has locational advantages, a favorable customer mix, and no immediate revenue crisis, there is **no urgent need to signal vulnerability**.

> **Verdict: Signaling theory says DO NOT CUT.**

### Theory 2: Lintner -- CUT (High Transaction Cost)

This is where the deep exhibit analysis paid off. The professor asked: "What has been the cost of maintaining such a high dividend?" The answer lies in **Exhibit 5** -- the 5-year cash flow summary.

**The five-year forest (not year-by-year trees):**

| Item | 5-Year Total |
|------|-------------|
| Cash from operations | ~5.5 billion USD |
| Capital expenditure | ~6 billion USD |
| **Free cash flow** | **Negative** (capex exceeded operating cash) |
| Dividend paid | ~2 billion USD |
| Shares issued (new equity) | ~1.9 billion USD |
| Long-term debt issued | ~4.1 billion USD |
| Long-term debt retired | ~4 billion USD |

Key observations:

1. **All operating cash went to capex** -- nothing left for dividends
2. **Debt was a wash** -- new borrowings roughly equaled repayments (refinancing old debt)
3. **Dividends of 2 billion USD were funded by issuing 1.9 billion USD of new equity**

This means FPL was effectively **issuing shares to pay dividends**. The transaction cost of equity issuance (underwriter fees, management fees) runs 5-7% of the amount raised. On 1.9 billion USD, that is over **100 million USD** wasted on fees alone.

One student (Raghav) identified this pattern early: "They had to issue stock to keep paying dividends." The professor confirmed this was correct.

> **Lintner's verdict:** The transaction cost of maintaining high dividends is enormous. Better to make a one-time correction (reset to 60-65% payout like the 1980s level) and then grow gradually. **CUT.**

### Theory 3: Tax Clientele -- NO CLEAR SIGNAL

The professor walked through Exhibit 10 (shareholder composition):

| Shareholder type | Percentage | Tax status | Dividend preference |
|-----------------|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Individuals and others | 51.9% | Mixed (some high-tax workers, some low-tax retirees) | Unclear |
| Pension funds and universities | 18.4% | Tax-neutral (pass-through) | Indifferent |
| Mutual funds | 13% | Tax-neutral | Indifferent |
| ESOP | 11% | Tax-neutral | Indifferent |
| **Tax-neutral block** | **42.4%** | -- | -- |

The 42.4% tax-neutral block (pension funds + mutual funds + ESOP) is indifferent -- they do not care whether return comes as dividend or capital appreciation, because they pay no tax either way.

The 51.9% individuals are a mixed bag. Some are retirees who depend on dividend income. Some are working professionals who (like the professor himself) do not even track their dividend receipts and prefer capital appreciation.

> **Verdict: No clear signal. Cannot definitively say cut or don't cut based on clientele alone.**

### Theory 4: Agency (Jensen) -- CUT

This is where the case gets juicy. The professor pointed students to the **proxy statement** mentioned in the case text (page 6), which revealed:

**New compensation plan proposed for management:**
- Total bonus target increased from 100% to **160%** of base
- Cash component increased from 30% to **40%** (stock component dropped from 70% to 60%)
- Bonus is now linked to **net income goals** (profit after tax)

**The agency conflict calculation:**

If FPL cuts dividends, they save approximately **150 million USD per year** in cash. Even if they simply park this in treasury bills at the risk-free rate of 7.3%:

> **Extra profit = 150 million x 7% = ~10 million USD per year**

This extra profit flows straight to the bottom line. With bonuses now linked to net income and weighted more toward cash, management **personally benefits** from cutting dividends.

Moreover, Exhibit 10 showed that insiders hold a **tiny fraction** of shares. The executives are **employees, not owners**. They will psychologically behave as employees -- optimizing for their own bonus, not shareholder value.

> **Professor's analysis:** "Their behavior psychologically will be more as acting as an employee rather than acting as an owner. If cutting dividend leads to higher bonus for them, they will cut -- but they will tell the market it is a one-time correction."

> **Verdict: Agency theory says managers will recommend CUT -- but their motive may be self-serving, not shareholder-aligned.**

### The Scorecard

| Theory | Verdict | Reasoning |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| **Signaling** | Don't cut | 47-year streak; no immediate crisis; cutting sends wrong signal |
| **Lintner** | Cut | Transaction cost is massive; issuing equity to pay dividends is unsustainable |
| **Tax Clientele** | No clear signal | 42% tax-neutral, 52% mixed -- cannot determine preference |
| **Agency** | Cut | New compensation plan incentivizes managers to cut for personal gain |
| **Final tally** | 2 cut, 1 don't cut, 1 unclear | -- |

## What Actually Happened: The Monday Announcement

On **Monday, May 9, 1994**, FPL's board announced a three-part strategy:

**1. Cut dividend by 32%** -- from 2.48 USD to 1.68 USD per share. This would save approximately 145 million USD per year and reset the target payout to **60-65%** (going back to 1980s levels, then growing again).

**2. Launch a share buyback** -- repurchase 10 million shares over 3 years. The idea: if the dividend cut drops the price, buying back shares should support it.

**3. Operational changes** -- cost reduction measures (not discussed in detail).

### The Market Reaction

| Timeframe | FPL stock | Utility index | Broader market |
|-----------|----------|--------------|---------------------|
| Immediate | **-13.7%** | -- | -- |
| Over following weeks | **-22.3% total** | -8.8% | -1.5% |

The stock fell 13.7% immediately on the dividend cut news. But then it kept falling -- another 8-9% -- even though the buyback should have provided support. Why?

### The Conflicting Signal Problem

The professor asked: "Was there a mixup in the signal between Strategy 1 and Strategy 2?"

Think about it:

**Strategy 1 (cut dividend):** The stated reason was to "strengthen financial flexibility" in the face of deregulation uncertainty. The CEO feared that a high payout ratio would limit the company's ability to respond to competitive threats.

**Strategy 2 (buyback):** But then they immediately spent that saved cash on buying back shares.

> **Professor's devastating analysis:** "If your stated objective is financial flexibility, then retain the cash. Why are you going for a buyback? Whatever 150 million you saved, you have paid. There is no financial flexibility left."

Even worse -- the dividend cut caused a 14% drop in share price. By launching the buyback **after** the price fell, FPL was buying shares cheap. Investors felt **doubly cheated:**

1. **First cheating:** You cut my dividend (I get less cash)
2. **Second cheating:** You buy my shares at a depressed price (I get less capital appreciation if I sell, or you benefit from buying cheap if I hold)

> **Professor's summary:** "Investors are saying: you are paying me low dividend AND low price appreciation because you are buying at a lower price. You have lost our confidence."

The extra fall from 14% to 22% was not about the dividend cut itself -- it was about the **confusing and contradictory signal** of claiming you need flexibility while immediately spending the cash.

### How the 32% Cut Was Derived

A student asked: "How do you derive that specific 32% number?"

The professor explained: you do not start with "32%." You start with the **target level** -- management looked at the 1980s when payout was stable at 60-65%. They decided to reset to that level. The 32% cut is simply the mathematical consequence of going from the current 2.48 USD to a level consistent with 60-65% payout.

> **"You don't decide 32. You decide the level you want to go, and 32 is a consequence."**

## The Deeper Lesson: Action Beats Words

The FPL case teaches a lesson that extends far beyond dividend policy:

**Markets respond to actions, not explanations.** FPL could have held an analyst call explaining the growth story, the deregulation strategy, the cost reduction plan. But the market looked at what they **did**:

- Cut dividend (action says: future is uncertain)
- Bought back shares at depressed prices (action says: taking advantage of weak shareholders)
- Increased management bonuses linked to income (action says: managers benefit from the cut)

These actions told a story that conflicted with the official narrative of "strengthening shareholder value."

> **Professor's final word:** "Please be careful what signal you are sending. You might be willing to send a strong signal, but your action may be confusing. Market looks at what you do, not what you say in the analyst meet."

## Class Discussion Highlights

**On the credibility of 47-year streaks:** Students noted that the case explicitly mentions other utilities that cut dividends and were "still talked about years later." The institutional memory of dividend cuts is long. Breaking a 47-year streak is not just a financial event -- it is a reputational one.

**On "bond surrogate" dynamics:** The professor connected this back to MM irrelevance -- for a company where 70% of return comes from dividends, MM simply does not apply. Dividend is not irrelevant here; it IS the main product the company sells to shareholders.

**On the proxy statement and agency conflict:** Raghav spotted the connection between the new compensation plan and the incentive to cut dividends. The professor confirmed this was the "nail on the head" -- the exact case fact that agency theory demanded.

**On Lintner's transaction cost argument:** Several students initially looked at year-by-year numbers and got confused. The professor emphasized: "Look at the forest, not the trees." Only the 5-year aggregate revealed the true picture -- that equity issuance precisely matched dividend payments, meaning the company was effectively issuing shares to fund dividends.

**On the case method itself:** The professor emphasized discipline throughout: "The beauty of a case is how I relate all the facts available in defending my answer. Anything you say, you have to tell me where it is mentioned exactly, and relate it with exhibits."

## The Real-World Takeaway

Picture a manager who has given the team a free lunch every Friday for years. It is not in anyone's contract. It is just "how things are done here." Then budgets tighten. The manager quietly knows the lunches have to go -- but everyone on the team has come to read "Friday lunch" as a signal that things are fine. Cancel it, and people do not just lose a sandwich, they start updating their beliefs about whether the company is in trouble. That is exactly FPL's problem, just swapped from dividends to lunch. A payment that has become a promise is no longer just a cost -- it is information. And the moment you touch it, people read meaning into the change whether you intended one or not.

The part of this case that will actually save you someday is not the dividend math -- it is the discovery that FPL announced two moves on the same day that undercut each other. They cut the payout to "preserve flexibility," then immediately spent the saved cash buying back shares, leaving zero flexibility. Nobody in that boardroom set out to send a mixed signal. It happened because two well-intentioned teams (the one managing the payout, the one managing the buyback) each made sense internally, and nobody stress-tested how the two would look side by side to an outside observer. That is a completely ordinary failure mode in any company, and it is why the market punished FPL harder than the dividend cut alone deserved.

There is also a quieter lesson buried in the agency-theory discussion: watch what happens to the compensation plan whenever a hard call is being justified as "for the shareholders" or "for the customer." When FPL's management bonus formula was rewritten to reward the same net income the dividend cut would boost, it did not automatically mean anyone was lying -- but it is exactly the kind of detail a sharp analyst, or a skeptical employee, should notice before accepting the official story at face value.

Where this shows up outside a finance job:

- **Cutting a long-standing perk or benefit** (free snacks, remote-work flexibility, an annual bonus structure) -- assume people will read the cut as a signal about the company's health, not just a cost-saving move, and plan your communication accordingly.
- **Making an unpopular but necessary call** (discontinuing a legacy product, pulling support from a client, reorganizing a team) -- check whether your other simultaneous actions contradict the reason you are giving for the hard call.
- **Announcing a "one-time reset"** (resetting a budget, a headcount target, a pricing tier) -- be ready to explain not just the new number, but the target level that produced it, the way FPL's 32% cut only made sense once you saw the 60-65% payout target behind it.
- **Reviewing any leadership decision that conveniently benefits leadership** (a restructuring, a policy change, a compensation tweak) -- look at whether incentives were quietly realigned before or alongside the decision, before assuming the stated rationale is the whole story.

**The one-liner for the interview room:** People do not believe what you announce, they believe what you do next -- so before you make a hard call, make sure your very next move is not quietly contradicting it.
