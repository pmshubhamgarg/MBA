# Session 1: Economic Value Added (EVA)

*Linking ICF to Advanced Corporate Finance through one integrated metric*

---

## The Big Picture: Three Decisions, One Goal

In Intro to Corporate Finance, you learned about two financial decisions:

1. **Investment Decision** — where to put money (maximize **NPV**)
2. **Financing Decision** — where to get money from (minimize **Cost of Capital / WACC**)

There's a third one you may not have covered:

3. **Distribution Decision** — how to share the profits (dividends, buybacks, bonuses)

These three decisions are **not sequential** — they don't follow a waterfall of "invest first, finance next, distribute last." In the real world, sometimes you grab financing first because the market is soft (money is cheap), even if your investment need is 6 months away. You **optimize across all three simultaneously** to maximize the value of the firm.

> **Key insight from the professor:** "You don't maximize these three decisions individually — you *optimize* them. Maximizing one may be counterproductive to the others."

---

## The Problem with Accounting Profit

### The Pizzeria Story

Imagine you open a pizzeria. You invest **Rs 5 lakh** of your own money. Your expected return (what you could earn elsewhere, say in a mutual fund) is **20% per year**, i.e., **Rs 1 lakh**.

At the end of the year, your P&L shows a **net profit of Rs 81,250**. Accounting says you're profitable. But are you really?

**No.** You could have sat at home, put that Rs 5 lakh in a mutual fund, earned Rs 1 lakh, and avoided all the stress of running a pizza shop. Your *actual* profit (Rs 81,250) is **less than** your *minimum expected* profit (Rs 1,00,000). You've suffered an **economic loss** of Rs 18,750.

This is the core idea: **accounting profit ≠ economic profit**.

### What's Missing in the P&L?

The P&L does deduct **interest cost** (cost of debt). But it completely ignores the **cost of equity** — the return shareholders expect for putting their risk capital into the business.

Equity cost is:
- An **opportunity cost** (not an actual cash payment like interest)
- Generally **higher than debt cost** (because equity holders take more risk)
- **Not deducted anywhere** in the P&L

### The "Two Companies" Thought Experiment

Consider two companies, everything else being equal:
- **Company A:** 100% equity financed
- **Company B:** 50% equity, 50% debt

Who shows higher **accounting profit**?

**Company A** — because it doesn't pay any interest at all. The costliest source of capital (equity) goes completely unpenalized in the P&L.

**Company B** shows lower profit because at least 50% of its capital (debt) has interest deducted.

But **Company B is actually more prudent** — it uses a cheaper mix of capital. The P&L is penalizing the wrong company!

> **Professor's exact words:** "So A should have been penalized more, but accounting P&L is penalizing B. That's the limitation of P&L, and that's what is captured in EVA."

## EVA: The Concept

**Economic Value Added (EVA)** answers: *How much value has the firm created over and above what investors expected?*

> **EVA = Actual Profit (NOPAT) − Capital Charge**

Where:

> **Capital Charge = Invested Capital × WACC (Cost of Capital)**

Think of Capital Charge as the **minimum profit** the firm must earn — the "rent" you pay for using investors' money.

### The Four Elements

| # | Element | Source | What it is |
|---|---------|--------|------------|
| 1 | **NOPAT** (Net Operating Profit After Tax) | P&L | Actual profit from operations |
| 2 | **Invested Capital** | Balance Sheet | Total money invested in the business |
| 3 | **WACC (C\*)** | Calculated | Opportunity cost of all capital providers |
| 4 | **Capital Charge** (= 2 × 3) | Derived | Minimum expected profit |

**EVA = Item 1 − Item 4**

### Alternative Formula (Spread Version)

> **EVA = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital**

The **(ROIC − WACC) spread** must be positive to create value. But don't just maximize the spread percentage — maximize the **dollar/rupee value** of EVA.

> **Professor's life lesson:** "A portfolio of Rs 100 earning 10% return makes Rs 10. A portfolio of Rs 1,000 earning 5% makes Rs 50. In percentage, you beat your friend. In value, he's five times richer. **Maximize value, not percentage.** This is true for EVA. This is true for life."

---

## Profit ≠ Value

- A firm can be **profitable** (positive accounting profit) but still **destroy value** (negative EVA)
- Value is created only when **return &gt; cost of capital**
- If you just meet investor expectations → EVA = 0 → share price stays flat → no new wealth created
- If you **beat** expectations → positive EVA → positive surprise → share price goes up

### The Bank Example

A bank puts all depositor money into government bonds earning 7-8%. Cost of deposits is 4-5%. The P&L shows profit. But the bank isn't doing its actual business (lending). As a shareholder, your "spread" is tiny — you could have bought government bonds yourself directly. **Profit exists, but no value is created.**

---

## The Four Ms of EVA Implementation

Just **measuring** EVA doesn't drive shareholder value. You need all four Ms:

| M | What | Why it matters |
|---|------|---------------|
| **Measurement** | Calculate EVA (NOPAT − Capital Charge) | You need the number first |
| **Management System** | Use EVA as the lens for all business decisions — capex, pricing, acquisitions | Decisions should optimize EVA, not just accounting profit |
| **Motivation** | Link variable pay / bonuses to EVA improvement | People optimize what they're paid for; without incentive linkage, they'll chase their own narrow metrics |
| **Mindset** | Change culture so managers think like owners | The hardest M; takes 12-18 months, sometimes 2 years; don't force it |

> **Important:** EVA bonuses are based on **delta EVA** (improvement), not the level. If you generated EVA of 100 last year and 100 again this year — no bonus. You need to show *improvement*.

---

## Case Study 1: Godrej (Indian FMCG)

**Approach:** Divided the company into **EVA Centers** — segments of the business for which you can create a separate balance sheet (identifiable assets and liabilities).

**Key features:**
- Each EVA center has its own cost of capital (not the company-wide WACC)
- The center head's variable pay is linked to the EVA created by *that unit*
- Uses **Performance Linked Variable Remuneration (PLVR)** — paid in **cash**
- Based on **actual EVA improvement vs. expected EVA improvement**
- **No cap** on bonus (since it's cash-based and paid from the surplus above investor expectations)

**EVA Drivers at each center:**
- Profit margins (revenue growth, cost management)
- Asset efficiency (lower asset base for same revenue → higher ROI)
- Working capital management

**Limitation of Godrej's approach:**
- Bonus is at the *unit level*, not the company level — a center head might optimize for their unit at the expense of the overall company
- Only implements **3 of the 4 Ms** (no mindset change, since it's cash-based)

## Case Study 2: Ball Corporation (US Packaging)

**30-year EVA legacy** — highly capital intensive.

**Approach:** Uses **Performance Share Units (PSUs)** — bonuses paid in **shares**, not cash.

**Key features:**
- 50% based on EVA + EPS, 50% on other targets
- Shares are issued at the **grant date price** (locked in), so if the company does well and share price rises, the employee's bonus is worth more
- **Cap at 200%** — because shares have unlimited upside potential (unlike cash), and issuing too many shares at below-market price would dilute outside shareholders
- **Floor at 0%** (threshold) — must beat last year's EVA just to start earning bonus
- **Interpolation** between threshold, target (100%), and maximum (200%)

### Ball Corporation PSU Calculation Example

| | Year 0 (Base) | Year 3 (Actual) |
|---|---|---|
| NOPAT | 800M | 1,000M |
| Invested Capital | 6,000M | 6,500M |
| WACC | 8% | 8% |
| Capital Charge | 480M | 520M |
| **EVA** | **320M** | **480M** |
| ROIC | 13.3% | 15.4% |

*(All figures in USD)*

- Accounting profit grew **25%** (800 → 1,000)
- EVA grew **50%** (320 → 480) — because ROI improved from 13.3% to 15.4% while WACC stayed at 8%, widening the spread from ~5% to ~7%
- Absolute EVA growth: **160M**

**Bonus calculation:**
- EVA threshold: 60M, Target: 120M, Max: 180M
- Actual growth: 160M → crossed target → payout = 100% + (40/60 × 100%) = **167%**
- EPS threshold: 3.00, Target: 3.50, Max: 4.00
- Actual EPS: 3.70 → payout = 100% + (0.20/0.50 × 100%) = **140%**
- Combined PSU payout: (167% × 50%) + (140% × 50%) = **153.5%**
- Target shares: 40,000 → Actual shares: **61,400**
- If share price rose from 50 to 55 → value delivered = **3.4M** (vs. 2M at grant)

### Why Ball Uses EPS Too

EPS anchors management to the bottom-line number shareholders watch closely. Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio is the most commonly used valuation multiple — and the "E" in P/E is EPS. So you need to grow both economic value *and* EPS. Note: they use **diluted EPS** (after accounting for share dilution from PSUs).

### Why Ball Has a Cap (But Godrej Doesn't)

- **Cash bonus (Godrej):** One-off transaction. The surplus is above investor expectations, so shareholders can't complain → **no cap needed**
- **Share bonus (Ball):** Shares given at a fixed grant-date price. If the share price rises significantly, employees get a massive windfall at the expense of outside shareholders who buy at market price → **cap needed** to limit dilution conflict

### Why Ball's Approach is Superior

Ball implements all **4 Ms**:
- Measurement ✓
- Management system ✓
- Motivation ✓
- **Mindset** ✓ — because employees receive *shares*, they think like **owners**, not employees. "Skin in the game."

## EVA as a System, Not a Metric

### Capital Charge = Rent

Think of capital charge as **internal rent** paid by each business unit to the corporate treasury. The treasury provides capital; the unit pays a "rental" based on cost of capital × invested capital.

### Real-World Example: Cement Division (Professor's Research)

**Before EVA:**
- Days Sales Outstanding (DSO): **45 days** (invoiced terms: 30 days)
- Finance team would draw up a list of overdue payments *after* 30 days expired, then send it to marketing to chase collections

**After EVA (within 1 year):**
- DSO dropped to **35 days** — a 10-day reduction
- With daily cement turnover of Rs 100+ crore, 10 days = **Rs 1,000 crore** of freed-up cash
- The team now drew up the overdue list at **day 20-21** and told marketing: "You have 7-10 days to collect — if you delay, there's a capital charge on me"

**What changed?** Not a new process — the *same* printout, the *same* phone calls. But EVA created a **psychological shift**. The cement head understood that every extra day of blocked receivables = extra capital charge = lower EVA = lower bonus.

### Budgeting Before vs. After EVA

**Before EVA:**
- Cement division asks for Rs 3,000 cr capex
- Finance goes cell-by-cell: "Why Rs 2,500/sq.ft for civil? Cut to 2,000"
- After a full day of tussle, budget gets cut to Rs 2,500 cr
- The division head is *secretly happy* — they padded the budget knowing it would be cut

**After EVA:**
- Finance says: "You want Rs 3,000 cr? Fine. Your cost of capital is 11.5%. That means you need to generate **Rs 330+ cr** in extra profit per year. Show me how."
- No more padding — if you inflate the number, *you* have to earn a higher return on it
- Discussion shifts from *amount of rupees* to **productivity of rupees**

> **Professor's key point:** "The whole entire discussion changed. There was no gaming. The approach moved from 'how much money are you asking for?' to 'what return will this money generate?'"

## Working Hypotheses (Theoretical Foundation)

| # | Hypothesis | What it means |
|---|-----------|--------------|
| **H1** | System beats metric | Don't just compute EVA — make it part of decision-making, incentives, and culture |
| **H2** | Incentive aligns owners | Link pay to EVA → people work toward maximizing EVA |
| **H3** | EVA is contingent | Dollar EVA depends on capital intensity and business maturity; not ideal for asset-light or early-stage companies |
| **H4** | EVA is behavioral | Changes how people think and act — collection efficiency, budgeting discipline, resource allocation |

---

## Limitations: Why EVA Isn't Universal

1. **Complexity in calculation** — NOPAT in EVA requires adjustments to accounting profit (covered in next session's reading)
2. **Size bias** — Larger invested capital = higher dollar EVA even with same spread. Workaround: use **EVA rate** (EVA ÷ Invested Capital) instead of absolute EVA (e.g., Asahi Glass in Japan)
3. **Political challenges** — When EVA is first implemented, bonuses often *drop* because accounting profit was overstating true performance. This creates unrest. Must be managed carefully.
4. **Cost of capital changes** — A regional head may perform well on revenue and customers, but bonus drops because treasury raised money at a higher cost. "I didn't raise the money — why am I penalized?"
5. **Not for early-stage companies** — In the initial growth phase, returns may be below cost of capital. Negative EVA doesn't mean shut down.

### Implementation Lessons

- **Start from the top.** Don't roll out EVA to the entire organization at once. Start with top management, prove it works, then cascade down slowly (e.g., Eli Lilly took 5 years)
- **Don't speak "EVA language" to subordinates.** The sales team gets targets in bags of cement sold. The *unit head* translates that into EVA impact internally.
- **Stage-manage it.** A proper EVA implementation takes 12-18 months minimum. "Don't do a 100-day mission here."

## EVA Integrates All Three Decisions

| Decision | Where it shows up in EVA |
|----------|-------------------------|
| **Investment** | NOPAT (the return on your investments) |
| **Financing** | WACC / Cost of Capital (how cheaply you raised money) |
| **Distribution** | Compensation design (how you share the surplus — cash vs. shares, caps vs. no caps) |

This is why EVA is the **single integrated metric** for Advanced Corporate Finance — it brings together everything from ICF into one number.

---

## Key Formulae

| Formula | Expression |
|---------|-----------|
| **EVA** | NOPAT − (Invested Capital × WACC) |
| **EVA (spread form)** | (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital |
| **Capital Charge** | Invested Capital × WACC |
| **ROIC** | NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital |
| **PSU Payout %** | Weighted average of individual metric payouts (interpolated between threshold, target, max) |
| **Shares Issued** | (Bonus amount ÷ Grant-date share price) × Payout % |

**ICF** (NPV, WACC, Cost of Capital) → **EVA** (integrates all three decisions) → **Four Ms** (Measurement → Management System → Motivation → Mindset) → **Value Creation** (positive EVA → share price ↑ → shareholder wealth ↑)

## The Real-World Takeaway

Here's the thing about EVA that actually matters years from now, long after you've forgotten the formula: **growth is not the same as value, and profit is not the same as value.** Remember the pizzeria owner? Their P&L said "profit," but they'd have been better off doing nothing and parking the money in a mutual fund. That gap between "looks good on paper" and "actually worth it" shows up everywhere, not just in finance.

Think about any manager reviewing a new initiative, a new hire, or a new business line. The instinctive question is "did it grow?" or "did it make money?" EVA teaches you to ask a sharper question instead: "did it make more than what we could have earned by doing something else with that same time, money, or people?" A team that grew revenue 20% but consumed capital, headcount, or management attention that could have generated a better return elsewhere isn't a success story — it's a Company A from the pizzeria example, dressed up in good news.

The other lesson worth keeping is about incentives and psychology, from the cement division story. Nobody changed the process — same phone calls, same spreadsheet. What changed was that people started feeling the cost of delay in their own pocket. That's a management trick you can use even without ever calculating a formal EVA number: make the invisible cost of capital (time, money, opportunity) visible and personal to the person making the decision, and behavior shifts on its own.

Here's how this shows up on the job, regardless of what your title is:

- **Evaluating a project proposal:** Before approving a budget increase, ask "what extra return will this generate?" instead of just negotiating the amount down — shift the conversation from "how much do you need" to "what will you deliver with it."
- **Reviewing team or business unit performance:** Don't just look at revenue or profit growth — ask whether that growth outpaced what the resources tied up (people, capital, time) could have earned elsewhere.
- **Designing bonuses or performance reviews for your team:** Reward improvement over last year's baseline, not just hitting a static target — it keeps people pushing rather than coasting once they've "made the number."
- **Deciding whether to keep funding a slow-growing pet project:** A project can be "profitable" and still be a bad use of scarce resources if a better opportunity is sitting right next to it, starved of attention.

If you remember only one line from this entire session, make it this: **making money isn't the same as making it worth it — always ask what you gave up to get there.**
