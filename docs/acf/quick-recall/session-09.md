# Session 9: Futures Contracts — Mechanics, Margins and Arbitrage Pricing

## The Story

Your company uses a lot of aluminum, and prices swing wildly. You lock in today's price using a futures contract so you're protected if prices spike later. Feels like free insurance — until a week later prices move *against* you and you get a call: pay up, right now, in cash, even though the contract hasn't even matured yet. That's a margin call — nobody warned you that "hedging" comes with real, immediate cash demands, not just protection you collect later.

Months later, aluminum prices genuinely spike hard. Competitors who didn't hedge are in serious trouble. You're not — because you locked in the price early, margin calls and all.

## The Concept

Futures let you lock in a price today, but they're not free — they demand ongoing cash discipline as the market moves, before you ever see the "protection" payoff. And underneath all of it is a simple rule: if there are two different paths to the exact same outcome, they must cost the same — the moment they don't, someone in the market exploits the gap instantly, which is why prices stay disciplined.

## Why This Matters as a Future Manager

Any manager responsible for raw materials, fuel, or currency costs eventually has to decide whether to hedge, and the ones who get burned aren't the ones who hedge — they're the ones who didn't realize hedging requires real cash along the way, not just a clean payoff at the end. Understanding the "two paths, same price" logic also trains you to spot a bad deal instantly — if a vendor is offering you something priced differently than an equivalent alternative, one of those numbers is wrong, and it's worth asking why.

## The One-Liner

*"A hedge isn't free protection — it demands real cash discipline along the way, and certainty always has a price somewhere."*
