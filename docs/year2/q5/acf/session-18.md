# Session 18: Corporate Valuation Bridge and the Interco Takeover Battle

*From enterprise value to your pocket — and a real 1988 boardroom war that shows what happens when nobody questions the consultant's spreadsheet*

---

## The Big Picture: Why This Session Matters

Imagine you have just built a beautiful discounted cash flow model. You have projected ten years of free cash flow, discounted everything back, and arrived at a big, satisfying number — the **enterprise value** of the company. Proud moment. Except your shareholders do not own the "enterprise." They own **shares**. So now you have to walk that big number back down to something a shareholder actually receives per share.

That is the first half of this session — the **bridge** from enterprise value to per-share value.

The second half is where it gets interesting. The professor picked a real case — **Interco** — where two teams (a hostile acquirer and the target's own consultants) built two *different* DCF stories using the *same* facts, and the entire outcome of a multi-billion-dollar takeover battle hinged on one sneaky assumption buried inside a valuation spreadsheet. This is the session that teaches you to stop trusting a valuation just because it has a discounted cash flow label on it.

---

## Part 1: The Bridge — From Enterprise Value to Value Per Share

### Quick Recap: What Is Free Cash Flow?

**Free cash flow (FCF)** is the cash generated from operations **after** meeting growth capital expenditure (capex).

> **Professor:** "By definition, free cash flow is cash from operations of the company after meeting the growth capex."

Capex itself is of two kinds:

| Type of Capex | What It Does | Rough Size |
|---------------|--------------|------------|
| **Maintenance capex** | Keeps current operations running at the same level | Roughly equal to depreciation |
| **Growth capex** | Expands the business — either organically (building new capacity yourself) or inorganically (acquiring another company or a stake) | Whatever is spent beyond maintenance capex |

Both organic and inorganic growth spending are part of growth capex, and both get baked into the free cash flow estimate.

FCF is also called **unlevered cash flow** — because this cash belongs to *both* providers of capital (lenders and shareholders), not just one side. Think of it like a shared pizza: the whole pizza (FCF) exists before anyone decides how many slices go to the lender and how many go to the shareholder. If the company has piled on too much debt, the lender's slice may eat up the whole pizza, leaving nothing for the shareholder.

### The Bridge: Enterprise Value to Shareholder Value

Once you discount every year's FCF and sum the present values, you get **enterprise value (EV)** — the value of the whole business, debt and equity combined. But shareholders need **equity value** (also called shareholder value). Getting there takes three steps.

> **The Bridge Formula:** Shareholder Value = Enterprise Value minus Net Debt plus Non-Operating Assets minus Non-Operating Liabilities

**Step 1 — Deduct Net Debt.** Pay the lender first; whatever is left belongs to the shareholder.

> **Net Debt = Gross Debt minus Cash**

Why net *of* cash? Because the company could always use its cash pile to pay down debt if it wanted to — so cash effectively offsets debt.

> **Professor:** "Please remember, this cash — the present level of cash — is not part of your FCF. There's no double counting. So that cash you have, you deduct it from debt."

A student asked whether this "minus net debt" is what's available to the lender. The professor clarified the real logic:

> **Professor:** "No, net debt is the amount outstanding that you have to pay to the lender... Deducting it because our ultimate aim is to find out equity value, shareholder value, from the total enterprise value."

**Interesting twist:** Net debt can be **negative** — meaning the company holds more cash than debt. In that case, it gets **added** instead of subtracted.

> **Professor:** "An immediate example would be Microsoft. They have cash balance more than the debt. They also have debt because of the data centers, but still they have a lot of cash. So your net debt can be negative."

**Step 2 — Add Non-Operating Assets.** Your FCF captures only your *core business* operations. But a company might also hold investments in government bonds, a stake in a joint venture, another subsidiary's shares, or idle land it could sell. None of that shows up in FCF, so you add its value separately.

**Step 3 — Deduct Non-Operating Liabilities.** Things like pension liabilities or ESOP obligations are also not part of the operating cash flow — so they need to be subtracted separately.

After these three adjustments, you arrive at **shareholder value**. Divide by the number of shares outstanding to get **intrinsic value per share**.

> **Value Per Share = Shareholder Value / Number of Shares**

> **Professor:** "Clear? So three steps. Deduct the debt net of cash. Add any assets whose value you have not yet captured in the cash flow. Similarly, deduct non-operating liability which also you have not considered in your cash flow, to get your shareholder value."

Once you have this intrinsic value, and the company is publicly listed, you can compare it against the market price to judge whether the stock is **undervalued** or **overvalued**.

## Part 2: The Interco Case — Setting the Stage

With the theory bridge in hand, the class moved to a real 1988 case: **Interco**.

> **Pedagogy note:** The professor was firm that students must come having read the case, calling out several students mid-class for trying to recall facts from memory instead of reading from the exhibits: *"Where is the case? Why are you telling it from your memory? You can keep the case in front of you... Ah, read it. So there's no point. You don't need to memorize the numbers."* Later, more bluntly: *"If you don't read... the session goes bad. I'm trying to create interest, but if you're not with me then it's a bad pedagogy. Case method is a bad pedagogy unless students come prepared."*

### The Company's Story

Interco began life as **International Shoe Company**. Over time, it diversified — not by building new businesses from scratch (organic, "greenfield" growth) but almost entirely through **acquisitions** (inorganic, "brownfield" growth). By the late 1980s it had roughly 25–30 subsidiaries spread across four business lines:

1. **Apparel** manufacturing
2. **General retail** merchandising
3. **Furniture** and furnishings
4. **Footwear**

> **Professor:** "It grew through inorganic root. It kept on acquiring companies... They're good at acquisitions, and by doing this they have diversified their product portfolio."

### The Silent Shift in Business Mix

A close read of the segment data (the case's "Table A") reveals something the company clearly already knew about itself:

| Year | Apparel + General Retail (share of revenue) | Footwear + Furniture (share of revenue) |
|------|----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **1984** | ~60% | ~40% |
| **1988** | ~40% | ~60% |

The mix had *flipped*. Interco was quietly de-emphasizing apparel and general retail, and leaning harder into footwear and furniture.

> **Professor:** "That means the company itself knew that maybe the future growth of top line lies in footwear-furniture. So they were anyway lowering their exposure to the other two businesses and increasing exposure to footwear-furniture."

### Digging Into Performance (Not Just Revenue Mix)

The professor pushed the class hard on a core finance habit: **never read absolute numbers, always compute ratios.**

> **Professor:** "Don't, in finance now — don't say by absolute numbers, it carries no meaning."

Walking through the segment operating-margin data (from the case's profitability exhibit), the class computed:

| Segment | ~1986 Operating Margin | ~1988 Operating Margin | Latest Quarter (ending May 1988) | Verdict |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------|---------|
| **Apparel** | ~7.35% | ~2.49% | Turned **negative** (a loss) | Deteriorating sharply |
| **General Retail** | ~7% | ~7.3% | ~8.38% (improving) | Stable, holding its own |
| **Footwear** | ~8.68% | ~10.44% | ~8.6% | Stable, most consistent |
| **Furniture** | ~14% | ~12% | ~12.2% margin, but revenue run-rate falling below last year | Most profitable margin, but shrinking top line |

> **Professor:** "So therefore it is not [that] general retail is doing bad. Apparel is doing bad... Footwear is the most stable business, but furniture top line is decreasing. So that's the current performance."

The **annual run rate** trick came up here too — multiply a single quarter's revenue by four to estimate the year ahead:

> **Annual Run Rate = Latest Quarter Revenue x 4**

For footwear, the run rate roughly matched the prior year's full revenue — implying a stable business. For furniture, the run rate came in *below* the prior year — a warning sign despite the healthy margin.

The professor connected this to governance: the company's **current CEO came from the footwear subsidiary**, which likely explained management's strategic tilt toward footwear and furniture.

### The Strategic Repositioning Program

Management had already announced a three-part plan:

1. Improve both **profit margin** and **topline (revenue) growth**
2. Increase **ROA (Return on Assets)**
3. Improve **return on shareholder equity**

But here is where trouble started. Despite real operational performance in some segments, **the stock price had gone nowhere for two and a half years** — trading in a lackluster range of roughly 35 to 50 (with occasional spikes to 45–50, always sliding back down).

> **Professor:** "The company share price was not showing any growth. It was a lackluster performance, trading within a range... shareholders were not happy, which means they're not happy with the current management. Maybe they're inefficient. They want somebody to unlock the value."

**A stagnant stock price plus decent underlying operations is a classic setup for a hostile takeover.** When the market is not rewarding a company's fundamentals, an outsider senses an opportunity to buy cheap and unlock the "hidden" value themselves.

## Part 3: The Hostile Bid Arrives

### The Offer

On **August 8, 1988**, **City Capital Associates** made a bid for Interco. They opened with 64 dollars per common share, and on the very morning of August 8 raised it to **70 dollars per share**.

### Reading Exhibit 9: Sizing Up the Deal

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Offer price | 70 per share |
| Number of shares | 37.5 million |
| **Implied equity value ("implied market cap")** | Offer price x shares ≈ 2.6 billion |
| Net debt (gross debt minus cash) | 318.5 million |
| **Implied Enterprise Value** | Equity value + net debt ≈ 2.9 billion |

> **Professor:** "This is called implied market cap — implied by the offer price... So if you add these two figures, 2.6 billion plus 0.3 billion, you are getting an enterprise value — this is without DCF, just based on the offer price."

The exhibit also expressed this 2.9 billion price in relative terms:

| Multiple | Value | What It Means |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| EV / Revenue | ~0.9x | Paying less than one year of total sales |
| EV / Cash Profit | ~9x | |
| EV / Operating Profit (EBIT) | ~11x | |
| Equity Value / Net Profit (PAT) | ~18x | |
| Equity Value / Book Value | ~2x | Twice the accounting net worth |

> **Professor:** "I can't judge [from] these — are just expressions. I can't judge whether this is a high price or low price. Right? So let us start from there."

### The Puzzle of the Premium Gap

The offer also came with **premium** figures — how much higher the offer was compared to Interco's own historical share price:

| Comparison Point | City's Premium |
|-------------------|----------------|
| 1 day prior to offer | ~17.9% (about 18%) |
| 1 month (4 weeks) prior | ~59.1% |

The professor asked why there was such a huge gap between an 18% premium (versus yesterday's price) and a 59% premium (versus a month ago) — surely both should tell a similar story?

Several students guessed at rumors and shareholder rights plans before the class converged on the real explanation, pieced together across two students' answers:

> **Student (Jagat):** "On July 15th, the restructuring plan was announced, and that is when the stock jumped... close to 15 or 16%."

> **Student (Manish):** "Maybe there was a news in the market that someone is trying to acquire [Interco]." 

> **Professor:** "That is a rumor. Rumor is just one-day-prior news leakage. It's called leaking of the news. That's why it is better to compare one month prior."

Putting it together: a month before the bid, the stock traded cheaply because the market had not yet priced in the restructuring announcement or any takeover speculation. In the days right before the bid, news of the impending offer likely **leaked**, pushing the price up close to the eventual offer — which shrank the *apparent* one-day premium. Measured against a "clean" price from a month earlier (before restructuring hype and before any leak), the premium was actually a much larger 59%.

> **Professor:** "CD [City] Capital is not fooled to offer such a huge premium if the company doesn't deserve [it]. Which means the stocks were currently underpriced under the current management, and hence it appears they are paying a large premium... Please remember, if you are available cheap in the market, people will make a hostile attempt. They will try to buy you out from the market because you're cheap."

### The Market's Own Verdict

Right after the 70-dollar offer was announced on the morning of August 8, the stock did not settle at 70 — it **closed that same day at around 72.5**, *above* the offer price.

The professor asked what signal this sends. After a few false starts from students:

> **Professor:** "This closing price above the offer — always, it is not only for this case — the standard [rule] is: if the closing price in the market after the announcement of the offer is higher, market expects revision in the bid — revision in the offer."

In other words, the market was betting the 70-dollar number was not final — either City Capital would sweeten its own bid, or a rival bidder would show up.

## Part 4: The Board Says No — Enter the Consultants

The Interco board **rejected** the 70-dollar offer, arguing it undervalued the company. To back this up, they hired the investment bank **Wasserstein Perella & Co.** as an independent valuation advisor. Wasserstein Perella came back with a valuation **range of roughly 68 to 80 (some panels even up to 88) dollars per share** — making the 70-dollar offer look like it sat at the *low* end of fair value.

### Exhibit 10 — Premium Paid Analysis (and Its Flaw)

Exhibit 10 compared City's offer premiums against the *average* premiums paid in other comparable 1988 deals, across four different reference points:

| Premium Measure | City's Offer | Average of Comparable 1988 Deals | Verdict |
|------------------|--------------|-------------------------------------|---------|
| 1-day prior | 17.9% | 56% | **Inferior** |
| 1-month (4-week) prior | 59.1% | 80.9% | **Inferior** |
| 52-week high | 17.2% | 15.8% | Superior (only one) |
| 52-week low | 137.3% | 171% | **Inferior** |

Three out of four measures made City's offer look cheap. But the professor pointed out that picking only the flattering column is a classic trap:

> **Professor:** "This is called cherry picking. You are picking a cherry which confirms your answer. But then you look at the other three [columns] — in all other three cases, City's offer is inferior."

**City's rebuttal:** City Capital rejected Exhibit 10 entirely, on two grounds:

1. The comparable deals shown were **cherry-picked "selected tender offers"** — not a comprehensive, unbiased set of all 1988 deals.
2. These were just **offers**, not confirmed, closed transactions — the buyer could always have walked away.

> **Professor:** "So exhibit 10 [is] therefore rejected by City. City said, 'You can't show exhibit 10 and say that my offer is an inferior offer.'"

### Exhibit 12 — The Discounted Cash Flow "Proof"

With premium analysis dismissed, Wasserstein Perella turned to a DCF valuation (Exhibit 12) — a full sensitivity table showing the resulting per-share value across different discount rates and different terminal-value exit multiples, ranging from about 63 dollars all the way up to 88 dollars.

> **Professor:** "If you believe in exhibit number 12, you will say 70 is an inferior offer. And this is DCF. This is not premium cherry picking... this is cash flow of Interco."

The professor then rebuilt the model live to show the class exactly how those numbers were produced — and, crucially, to reveal a hidden flaw.

**Assumptions used (kept unchanged from the case, deliberately not questioned so the model could be replicated exactly):**

| Assumption | Value |
|------------|-------|
| Sales growth | ~7.1–7.2% per year |
| Operating margin | Rising steadily from ~9.2% (year 1) to ~10.1% (year 10) |
| Capex | Equal to depreciation |
| Increase in working capital | ~11.6% |
| Tax rate | 41% |
| Forecast horizon | 10 years |

**Step 1 — Discount the 10 years of explicit cash flow at 10%:**

> Sum of PV of Years 1–10 Free Cash Flow (at 10% discount rate) = **1,495** (million)

**Step 2 — Compute the terminal value.** Wasserstein Perella's exhibit applied a multiple (14x, 15x, or 16x depending on the column) to the year-10 free cash flow:

> **Terminal Value (Year 10) = Year 10 FCF x Exit Multiple**

Using the 14x column: Terminal Value at year 10 ≈ **4.7 billion**. Discounted back 10 years at the 10% discount rate:

> PV of Terminal Value = **1,828** (million)

**Step 3 — Add and bridge to per share:**

> Enterprise Value = 1,495 + 1,828 = **3,323** (million)
> Shareholder Value = 3,323 − 318.5 (net debt) ≈ 3,004 (million)
> Value Per Share = 3,004 / 37.5 million shares ≈ **80 per share**

This matched the top-left cell of Wasserstein Perella's own sensitivity table almost exactly.

### The Hidden Flaw

The professor then asked the class — assuming none of the growth, margin, or working-capital assumptions are questioned — is there still a problem with this analysis?

Several students groped toward the answer (guessing it was about the size of terminal value relative to the explicit period, or about sensitivity of the multiple). The professor confirmed terminal value dominance is *normal*, not the flaw:

> **Professor:** "In all valuation exercise you will find terminal value explaining more than 50% of the total enterprise value, always."

The real flaw was in **what the 14x multiple silently implies.** A no-growth perpetuity value is simply cash flow divided by the discount rate:

> **Perpetuity Value = Cash Flow / k** (where k is the discount rate)

Which means the multiple used is really **1/k**. If the exhibit used a multiple of 14x, that implies:

> **k = 1 / 14 ≈ 7.1%**

But the explicit 10-year cash flows were being discounted at **10%** — a full ~3 percentage points higher. The professor flagged this as internally inconsistent, and worse than it looks:

> **Professor:** "You are discounting the cash flow of next year at 10%, and you are discounting cash flow of the 11th year at 7%. It is possible that is more uncertain — it should be discounted at a higher rate. But you are discounting the cash flow beyond year 10 at a lower rate. And if you discount at a lower rate, present value will go up."

He even pointed to Exhibit 14, which showed the **1-year Treasury bill rate at the time was 7.8%** — meaning even the "risk-free" government borrowing rate was *higher* than the 7.1% implicitly used to value Interco's terminal cash flows, a company that is obviously riskier than the government.

> **Professor:** "The rate at which government borrows money is itself higher than 7.1%. And here a company is borrowing money."

**Fixing the inconsistency:** If the terminal value is instead discounted at the *same* 10% rate used for the explicit period (i.e., using a consistent multiple of 1/0.10 = 10x rather than 14x):

> PV of Terminal Value (consistent rate) = **135** (million, versus the original 1,828)
> Revised Enterprise Value = 1,495 + 135 = **1,630** (million)
> Revised Value Per Share ≈ **66 per share**

> **Professor:** "So if you are consistent with your discount rate and the terminal value discount rate, you will not get 80, 88. You'll get under 70... City Capital said, you know, it's wrong — your DCF is wrong."

**The lesson:** a lower discount rate baked quietly into the terminal-value multiple can inflate an entire valuation — and it hides easily inside a single innocent-looking cell like "14x."

> **Professor:** "This case shows that you have to know how to read through the lines, go behind the numbers, question the assumptions behind a valuation — not the valuation [itself]."

## Part 5: Relative Valuation — Three Ways to Value by Comparison

DCF is not the only valuation tool. **Relative valuation** values a company by comparing it against a benchmark, rather than projecting its own cash flows. The professor laid out three benchmark types:

| Benchmark Type | What You Compare Against | When to Use |
|-----------------|---------------------------|-------------|
| **Trade multiple** | Multiples (like P/E) at which similar *listed* companies currently trade in the market | When comparable public companies exist |
| **Transaction multiple** (deal multiple) | Multiples paid in actual *past M&A deals* in similar industries | When you want real acquisition premiums, not just trading levels |
| **Past transaction method** | The valuation/multiple from the company's *own* last fundraising round (e.g., a startup's Series A, Series B) | When there are no comparable listed peers *and* no similar M&A deals — typically for young, innovative private companies |

> **Professor:** "Use that multiple to value this deal — that is called transaction multiple, or deal multiple. Actual deal happened, I'm using those multiples."

In the Interco case, only **transaction (deal) multiples** were used (no trade multiples), because the discussion focused on comparable M&A deals in furniture and footwear manufacturing.

### Exhibit 11A — The Apples-to-Oranges Mistake

Exhibit 11A looked at past M&A deals specifically in the **furniture manufacturing** space. One example cited: an acquirer (referred to in class as "La-Z-Boy") paying **63 million** for a target — a price representing **22x** the target's earnings, roughly **2x** its book value, and about **0.8x** its revenue.

Lining these up against City's implied multiples for *all of Interco* (18x earnings, ~2.2x book value, ~0.9x revenue) made City's offer look inferior across most of these comparable furniture deals.

But the professor caught the trap immediately:

> **Professor:** "The comparison is apple with orange, because City's offer is for the entire company — all four business [lines] put together — Interco as a whole. But these are segments. How can you apply a furniture multiple to value the entire — City's entire offer? How can we apply footwear multiple to value Interco, because footwear is only one part of the Interco business?"

When this exhibit was presented to City Capital, they rightly rejected it on the same grounds.

### Exhibit 11B — The Fix: Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP)

To compare apples to apples, Wasserstein Perella built **Exhibit 11B**, using the **SOTP (Sum-of-the-Parts) method**: value each of Interco's four segments *separately* using its own industry's comparable multiples, then add up the segment values to get a total company value.

> **Professor:** "SOTP stands for sum of the parts method. You value each part and then sum them up. Then compare with the deal. Then it is apple and apple."

**Worked example — the apparel segment:**

| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Apparel segment revenue (1988) | 813.2 million |
| Comparable revenue multiple range (from deal comps) | 0.4x to 0.9x |
| Value at low end (0.4x) | 325 million |
| Value at high end (0.9x) | 731 million |
| **Median value assigned to apparel** | **569 million** |

Repeating this exercise for all four segments and summing the results gave a total **sum-of-the-parts value**:

| Basis | SOTP Total Value | City's Offer (Implied EV) | Verdict |
|-------|--------------------|------------------------------|---------|
| Revenue multiples | ~3.66 billion | 2.9 billion | Offer inferior |
| Profit multiples | ~4.76 billion | 2.9 billion | Offer inferior |
| Cash-flow multiples | ~3.4 billion | 2.9 billion | Offer inferior |

> **Professor:** "So exhibit 11B, in all the multiple parameters, shows City Capital's offer inferior."

### City Raises the Bid — With a Catch

Facing mounting evidence, City Capital raised its offer twice more: from 70 to **72**, then to **74** per share. But the 74-dollar offer came with a critical condition:

> **Professor:** "City says, my 74-dollars-per-share cash offer is conditional on board accepting my offer... Otherwise I'll withdraw my offer."

This raised an obvious question in class: shareholders might be thrilled to sell at 74 dollars against a stagnant 45-dollar market price — so why did City need *board* approval rather than just going directly to shareholders?

> **Professor:** "The answer lies in Exhibit 5."

## Part 6: The Poison Pill and the Golden Parachute

### The Poison Pill Mechanics

Exhibit 5 described Interco's **shareholder rights plan** — commonly known as a **poison pill**. In plain terms: if the board declares an incoming bid "hostile" (unwanted), the plan is triggered automatically. Specifically:

> **Professor, reading Exhibit 5:** "The amended rights plan declared that it would receive a dividend of one share purchase right per share of common stock."

In other words: a **1-for-1 stock dividend** — every shareholder instantly gets one free bonus share for every share they already hold, **the moment the board declares a bid hostile**.

Normally, a 1-for-1 bonus issue simply halves the share price (100 shares of value 1 each split proportionally into 200 shares of 0.50 each — the pie is the same size, just cut into more slices). But in a takeover fight, the **acquirer's offer price does not adjust** for this dilution:

> **Professor:** "Unlike in a bonus share issue [in a] normal situation... it does not happen in M&A. So City's offer is 70 dollars per share... if the poison pill is triggered, number of shares goes up to 75 [million], but the price remains — offer price remains the same, 70 dollars."

> **The Poison Pill Cost Doubling:** Original Deal Cost (2.9 billion, at 37.5 million shares) becomes Deal Cost x 2 (5.8 billion, at 75 million shares) — because share count doubles while the offer price per share stays fixed.

That is a fatal blow to any hostile bidder's economics — which is exactly why City Capital needed the board's cooperation to *avoid* triggering the pill in the first place.

> **Professor:** "That's why City is saying, you management, you better accept my offer, because if they don't accept and treat it as hostile — immediately one-is-to-one bonus — and City said, I cannot pay 5.9 billion when the valuation is 2.9."

### Why Would Shareholders Ever Approve This Weapon?

The professor pushed the class on a genuinely tricky governance question: the poison pill is not something management can impose unilaterally — **shareholders themselves must vote to approve it.** So why would shareholders willingly hand the board a weapon that could scare away a good offer?

After several attempts, the professor summarized the intended purpose:

> **Professor:** "This is a protection, you know — handle. The shareholders are telling the management: look, we don't look after the company, we don't know what is the strategic competitive space and so on, who is good, who is bad, we don't know. You have a fiduciary relationship, a duty. So if you find a bid is not good — because the bidder is not good — then please trigger poison pill. Please save [us]. Any value-destroying bidder [who] takes us over and then strips the assets — you better not allow them."

But this protective tool has a dark flip side: **entrenchment**. A board that dislikes a takeover for self-interested reasons (say, because a new owner would replace the CEO) can hide behind the same weapon to block even a genuinely good offer.

> **Professor:** "There is a danger [that the] board can create an agency... an inferior board — even if there is a superior offer... the current management can get entrenched. They cannot be unseated, because anytime they feel a threat, they'll trigger the poison pill."

### The Golden Parachute Solves the Conflict-of-Interest Problem

So how do you make sure the board uses the poison pill to protect *shareholders*, not just *themselves*? Part of the answer is the **golden parachute** — a severance package promised to top executives if they lose their jobs due to a takeover.

> **Professor:** "Poison pill without golden parachute is a bad idea. But if you give golden parachute — which says that, you know, if someone is taking over this company and you are thrown out, you'll get a severance package — a good deal — you can see 16.3 million [total], top executives... almost 1 million each."

By guaranteeing management a soft landing regardless of outcome, the golden parachute removes their personal financial incentive to reflexively block every takeover — meaning if they *still* trigger the poison pill, it is more credibly because the deal is genuinely bad for shareholders, not because executives fear for their jobs.

> **Professor:** "Through the golden parachute you are saying: hey guys, you protect our interest, not your interest. If your job goes, here is 1 million. And this money — who will pay? City has to pay, on top of the 2.9 billion offer. So this is a costly thing for them, so unless they're serious, they will not bid."

### The Final Act

As shareholder unease grew over the board's refusal of a rich 74-dollar offer, Wasserstein Perella issued one more note (Exhibit 13): if the board's own restructuring plan (fully divesting apparel and general retail, and doubling down on furniture and footwear) played out, share value could reach roughly **68 to 80 dollars** on its own — no takeover required. This was meant to reassure jittery shareholders to stay patient.

**What actually happened:** the board rejected the 74-dollar offer, following its advisor's recommendation. City Capital withdrew entirely. With no deal on the table, Interco's share price — which had been trading above 74 — **collapsed within a year to under 1 dollar**, a penny stock. Wasserstein Perella was fired, the CEO was fired, and a new leadership team was brought in. The shareholders — who could have exited at 74 dollars — were left holding shares worth almost nothing.

> **Professor:** "Who lost out? The shareholders. They lost the opportunity of selling or exiting at 74, because at penny stock, who will buy their shares?"

### The Professor's Closing Lesson

> **Professor:** "This is a classic case of the perils of using consultants — unless you know, unless you go through the fine print. Don't let a consultant present a PPT... ask them to open the Excel sheet, and then you will know how they got the DCF valuation. Then only [can] you question why are you using a 14 multiple, and so on and so forth. So unless you have your internal team to critically evaluate the recommendation of the consultant, please don't hire consultants."

He added a cynical but realistic note on why organizations hire consultants anyway:

> **Professor:** "We do hire, because the CEO wants her decision to go through, and she employs a consultant, and tells the board: look, this is an independent view. So [the] consultant is given a brief that I want you to show valuation more than 70, and they will show it. They get a fee."

**The one-line exam takeaway:** question the *assumptions* behind a valuation — not merely the final valuation number itself.

## Preview: What's Coming in Trade Multiples (Alphabet Example)

The professor briefly previewed the next class's example — valuing **Alphabet (Google)** using **trade multiples** (comparable listed companies), which requires understanding Alphabet's unusual **three-class share structure**:

| Share Class | Voting Rights | Listed / Traded? | Held By |
|-------------|----------------|---------------------|---------|
| **Class A** | 1 vote per share | Listed (common shares) | Public shareholders |
| **Class B** | 10 votes per share | **Not listed** — privately held | Founders/promoters |
| **Class C** | No voting rights | Listed | Public shareholders |

Because Class A carries voting rights and Class C does not, Class A typically trades at a slightly higher price — the difference is called the **voting right premium**: the value of being able to influence board decisions.

> **Professor:** "You'll find the class A shares have a price slightly higher than the class C, and this difference is called voting premium — voting right premium — that I can exercise my right to influence the decision of the board."

## Key Formulae

| Formula | Expression |
|---------|-----------|
| **Free Cash Flow (unlevered)** | Cash from operations − growth capex (available to both lenders and shareholders) |
| **Enterprise Value** | Sum of PV(FCF, explicit forecast period) + PV(Terminal Value) |
| **Net Debt** | Gross Debt − Cash |
| **Shareholder (Equity) Value — The Bridge** | Enterprise Value − Net Debt + Non-Operating Assets − Non-Operating Liabilities |
| **Value Per Share** | Shareholder Value / Number of Shares |
| **Terminal Value (no-growth perpetuity)** | Year N Cash Flow / k (discount rate); equivalently, Cash Flow x (1/k) |
| **Implied discount rate from a terminal multiple** | k = 1 / (Exit Multiple) |
| **Implied Equity Value from an offer** | Offer Price per Share x Number of Shares |
| **Implied Enterprise Value from an offer** | Implied Equity Value + Net Debt |
| **Premium Over Historical Price** | (Offer Price − Historical Price) / Historical Price |
| **Annual Run Rate** | Latest Quarter Revenue x 4 |
| **Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) Value** | Sum across segments of (Segment Financial Metric x Comparable Multiple) |
| **Poison Pill Dilution Effect** | New Deal Cost = Offer Price x (Original Shares x Dilution Factor), while price per share stays fixed |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Strip away the finance jargon and Interco is a very ordinary office story wearing a very expensive suit: a company gets a big, exciting offer, hires a smart-sounding advisor who produces a slide with a reassuring range of numbers, everyone nods because the slide "looks rigorous," and nobody opens the actual spreadsheet behind it. The one person in the room who did open the spreadsheet found that the entire justification for rejecting a great offer rested on a single quiet assumption — a terminal-value multiple that implied a discount rate lower than what the government itself pays to borrow money. That one number was doing more work than anyone in the boardroom realized.

The bridge from enterprise value to per-share value is worth remembering too, but mostly as a habit, not a formula: any time someone hands you a big, impressive top-line number — a company's valuation, a project's total return, a department's total savings — ask what has to be paid out of it before it reaches you, and what extra pieces (a stray asset, a hidden liability) haven't been counted yet. Big numbers are only useful once you know whose pocket they actually land in.

And the poison pill and golden parachute together teach something bigger than takeover defense: whenever you hand someone a powerful tool "for everyone's protection," you also have to think about what stops them from using it to protect themselves instead. Good governance design always asks both questions at once — protect us from bad outsiders, but also don't let the insiders hide behind the same shield.

Use this when:
- Someone hands you a valuation, forecast, or business case built almost entirely on a "terminal" or "steady-state" assumption tucked far in the future — ask what discount rate or growth rate is silently baked into that one cell, because that is usually where the whole conclusion is hiding.
- You are asked to sign off on a consultant's recommendation — ask to see the underlying model, not just the summary deck, and question the assumptions rather than the final number.
- You are designing a policy that gives someone (a manager, a committee, yourself) discretionary power "to protect the team" — build in a check that keeps their personal incentives aligned with the people they're supposed to be protecting.
- You are handed a big headline number (a valuation, a budget, a savings estimate) — before celebrating or panicking, ask what gets deducted or added before that number becomes something you can actually use.

One-liner to remember: the scariest number in any valuation is never the one on the summary slide — it's the one quietly implied by the assumption nobody bothered to open the spreadsheet and check.
