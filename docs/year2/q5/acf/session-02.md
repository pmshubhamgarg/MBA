# Session 2: Capital Structure — The Financing Puzzle

*From EVA strategies to the six-decade quest for the perfect debt-equity mix*

---

## Quick Recap: EVA Formulae and the Spread

Before diving into today's topic, the professor revisited the two EVA equations from Session 1:

> **Equation 1:** EVA = NOPAT - Capital Charge = NOPAT - (Invested Capital x C*)

If you divide both sides by Invested Capital:

> **Equation 2:** EVA = (ROIC - C*) x Invested Capital

The **(ROIC - C*) spread** must be positive to create value. If ROIC is 15% and C* is 10%, you are creating value. If C* exceeds ROIC, you are destroying value. That is the entire message of EVA.

### The Time Lag Adjustment

A student asked: "If I invest money today, can I expect returns immediately?" The answer is no. There is always a gestation period. So practitioners often use **lagged invested capital** in the formula:

> **EVA at time t = NOPAT(t) - Invested Capital(t-1) x C***

For mature industries, a **one-year lag** is typical. For industries with longer gestation periods (say, heavy manufacturing), the lag could be **two or three years**. The key rule: **be consistent** once you pick a lag period.

> **Professor's key point:** "Invest today, get return tomorrow — there must be a lag. For mature industry the lag may be 1 year, but for industry with a larger gestation period the lag could be 2 years, 3 years."

## EVA Drivers: From the Shop Floor to the CEO

EVA is not a magic number that appears from nowhere. It has **three major drivers** that everyone in the organization can influence:

| Driver | What it means | Who drives it |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| **Grow Sales** | Better market share, new markets, cross-selling, upselling | Sales and marketing teams |
| **Control Costs** | Operational efficiency, reduce waste | Operations and procurement |
| **Manage Assets** | No unproductive assets, every rupee invested must earn ROIC &gt; C* | Business unit heads, finance |

> **Professor's warning on real estate:** "Buildings don't earn return. They house people. Too much investment in real estate for an operating company is not a good idea." He cited the example of **SR Group** building a 200 crore headquarters in Mahalakshmi in the early 1990s — that was the beginning of the group's decline. In contrast, **Reliance under Dhirubhai Ambani** operated out of a single rented building in Nariman Point for years.

## Four Strategies to Improve EVA: Operate, Build, Harvest, Optimize

Think of these as **four handles** you can pull when EVA is under pressure:

| Strategy | When to use | What you do |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Operate** | ROIC &gt; C* (positive spread) | Improve return on existing capital — same business, better ROI |
| **Build** | ROIC &gt; C* (positive spread, growth opportunity) | Invest more capital in businesses where you have a competitive edge |
| **Harvest** | ROIC &lt; C* (negative spread, persistent) | Divest — sell the business while you still have some market share |
| **Optimize** | ROIC &lt; C* (but close, salvageable) | Reduce C* by changing the capital structure — **today's main topic** |

> **Professor's definition of competitive advantage in finance language:** "Where you earn a return more than cost of capital, you have a competitive edge."

### The IBM Example (Harvest)

IBM was originally a machine and equipment manufacturing company. Then it became a PC and laptop maker. Eventually, it sold the PC business to a Chinese company (Lenovo). **They sold while they still had market share** and got a premium. Had they waited, they would have gotten nothing. Today, IBM is entirely a services company. That is the harvest strategy in action.

## The Coca-Cola Case Study: All Four Strategies in One Company

The first company to implement something like EVA (before the term even existed) was **Coca-Cola, around 1989-90** under CEO **Roberto Goizueta** (the Goizueta Business School in Atlanta is named after him).

### The Problem

One Friday afternoon, Goizueta was reviewing five years of quarterly data (20 quarters). He noticed:

- **Revenue** was growing year on year
- **Profit** was growing year on year
- But **share price** was trading within a range — not reflecting the performance

He called his CFO and said: "Explain this. Monday morning."

### The CFO's Analysis

The CFO broke Coca-Cola's revenue into four business verticals:

| Business | ROI | C* (Company-wide) | Spread |
|----------|-----|-------------------|--------|
| **Beverages** | **26%** | 13% | **+13%** (creating value) |
| **Restaurants** | **9%** | 13% | **-4%** (destroying value) |
| **Bakery Products** | **11%** | 13% | **-2%** (destroying value) |
| **Ice Cream** | Low | 13% | Negative (destroying value) |

The insight: every time Coca-Cola opened a new restaurant, revenue went up, profit went up, but ROI was only 9% because **restaurants are capital guzzlers** — huge investment in construction, kitchen equipment, furniture. With a cost of capital of 13%, every new restaurant was **destroying value**.

### The Four-Strategy Playbook

**Step 1 — Harvest:** Goizueta convinced the board. He sold three underperforming businesses (restaurants, bakery, ice cream). He got cash.

**Step 2 — Optimize:** With the cash, he prepaid all debt. Coca-Cola became a **zero-debt company**. Debt cost dropped to zero. C* went down. Then he did **share buybacks** — reduced the equity base, which reduced share supply, pushing the share price up. This is the optimize strategy.

**Step 3 — Build:** He pumped the remaining cash into the beverage business — expanding globally, increasing footprint worldwide, raising entry barriers for competitors.

**Step 4 — Operate:** Goizueta noticed that syrups were shipped in **metal containers** (like oxygen cylinders) with a **15% annual maintenance cost**. He proposed replacing them with plastic. His team objected — "The chemical compound might react with plastic!" But Goizueta said: "None of you are PhDs in chemistry." He commissioned a university in Atlanta (under NDA) to test it. The lab confirmed it would work with two conditions:

1. The plastic must be a specific grade
2. Maximum transit time must not exceed **45 days**

His logistics team said 45 days from Atlanta to every corner of the globe was impossible. So Goizueta set up **distributed production hubs** — one in Asia, one in Europe, one in Africa, one in Australia. From local hubs, maximum transit was under 30 days. Result: **50% reduction in packaging costs**. Same revenue, lower investment, higher ROI. That is operate.

> **Professor's takeaway:** "This is not true only for Coca-Cola. This is a conceptual framework which anyone can use to improve their performance — whether you believe in EVA or not."

## Class Discussion: When NOT to Apply EVA

### Infrastructure and Regulated Industries

A student asked about applying EVA to airports and transmission lines. The professor was direct:

> **Professor's answer:** "Don't apply. In those projects you need government support — tax concessions, clearances, tariff revision. Pricing is mentioned in the contract. Anywhere in a regulated industry where there is a price mechanism and if there's a deficit you are paid through annuities — don't apply EVA."

EVA works in a **competitive framework** where you earn your return, not where returns are contractually given to you.

### Service Companies and Asset-Light Businesses

For companies that do not need much capital in dollar terms, use **EVA rate** (EVA divided by Invested Capital) instead of absolute EVA. But banking is an exception — banks are service companies with large balance sheets. Under **Basel norms**, the concept of **economic capital** is used, and all provisioning for market risk and credit risk is based on it. Capital base need not mean plant and machinery.

### The Adani-Ambuja Example

A student brought up Adani Group acquiring Ambuja Cements. Three and a half years back, the share price was around 380, capacity was 65 million tons, and the books had about 10,000 crore in cash. Since then, capacity grew from 65 to 110 million tons, but the share price barely moved to 440-450.

> **Professor's response:** "Capacity investing alone will not guarantee growth in shareholder value. It is the productivity of investment — how much you are earning on investment, and whether this earning is greater than your C*."

## What IS Capital Structure?

Capital structure is the **mix of debt and equity** on the right-hand side of a company's balance sheet. But not all items on the liabilities side count as "debt."

### All Debts Are Liabilities, But All Liabilities Are Not Debt

This distinction tripped up multiple students in class. Here is the key:

> **Debt is a fixed contractual obligation. Interest must be paid.**

If you owe your supplier 100 rupees and pay exactly 100 rupees after 30 days — no interest — that is a **liability** (accounts payable), not a debt.

**Three common types of debt:**

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| **Bank Borrowing** | Term loans, working capital loans |
| **Corporate Bonds / Debentures** | Bonds issued directly to the market |
| **Lease Liabilities** | Aircraft leases (airlines), building leases — interest is built into lease payments |

> **Professor's key distinction:** "Lease is a debt whereas a payable to my supplier is not a debt."

---

## Debt vs. Equity: The Core Trade-Off

| Feature | Debt | Equity |
|---------|------|--------|
| **Nature** | Obligation (contractual) | Promise (aspirational) |
| **Payment** | Interest (must pay, or face default) | Dividend (may or may not pay) |
| **Tax Benefit** | Yes — interest is tax-deductible | No — dividends are not tax-deductible |
| **Ownership** | No dilution | Dilution of ownership |
| **Risk** | Creates **financial risk** | No default risk |
| **Default Consequence** | "You will be hauled up" | "You will not be hanged for it" |

> **Professor's memorable line:** "Debt is an obligation. Equity is a promise. There is no default in equity. It's a promise. I may meet the promise. I may not meet the promise."

So debt gives you **tax benefit** and **no dilution**, but it creates **financial risk**. That is the fundamental tension in capital structure.

## Measuring Capital Structure

Four ways to measure leverage:

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| **Debt-to-Equity (D/E)** | Interest-bearing liabilities / Equity | Static snapshot of capital mix |
| **Leverage Multiple** | Debt / EBITDA | Dynamic — can you repay? If this ratio is 2, your debt is just twice your annual surplus |
| **Interest Coverage** | EBIT / Interest expense | How many times can you cover your interest? (3x, 4x, 5x) |
| **Capital Adequacy Ratio** | (For banks only) | Banks are different — debt (deposits) is their raw material, so D/E does not apply |

### The Leverage Multiple Explained

If Debt/EBITDA = 2, it means your debt is twice your current annual operating profit. If a lender gives you a 5-year repayment window, and this ratio is less than 5, the lender is comfortable — you can repay within the term. If the ratio is 7, the lender will refuse because you cannot repay within 5 years.

> **Professor's point on banks:** "For a bank, debt is a raw material. They take money from you and lend it. So you don't apply debt-to-equity ratio there."

---

## Business Risk vs. Financial Risk

This is critical to understanding capital structure decisions:

> **Total Risk (to shareholder) = Business Risk + Financial Risk**

| Risk Type | Measured By | Source |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Total Risk** | Standard Deviation of **ROE** (20 quarters) | Everything combined |
| **Business Risk** | Standard Deviation of **ROA** (20 quarters) | Nature of the industry, technology, competition |
| **Financial Risk** | Total Risk minus Business Risk (residual) | How much you borrow |

**The Rule:**

- If **business risk is HIGH** (R&D-intensive, intangible assets, innovation-driven) --> keep financial risk **LOW** (don't borrow much)
- If **business risk is LOW** (mature industry, stable cash flows, like cement) --> you can afford **higher financial risk** (borrow more)

> **Professor's insight:** "If business risk is high and you also borrow more money on top of it, you will maximize total risk. And if you maximize total risk, your C* will go up — it will destroy value."

## Numerical Example 1: Finding Optimal Capital Structure via WACC

A company is evaluating different debt levels. For each level, the after-tax costs of debt and equity are given. The question: which debt-equity mix minimizes WACC (C*)?

The class worked through the table and found:

- **Optimal Debt-to-Total ratio: 20%** (meaning 20% debt, 80% equity)
- **D/E ratio = 20/80 = 0.25**
- **Minimum WACC = 11.2%**

The method: compute WACC at each debt level as the weighted average of individual costs of debt and equity. Pick the level where WACC is lowest.

> **WACC = (Weight of Debt x Cost of Debt) + (Weight of Equity x Cost of Equity)**

## Numerical Example 2: Finding Optimal Capital Structure via EPS

A company needs **10 million rupees** for a new greenfield project. EBIT is 30% of capital (3 million). Tax rate is 40%. Three financing alternatives:

| | **Alternative A** | **Alternative B** | **Alternative C** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | 100% Equity | 40% Debt, 60% Equity | 50% Debt, 20% Pref Shares (12% dividend), 30% Equity |
| Share price | 20 | 16 | 20 |
| Shares issued | 500,000 | 375,000 | 250,000 (approx) |
| EBIT | 3,000,000 | 3,000,000 | 3,000,000 |
| Interest | 0 | 480,000 | 330,000 |
| Pref Dividend | 0 | 0 | 240,000 |
| Earnings for equity holders | 1,800,000 | 1,512,000 | 1,360,000 |
| **EPS** | **3.60** | **4.03** | **4.89** |

**Option C wins** even though earnings available to equity shareholders are the **lowest** (1.36 million vs. 1.8 million in A). Why? Because the **denominator** (number of shares) is also the smallest. Fewer shares means each share gets a bigger slice.

> **Key insight:** EPS is not just about maximizing profit — it is about maximizing **profit per share**. A smaller equity base can produce higher EPS even with lower total earnings.

### Important Caveat

The professor emphasized: these examples show the **effect** (consequence) of capital structure, not the **cause**. In real life, you decide the structure first and then observe the effect. You cannot do this exercise beforehand because these are post-facto calculations. The real question is: **what causes a particular capital structure?** That is where theory comes in.

## Theory Pillar: Six Decades of Debate

> **The central question:** "What does six decades of research say about how firms **should** and **do** choose their financing mix?"

"Should" is the ideal. "Do" is what actually happens. Sometimes companies deviate from the ideal — and understanding why is the whole point.

### Timeline of Major Capital Structure Theories

| Year | Theory | Key Idea |
|------|--------|----------|
| **1958** | Modigliani-Miller (without tax) | Capital structure is **irrelevant** to firm value |
| **1963** | Modigliani-Miller (with tax) | Value increases by the tax shield on debt |
| **1973/1984** | Trade-Off Theory | Balance tax benefit of debt against distress costs |
| **1984** | Pecking Order Theory | No "optimal" — firms follow a sequence (internal funds first) |
| **1986** | Agency Theory | Debt disciplines managers (reduces agency costs) |
| **2002** | Market Timing Theory | Firms issue equity when stock is overvalued |

## Modigliani-Miller 1958: The Pizza Theory

Both Modigliani and Miller won Nobel Prizes (Miller specifically for this capital structure work). Their 1958 proposition was radical:

> **M&M Proposition I (without tax):** Value of a Levered Firm (VL) = Value of an Unlevered Firm (VU)

Translation: it does not matter whether a company has 10% debt or 90% debt. The value stays the same. Value comes from the **asset side** of the balance sheet — the quality of assets, the business model, the nature of the business. How you fund it is irrelevant.

### The Pizza Story

When Miller won the Nobel Prize, he gave a lecture in Stockholm. Journalists gathered afterward and said: "We have to cover this in the newspaper, but we didn't understand. Explain it in simple terms."

Miller tried and failed. Then he grabbed a pizza:

> **"Take a pizza — that's the value of the firm. If two people claim it, each gets half. If five people claim it, each gets one-fifth. You have more pieces, but not more pizzas."**

The next day's newspaper headline called it **"The Pie Theory"** — more pieces of the pie, but not a bigger pie.

### The Valuation Formula

> **VU = EBIT / KU**

Where KU is the cost of equity for an all-equity (unlevered) firm, calculated using the **CAPM** model from your introductory corporate finance course.

### M&M Proposition II: Cost of Equity Rises with Leverage

Even though M&M say value is independent of capital structure, they acknowledge that the **cost of equity rises linearly with leverage**:

> **KE = KU + (KU - KD) x (D/E)**

As you take on more debt, shareholders demand higher returns to compensate for the additional financial risk. Earlier (at zero debt), they were happy with KU (say 10%). But as debt increases, their expectation rises to 15%, 18%, and beyond. If your ROE cannot keep pace with this rising expectation, you have a problem.

> **The tension:** Proposition I says value does not change. Proposition II says cost of equity keeps rising. At some point, you simply cannot generate enough return to satisfy shareholders.

---

## Modigliani-Miller 1963: The Tax Correction

Critics pointed out: if a company with debt gets a tax benefit (interest is tax-deductible) and an all-equity company does not, how can their values be the same?

Five years later, M&M revised their model with one concession:

> **VL = VU + PVTSD**

Where **PVTSD** = Present Value of Tax Shield on Debt

### How to Calculate PVTSD

Under M&M's simplifying assumptions:

1. The total debt (D) remains **constant** forever (if you repay, you reborrow the same amount)
2. The interest rate (KD) is the **same regardless of how much you borrow** (risk-free rate assumption)

Given these assumptions:

- Annual tax shield = D x KD x T (debt amount x interest rate x tax rate)
- Since D, KD, and T are all constant, the annual tax shield is a **perpetuity**
- Present value of perpetuity = (D x KD x T) / KD
- KD cancels out, leaving:

> **PVTSD = D x T**

So if debt is 100 crore and tax rate is 30%, the present value of all future tax benefits is simply **30 crore**.

### The 100% Debt Problem

Here is where it gets interesting. The class worked through the logic:

- VU is fixed (does not depend on capital structure)
- To maximize VL, you need to maximize PVTSD
- PVTSD = D x T, so it is maximized when D (debt) is maximized
- Therefore: **optimal capital structure = 100% debt**

A student named Senthil got it right: "100% debt and 0% equity."

> **Professor's reaction:** "Both Senthil and Subham are correct. You want to maximize value of the firm, and M&M says it does not matter whether you have 10% debt or 100% debt — interest cost is the same. So maximum tax benefit = maximum loan."

But this is a **corner solution** — 100% debt or 100% equity. Not an optimal interior solution. That is the fundamental limitation of M&M: **it is not an optimization theory**. It tells you to go all the way to one extreme, which is clearly unrealistic.

M&M's own Proposition II partially saves them: as you pile on debt, cost of equity keeps rising, so "somewhere you have to stop." But M&M never defined **where** that somewhere is.

---

## Trade-Off Theory: The Realistic Improvement

The Trade-Off Theory (developed 1973, revised 1984) directly addresses M&M's limitation by adding a **cost of debt** that M&M ignored:

> **VL = VU + PVTSD - PVDC**

Where **PVDC** = Present Value of **Distress Costs** (also called bankruptcy costs)

### The Core Idea

Think of it as a seesaw:

- As you take on more debt, you earn **tax shield** (PVTSD goes up) -- value increases
- But beyond a certain point, **distress costs** kick in (PVDC goes up) -- value starts to decrease
- The **optimal point** is where the gap between PVTSD and PVDC is the widest

So long as PVTSD &gt; PVDC, keep borrowing. The moment distress costs start to catch up with tax benefits, stop. That is the optimal capital structure.

### What Happens as Debt Increases

1. Your **Debt/EBITDA** multiple gets too high (say, 5x, 6x, 7x)
2. Lenders start charging **higher interest rates**
3. Your credit **rating drops** (from AAA to BBB)
4. If you are loss-making, you may **not even get the full tax benefit**
5. The distress cost snowball begins

### Distress Costs Go Way Beyond Interest

This is where the class discussion got rich. The professor pushed students to think beyond just "higher interest expense." Distress costs include:

| Distress Cost | How it works |
|---------------|-------------|
| **Loss of Customer Confidence** | If customers doubt your survival, they stop buying. Example: **Daewoo Motors** had the popular Matiz car in India. The car was good, but the company went into deep trouble. Customers stopped buying because they feared no after-sales service. |
| **Loss of Supplier Credit** | In good times, suppliers give 30-day credit. When your rating drops, they demand **cash on delivery**. You lose the working capital benefit. |
| **Employee Flight** | Key employees jump ship when they sense the company is in trouble. |
| **Higher Borrowing Costs** | The default spread widens — a triple-B company pays much more than a triple-A company. |

> **Professor's clarification:** "Distress cost has wider ramification. It has nothing to do with product quality. Your product may be excellent, but if customers see your debt piling up and profits declining, they worry about your going concern — whether you will remain in existence."

### Trade-Off Theory is Graphically an Inverted U

If you plot firm value (VL) against debt level:

- Initially, value **rises** as you add debt (tax shield dominates)
- At the peak, you have the **optimal** capital structure
- Beyond the peak, value **falls** (distress costs dominate)

If you instead plot WACC against debt level, it is a **U-shape** — WACC falls, hits a minimum (optimal point), then rises.

### Why Trade-Off Theory Challenges M&M

Trade-Off Theory challenges one specific M&M assumption: that you can borrow at the **same interest rate regardless of how much you borrow**. In reality, lenders charge more as your leverage increases. And beyond interest, there are all the distress symptoms listed above.

> **Professor's summary:** "Where M&M is not an optimal theory, Trade-Off Theory is. It gives you an inverted-U shape — value goes up, then comes down. The peak is your optimal capital structure."

## The Big Limitation of Trade-Off Theory

The professor pushed the class hard on this. After several wrong guesses (measurement difficulty, tax policy changes, managing the optimal ratio), he laid it out:

**Consider two companies:**

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Highly profitable | Struggling (still profitable, but under pressure) |
| Rating | AAA | A or BBB |
| Distress cost | Very low (close to zero) | High |

**As per Trade-Off Theory:** Company A should borrow more. Why? It has zero distress cost and maximum tax benefit capacity. Company B should borrow less because its distress cost is already high.

**In real life:** The exact **opposite** happens. Profitable companies generating tons of cash **don't borrow**. Struggling companies borrow more, hoping future expansion will improve things.

> **Professor's conclusion:** "Profitable companies which generate lots of cash, they don't borrow. Whereas companies that are struggling, they borrow. Just opposite to what trade-off theory is saying."

This real-world contradiction is the fundamental limitation of Trade-Off Theory, and it is what gave birth to the next theory.

## The Real-World Takeaway

Imagine two friends who both start small consulting side-gigs. One borrows a small loan from a family member to buy a laptop and some software. The other brings in a partner who puts in cash for a slice of the business. Neither is wrong. The first one keeps full ownership but now has a fixed payment due every month, no matter how slow business gets. The second one has no fixed payment, but now has to share every future rupee of profit with the partner, forever. That, in a nutshell, is the whole capital structure puzzle: debt is a promise you must keep or things get ugly, equity is a promise you make but nobody can force you to keep.

You do not need to be in finance for this to matter. Every time you are deciding between hiring a full-time employee (fixed cost, like debt) versus a freelancer or a revenue-share partner (flexible, like equity), you are making a capital-structure-style call. Every time your project team debates "should we lock in a fixed-price vendor contract or a variable, pay-as-you-go one," you are weighing the same tradeoff: fixed obligations are cheaper when times are good but dangerous when times are bad, while flexible arrangements cost more but let you breathe during a downturn. The professor's line about business risk and financial risk is the real takeaway here: if your business or project is already unpredictable, do not stack a rigid, unforgiving commitment on top of it. Save the fixed commitments for the safe, boring, predictable parts of your work.

The other thing worth carrying forward is the Coca-Cola story, not for the numbers, but for the habit it represents: every few years, someone senior should ask "where is our capital tied up, and is it actually earning more than it costs us?" That question applies to a marketing budget, a headcount plan, or a real estate lease just as much as it applies to a balance sheet. Goizueta's real skill was not spotting the syrup container savings itself, it was building a habit of continuously asking "is this investment pulling its weight, or is it just there because it was always there?"

A few concrete moments where this shows up at work, even outside finance:

- When your team is deciding whether to buy an expensive tool outright versus subscribing/renting it, ask which one matches how confident you are about needing it long-term.
- When a client or vendor wants you to sign a long fixed-term contract versus a flexible month-to-month one, remember: fixed terms are cheaper only if you are sure the future looks like the past.
- When your manager asks "why are we not growing faster if revenue is up," check whether the growth is actually productive, or whether it is quietly eating more capital than it returns, like Coca-Cola's restaurant business.
- When someone proposes a big new office, a big new headcount, or a big new fixed cost of any kind, ask the boring but powerful question: is this asset going to earn its keep, or does it just look impressive?

The one-liner to keep in your back pocket: debt is a bill you must pay, equity is a bet you might have to share.
