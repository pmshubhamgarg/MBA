# Session 3: Capital Structure Theories and Real-World Evidence

## The Story

You have a friend who's genuinely rich — plenty of savings, stable income. Yet whenever he buys something big, he insists on paying cash, never uses a credit card, even though the card would give him rewards and technically "free money" for a month. Meanwhile another friend, always tight on cash, is drowning in credit card debt just to get by.

If you went purely by textbook logic — "debt/credit gives you free leverage, use it" — your rich friend is behaving irrationally. But he's not. He simply doesn't want the obligation, doesn't want anyone tracking his spending, doesn't want to explain himself to a bank. He uses his *own* money first, always, and only ever borrows as a last resort.

## The Concept

This is exactly how real companies behave, and it often contradicts the "optimal" capital structure textbooks calculate. Cash-rich, highly profitable companies (think Infosys, TCS) often borrow far less than theory says they "should" — because using your own money first, then debt, then equity only as an absolute last resort (this pecking order avoids external scrutiny and avoids sending a bad signal to the market). Meanwhile a struggling company might pile on debt not because it's optimal, but because it's out of options.

## Why This Matters as a Future Manager

You'll often walk into a room with a clean, mathematically "optimal" financing recommendation — and watch leadership reject it for reasons that have nothing to do with the math. Maybe they don't want a lender's covenants restricting decisions. Maybe issuing new shares right now would signal to the market "we think our own stock is overpriced," and the stock would drop on the news alone. The manager who only trusts the formula gets blindsided in these meetings. The manager who understands *why* real companies deviate from theory is the one who can actually advise leadership, not just build a spreadsheet.

## The One-Liner

*"When the stated reason and the math don't add up, trust the math — but always go find out what the real reason is."*
