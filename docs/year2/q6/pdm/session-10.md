## What Is Actually Wrong with Samantha Green's Roadmap?

The class diagnosed the case quickly. Samantha Green, a meticulous product manager at Nexent, built a **comprehensive but feature-centric** 2024 roadmap for **Nexent ECM** (an enterprise content management system) across three releases. Her boss, chief product officer **Matthew Short**, rejected it. The students (Chirag, Manish, Prasoon, Vikas) surfaced the real problems:

- It was a **laundry list** of features with no logical connection.
- It did not **tell a story** — no **thematic cohesion** ("part one now, part two later on the same theme").
- It lacked **customer-centricity** and a clear mapping to **jobs-to-be-done**.
- Above all, it lacked **product vision** (Prasoon's point).

> **Professor Banerjee:** "The customer may say many things. The customer does not know what will make it happen. The product manager has to know this. This is where product roadmapping happens."

---

## The Three E's of Product Development

The professor framed the product manager's balancing act around three demands:

| The Three E's | What It Means |
|---------------|---------------|
| **Effectiveness** | Fulfilling what customers are actually saying they need |
| **Efficiency** | Delivering it using development resources efficiently (in phases) |
| **Equity** | Being fair to all stakeholders across development, GTM, and adoption |

> **Professor Banerjee:** "The business angle and the technical angle — the marriage of these two is what the product manager has to do, and this is not an easy task."

### From Business Requirement to Product Requirement

The translation skill is what separates a good PM from an average one. A user might ask for 20 things. Ask them to distribute 100 points across those 20 and they will *not* give 5 each — they will reveal **relative importance**. That prioritisation feeds the PRD, and features can then be scored with a framework like **RICE**.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "Suppose I board the Rajdhani from Mumbai to Delhi. When the train halts at Vadodara I must know it is *not* Delhi — we reached part of the way. So we must know how many releases, how many stops, there are going to be."

That train — Mumbai → Vadodara → Jaipur → Delhi — became the session's governing metaphor: **each release is a scheduled stop that proves you are progressing on time toward the destination.**

## The Product Vision Narrative

Before mapping features, the roadmap needs a vision. The professor asked the class to box one slide:

:::tip[Important]
A **Product Vision Narrative** must articulate three things:
1. The **product's objectives**
2. The **customer problems** it addresses
3. The product's **key differentiators**

We may not do everything — but *what* we do has to be very clear.
:::

For **Nexent ECM** the vision: the most **intuitive and adaptable** enterprise content management system for modern organisations — combining **AI-driven functionality with user-friendly personalization**, acting as an **intelligent partner / co-pilot** that anticipates user needs, simplifies complex content challenges, and supports hybrid ways of working.

He justified vision with a **child-raising analogy**: parents hold a supportive, long-horizon vision (school → board exams → graduation → the choice to work or study further), channelling the child toward their propensities. And with cricket: **Vaibhav Suryavanshi** setting the field alight, and lesser-known **Abhigyan Kundu** (a Navi Mumbai wicketkeeper-batsman) who wakes at 5 a.m. and hits **800 strokes daily** — vision plus dedication, with a conscious trade-off (a player cannot also top academics).

### Vision Must Anticipate the Customer's Own Evolution

The subtle, exam-worthy point: the organisations using your product **also evolve**, so the product must evolve with them.

> **Professor Banerjee:** "It is not that I have done it today and I stop. With the evolution of the organizations where it is used, even I have to evolve."

His live example: today Dropbox-style inbound sales use call agents; tomorrow, **AI agents**. Companies are moving from **automated AI deployment** (agents doing easily-automatable tasks) toward **augmented AI deployment** (a human supervising agents that carry more logical content). An ECM serving an automated-AI firm needs **harder, more static data structures**; one serving an augmented-AI firm needs data structures **visualised differently** so upgrades don't force a rebuild from scratch. That foresight *is* the product vision. He shared his own scar: a team developed something over **14 months**, the user organisation's process then transformed, and only the parts a colleague had designed **with anticipated change in mind** survived.

The **Tata Motors** example sharpened Priyanku's question (shouldn't the PM *lead* the change, not just react?). Tata Motors moved from selling trucks and buses to selling a **mobility solution** — electric buses plus an ecosystem (training drivers, conductors, passengers, mechanics; route planning). A content-software supplier to Tata Motors has to *see that direction coming* — which demands very close, customer-centric contact, because the customer's domain expertise in its own vertical often exceeds the PM's.

## Building the Roadmap: Themes × Releases × JTBD

The mechanics. At a strategic level, someone ratifies **how many releases** (here, three). Then the PM identifies **key themes** that are carried across all releases and mapped to the customer's **jobs-to-be-done**. Crucially, **each theme becomes a development sub-team**, so theme identification also structures the org.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
The **product roadmap** is built by:
1. Identifying **core themes** (aligned to business objectives and customers' evolving needs)
2. Mapping each theme's features to a specific **job-to-be-done** (pains → pain relievers, gains → gain creators)
3. Sequencing them across **releases** along the PLC (at least Introduction and Growth)
4. Ensuring a **logical progression** and a **customer-centric narrative**

The professor's five themes for Nexent ECM and their jobs-to-be-done:

| Theme | Job-to-be-Done |
|-------|----------------|
| **Collaborative Content Dynamics** | Support distributed teams and hybrid work |
| **Intelligent Content Navigation** | Make content easy and intuitive to manage |
| **Security & Compliance Assurance** | Remove user anxiety; give peace of mind |
| **Adaptive User Experience** | Flexibility and personalization |
| **Unstructured Content Mastery** | Tame a "data lake" of unstructured content |
:::

### The Worked Example (Collaborative Content Dynamics)

The professor demonstrated one theme so the class could do the rest:

- **Release 1:** real-time document editing
- **Release 2:** document collaboration, version tracking, conflict resolution
- **Release 3:** collaborative workspace integration

The teaching point: you don't deliver Release 3 in Release 1, but **you architect Release 1 so it can seamlessly grow into Release 3** over time. "Plan your digital resources so they can accommodate it."

## The Breakout Rooms — The Class Builds the Roadmap

Eight rooms each mapped a theme across three releases. The student output was strong enough that the professor said, only half-joking, "I think you guys are experts; I'm the person who has to learn."

| Theme | Release 1 | Release 2 | Release 3 |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Intelligent Content Navigation** (Jagat, Riddhi) | Automated tagging, bulk upload auto-categorization, OCR image-to-text | NLP natural-language queries, custom metadata fields | AI-driven / predictive content recommendations |
| **Security & Compliance** (Raghav, Varun) | Access control (no unauthorized access), secure storage, automated regulatory updates | Workflow / policy-based controls, monitoring & reporting | Auditing, data-breach prevention & version-reset recovery |
| **Adaptive User Experience** (group 3, Sarang) | Personalized dashboard (recent files surfaced) | Custom metadata fields, real-time notifications, NLP queries | AI-driven content recommendation, retention policies, mobile access |
| **Unstructured Content Mastery** (Anish, Vikas) | Bulk ingestion + auto-categorization + OCR + **start** tagging | Advanced indexing, multimedia (image/video) tagging | Agentic AI recommendations, extended media support |

Two coaching notes from the professor landed as principles. On unstructured content he corrected the sequencing: **tagging must *begin* in Release 1** because "auto-categorization would need tagging." And the students' mnemonics for the security theme — **"Protect, Automate, Assure"** (Anoj) and **"Establish trust, Automate compliance, Advance governance"** (Gorav) — captured the release logic perfectly.

The overall narrative logic he drew out: **Release 1 = the individual job, Release 2 = collaboration, Release 3 = integration/unification** — a strategic build-up of capabilities that makes the roadmap a story, not a list.

## Silo vs. Integrate — and the Golden Thumb Rule

Priyanku noticed releases across themes overlapped. The professor gave the two approaches:

> **Professor Banerjee:** "When there is overlap there are two approaches. One is to **silo** it — no overlap. The second is to **integrate** it. It's a good idea to have some integration planned."

When Manish asked for a thumb rule on which to choose, the professor deflected ("call me as a consultant... whatever I decide may not be right all the time; I've been hit many times") — but gave one non-negotiable rule:

:::tip[Important]
Whenever you integrate (or change a silo into an integration), **document the impact analysis** — "however small it may be" — as a **golden thumb rule** to prevent later heartache. On a team of even 60 people, skipping this is "enough to ensure you sleep two hours a day and no more."
:::

He captured the daily reality with the old FMCG **area sales manager** line: their job is to "manage **organized chaos**" — you plan the day at dawn, and by the time you finish the second task the day is over. Exciting work, but meticulous, painstaking, and humbling.

## Exciting, But Not Easy — On Failure and Resilience

The session had a strikingly human register. Discussing decline and failed ideas, someone had told the professor "failures are exciting," and he pushed back gently: "You have to go through failure to understand the pain at times." His antidote was not bravado but equanimity — assume things will go wrong, treat praise as a bonus, take a recovery period, go on a holiday: "We are human beings."

There was also a warm exchange on exam anxiety. When students pressed repeatedly on procedural details he was not authorised to decide, a student, Chandan, spoke for the class: "By over-explaining, it's a bit embarrassing for us now — we don't want you to be in this situation." The professor's framing of *why* exams exist is worth keeping:

> **Professor Banerjee:** "A course mandatorily has some method of evaluating the understanding of the participant — at a differential level. That is what an examination does. We try to keep it simple by distributing the components."

He acknowledged the real sources of exam stress — time constraint, uncertainty about what is asked, and relative grading — while reminding students that "what you control is your preparation." The recurring product-management lesson bled straight into this: control what you can, brace for what you cannot, and stay resilient.

## Course Recap (Recipe Chain)

**How the ten sessions build on each other:**
You start by learning **what a PM is** and the discipline of separating **problem space from solution space** (redBus). You learn to **position** a product and write a crisp value proposition (Zenatix). You learn to **develop and validate** features with **A/B tests** (LendenClub). You learn to take a validated product **to market** — pricing, place, promotion, and the **LTV/CAC** allocation decision (Dropbox). And finally you learn that launching is not the end: you must drive **adoption and diffusion**, cross the **chasm**, and plan the crossing with a **themed, JTBD-mapped product roadmap** (Nexent). The through-line is the PM as the bridge between what the customer wants and what the product delivers — release by release, stop by stop, Mumbai to Delhi.

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **Product Roadmap** | A themed, sequenced plan mapping features to jobs-to-be-done across releases and the PLC |
| **Product Vision Narrative** | A statement of the product's objectives, the customer problems it solves, and its key differentiators |
| **The Three E's** | Effectiveness (meet customer needs), Efficiency (use dev resources well), Equity (fair to all stakeholders) |
| **Theme** | A cohesive capability area carried across releases; also defines a development sub-team |
| **Silo vs. Integrate** | The two ways to handle overlapping themes across teams |
| **Impact Analysis** | The mandatory documented assessment of what an integration/change affects — the golden thumb rule |
| **Automated vs. Augmented AI** | Agents doing simple tasks vs. humans supervising agents on higher-logic work — a driver of product evolution |
| **CCCA** | Conceptual Clarity → Contextual Applicability, the course's teaching method |
| **Business → Product Requirement** | Translating what customers say into prioritised, buildable features (via RICE, JTBD) |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Farah had a reputation for thoroughness, and she was proud of it. As product manager for a mid-market HR platform, she had spent three weeks assembling the most complete roadmap her company had ever seen — 63 features across three quarterly releases, each one traceable to a customer request, a competitor gap, or a support ticket. She walked into the review certain she would be congratulated. Her VP scrolled for ninety seconds and said, "This is a shopping list. Where's the story?"

Farah was stung. Every feature was justified. But her VP pressed: "Tell me, in one sentence, what a customer's life looks like after Release 1 that it didn't before. Now Release 2. Now Release 3." Farah couldn't. The features were real, but they were scattered — a little onboarding here, a little reporting there, a compliance fix somewhere else. No customer would *feel* a coherent step forward at any release. It was 63 improvements and zero journeys.

So she started over, but differently. She grouped every feature under a handful of **themes** tied to what her users were actually trying to get done: *hire faster, keep people engaged, stay compliant without fear*. Under each theme she asked the train question — if Release 1 is Vadodara and Release 3 is Delhi, what does each stop deliver? "Stay compliant without fear" became: Release 1, lock down who can see sensitive employee data; Release 2, automate the policy checks so managers stop policing manually; Release 3, add audit trails and breach recovery. Protect, automate, assure. Suddenly a customer could feel the product getting safer, release by release.

She also did something she'd skipped before. Where two themes shared a feature, she made a call — silo or integrate — and wrote a short **impact analysis** for every integration, even the trivial ones. Her engineering lead, who had been burned by an undocumented dependency the previous year, read it and said only, "Thank you." Six months later, when a customer's compliance process changed unexpectedly, the roadmap bent instead of breaking, because the vision had anticipated the evolution and the impact of every change was on record.

Her VP never did congratulate her on the 63-feature list. But at the next board meeting he put her three-release narrative on the screen and let it explain itself. Nobody argued with a story that a customer could feel.

**A feature list tells you what you're building. A roadmap tells the customer where they're going — and a great product manager never confuses the two.**
