# Session 11: The Kirana Shop Goes Digital — Attract, Convert, Retain

*Why an online store — a ready-to-cook brand, a quick-commerce ad, even a bank pushing a loan — runs on the exact same sales logic a corner kirana shop has used for decades, and how to read the data to find where it leaks.*

## Quick Recap: Analytics (From the Previous Session)

Before performance marketing, a fast recap of analytics — *measuring, analysing, and optimising* a campaign's performance, powered by real-time digital data.

**Campaign analytics** flows in a chain: marketing objective → campaign goals → **KPIs** (two-to-four metrics that reliably signal progress toward the goal) → KPI targets → **segments for analysis** (break the campaign down so you can see which segment is dragging performance and shift budget accordingly).

**Website analytics** uses the **ABO framework** — the same structure Google Analytics is built on:

| Letter | Meaning | Example metrics (e-commerce) |
|--------|---------|------------------------------|
| **A — Acquisition** | Who visits, from where | Visits by source (organic search, paid search, social, email); new vs. returning |
| **B — Behavior** | What they do on-site | Bounce rate, session duration, click path, event tracking (add-to-cart, wishlist) |
| **O — Outcome** | What the site exists to achieve | Conversion rate, revenue per visitor, average order value |

The whole point: if you are not hitting the **outcome**, segment the data to find whether the problem lies in **acquisition** (wrong traffic) or **behavior** (a site that doesn't nudge the right actions).

:::note[Good to Know]
A student asked how all this gets tracked. Answer: link a tool like Google Analytics and it captures everything automatically — via **cookies** (a cookie ID per visitor, with a set retention window) and the **Google Click ID** passed along each page and action. Incognito or cleared cookies means the visit isn't stored.
:::

## From Sales vs. Marketing to Brand vs. Performance

To define performance marketing, the professor went back to a traditional distinction — **sales vs. marketing.**

Marketing is the broad basket. It *creates value* — it starts by understanding the need, then builds, prices, and communicates the offering. It is the **pull** side: it takes the customer on a journey from need to aspiration. Sales is narrower — the **push** side — the act of finally converting all that into a transaction.

> **Professor:** "Marketing creates demand, sales converts demand. In a way brand marketing creates demand, and performance marketing converts that demand."

So the brand/performance split sits in the same basket:

| | Brand Marketing | Performance Marketing |
|---|-----------------|----------------------|
| **Job** | Create pull / awareness | Convert demand into sales |
| **Horizon** | Builds long-term equity | Seeks near-term leads & sales |
| **Speed of impact** | Slow — returns build over time | High — you spend and expect traction soon |
| **Example** | Tutorials, content marketing | Discount ads, offer ads, "shop now" |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Performance marketing = all paid campaigns aimed at generating *immediate* sales.** Usually time-bound ad campaigns that drive immediate clicks and purchases.

The trap: **not everything paid is performance marketing.** Paying influencers or an SEO agency is paid marketing, but it isn't aimed at an immediate transaction. The test is the **journey stage** an ad targets — anything aimed at the *purchase* stage (click now, shop now) is performance marketing; anything aimed at awareness or consideration is brand marketing.
:::

:::note[Good to Know]
Is brand marketing measurable? Yes — awareness, recognition, and brand associations can all be measured, and in the digital world even its short-term impact shows up (for instance, as **CAC falling** once brand marketing kicks in). The full answer — and *how to split budget between the two* — is the whole of Session 13.
:::

## The Kirana Shop Thought Experiment

Here is the heart of the session. The professor asked a disarmingly simple question: *you own a kirana shop and want to increase sales — what do you do?*

Students rattled off ideas: pamphlets with QR codes, a WhatsApp ordering channel, hoardings and visibility boards, a society kiosk with early access, quick delivery, discounts, offers, loyalty programs.

The professor then sorted **everything** said into three baskets:

| Basket | What the kirana shop does | The job |
|--------|---------------------------|---------|
| **1. Attraction** | Pamphlets, hoardings, paper ads, visibility | **Get people to walk into the shop** |
| **2. Conversion** | Quick delivery, discounts, offers, WhatsApp orders | **Get them to actually buy** |
| **3. Retention** | Loyalty programs | **Get them to keep buying** |

> **Professor:** "Whatever you do to make them come to your shop is attraction. Then you convert, and then you retain. And all of this contributes to increasing your sale."

The insight: "improve sales" does **not** just mean "give discounts." Discounts are *conversion* — but before anyone can convert, **they have to come**. Age-old sales has always done all three in order.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "ACR: Attract, Convert, Retain"]
Every shop — kirana or online — runs on the same three steps. Get them in, get them to buy, get them to come back.
:::

## Performance Marketing Is Just an Online Shop

An e-commerce store *is* an online shop, so it follows the same logic — only now the walk-in traffic is **digital traffic**:

- **Attraction** → pull digital traffic to your website / app / brand page
- **Conversion** → get that traffic to transact
- **Retention** → keep them coming back (measured by CLV)

:::tip[Important]
**In practice, "performance marketing" = attraction + conversion.** Retention is run *separately* — by different teams, with different CRM tools and campaigns. Conceptually retention belongs in the same sales chain, but the industry treats acquisition and retention as distinct. So for this session: performance marketing is **attraction and conversion.**
:::

### The Three Pillars, Up Close

| Pillar | Why it matters | What you watch | Common problems |
|--------|----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Attraction** | Low traffic = low sales, weak SEO signal, and no volume to A/B test and optimise | Traffic mix (paid vs. organic — organic grows over time as brand builds); traffic quality (bounce rate, pages/session, time on site) | **Wrong placement** — wrong platform or ad format for the segment (e.g. running Instagram reels to attract corporates) |
| **Conversion** | If they don't convert, the money spent attracting them is **wasted** | Conversion rate by source; **channel interaction** between sources | Poor traffic quality, weak website (load time, UI), value-prop or pricing mismatch, market too small / too early |
| **Retention** | Plug the leaky bucket — acquiring customers who keep leaving leads nowhere | **CLV (Customer Lifetime Value)** — profit from a customer over their lifetime with you | Covered in Sessions 13–14 |

:::tip[Important]
**Channel interaction** is a subtle trap. Say display ads look expensive and search ads look cheap, so you cut display. Then your *search* conversion mysteriously drops. Why? People were seeing the display ad first, ignoring it once or twice, then clicking the search ad later. The channels weren't independent — don't kill a channel on cost alone without checking its assist effect.
:::

## Watching Attraction and Conversion in a Real Ad

The professor used **Curato** — a ready-to-cook food brand (masalas, ginger-garlic paste, chicken curry packets) founded by IIMU alumni, later featured on Shark Tank and invited to class as guest speakers — to show real campaigns.

:::note[Good to Know]
The transcript renders the brand name inconsistently (heard variously as "Curry / Curate"); it is written here as **Curato** for readability. Treat the *name* as approximate; the *lessons* are exact.
:::

### Example 1 — A Quick-Commerce Category-Page Ad (Blinkit / Zepto)

On a quick-commerce category page (oil-ghee-masala, or chicken-meat-fish), one ad does **both jobs at once**:

- The **sponsored brand banner** — "preservative-free masala" — tells you *why to consider this brand*. That is **attraction**.
- The **pinned product tiles** with 50% / 25% off and a one-tap **Add** button — that is **conversion**.

A crucial clarification came from a student who assumed that a shopper already on Blinkit means "attraction is done":

> **Professor:** "They have come to a mall where there are many shops — yours is also one shop there. Coming to your shop means they have clicked on your particular brand page where only your products are present. That is the equivalent of coming to your shop."

So on a marketplace the brand must still do **both** attraction and conversion.

:::note[Good to Know]
**Who pays for what?** The brand controls its own prices and discounts and *finances the deal itself* — Blinkit won't fund your discount. Separately, the brand **pays the platform** to show the ad: usually **per click**, but **per impression (CPM)** for pure-attraction formats. (A platform only funds your deal when *it* is the new, needy one — not when it's an established Blinkit and you're the new brand.)
:::

### Example 2 — A Quick-Commerce Search Ad

Search the keyword "ginger garlic paste" and Curato appears as a **banner ad** (attraction: "50% ginger, 50% garlic — clean label, no preservatives") plus a **top-of-search product** (conversion: flat 50% off, shop now). This one is **attraction-light, conversion-heavy.**

### Example 3 — Bidding on a Competitor's Keyword

Curato bids on "biryani" and even on a competitor brand's name ("Cooked") so its ad shows against them — again attraction on top, conversion (20% off, add-to-cart) below.

> **Professor:** "You have to attract before converting. If you just say I'm giving you 15% off, 20% off — okay, fine — but then how are you different from others?"

The lesson: in performance marketing you will **rarely** find an ad with no conversion element (that's the whole point). What varies is **how attraction-heavy** it is. And leaning only on discounts, while ignoring your real differentiators (cooks in 15 minutes, preservative-free, clean label), means you haven't understood the sales process.

### How You Measure Each Job

| Attraction metrics | Conversion metrics |
|--------------------|--------------------|
| Impressions, banner/keyword **click-through rate (CTR)**, product-detail-page (PDP) visits | **Add-to-cart rate**, conversion rate by keyword, **cost per acquisition (CPA)**, **ROAS (return on ad spend)** |

:::note[Good to Know]
On these platforms, whoever **pays more gets promoted more**, for as long as they keep paying. There's an auction, but simpler than Google search ads — platforms deliberately keep it less complex so advertisers aren't scared off.
:::

## Reading the Data: Attraction Problem or Conversion Problem?

The real skill is diagnosis. The professor walked through **four Meta ad campaigns** (Campaigns 1, 2, 3, and a **Remarketing** Campaign 4) that drive traffic from Facebook/Instagram to Curato's own website.

First, the **funnel** for one campaign — follow a rupee through it:

| Step | Number |
|------|--------|
| Spend | ₹5,73,000 |
| Link clicks | 31,000 (CTR ≈ 1%) |
| **Landing-page views** | 16,000 — only **52.8%** of clicks actually landed (rest lost to load/tagging issues) |
| Cost per landing-page view | ≈ ₹34 |
| Add to cart | ~4,000 |
| Checkout | ~2,000 |
| Purchase | ~900 |
| Cost per purchase (CPA) | ≈ ₹600 |
| Average order value (AOV) | ₹777 |

**ROAS = AOV ÷ CPA.** Here ₹777 ÷ ₹600 — thin, but positive.

> **Professor:** "If your ROAS is less than one, you spent more in getting that person to your shop than that person spent — you are making a loss."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Diagnosing a campaign — the exam move.** Compare campaigns and locate where the problem starts:

| Campaign | CTR (attraction) | Add-to-cart / conversion | Diagnosis |
|----------|------------------|--------------------------|-----------|
| **1** | Okayish | Okayish | **Balanced** |
| **2** | **Lowest** CTR, cheapest reach, **ROAS lowest (< 1)** | Low | **Attraction problem** — wrong group, not interested in the category. ROAS < 1 → **stop the campaign** |
| **3** | **Best** CTR (most expensive reach) | **Weakest** add-to-cart | **Conversion problem** — interested in category, but something holds them back (price, deal, missing variant/flavour) |
| **4 (Remarketing)** | CTR still lowish (ad fatigue) | **Best** add-to-cart, **best** CPA & ROAS | Already-interested audience — converts best |

**Rule of thumb:** a problem that shows up at **CTR** is an *attraction* problem; a problem that shows up at **add-to-cart / conversion rate** is a *conversion* problem.
:::

Two more reads from the data:
- **Checkout-to-purchase is low across all campaigns** → streamline checkout. People initiate checkout then abandon — maybe too many fields, forced registration, or missing payment options (no UPI, no credit card).
- Remarketing wins on conversion because it targets people who **already showed interest** — but repeated exposure brings **ad fatigue**, dragging CTR down.

## Attraction Is a Performance Problem, Not a Brand Problem

A myth worth killing: "brand marketing will take care of attraction, so performance marketing is only about conversion." Not when you're starting out.

When you're a nobody, **nobody knows you** — so your performance campaigns must do the attracting *and* the converting, which is exactly why those early campaigns are expensive (recall: ₹600 to land one ₹777 order).

> **Professor:** "Attraction is not a brand marketing problem — attraction is a performance marketing problem."

As brand marketing builds over time, the payoff compounds: once people already associate "Curato = preservative-free, easy to cook," you no longer repeat that in every ad. You can switch to **cheaper, conversion-only ad formats** (a do-both format costs more because the platform lets you do two jobs). So good brand marketing makes performance marketing **more efficient** — but it doesn't replace attraction early on.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Nobody knows you, so you pay to be seen"]
Early-stage = expensive performance campaigns, because attraction isn't free yet. Brand marketing is what eventually makes attraction cheap.
:::

## Two Closing Asides (Common Exam Confusions)

**The abandoned-cart nudge** — the "an item in your cart is now discounted" popup/email — is **retention**, run through a **CRM-triggered journey** (added to cart, did nothing for 2–3 days → auto-send a discount). It's conceptually part of the attract-convert-retain chain, but handled by the retention team, not acquisition — "in between both."

**A bank pushing a loan, credit card, or overdraft** is the **same thing** — a bank is also running a shop, trying to attract then convert. The channel just changes: arriving by email = email marketing; on social = a social ad. The logic is unchanged.

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|--------------------|
| **Performance Marketing** | All paid campaigns aimed at generating *immediate* sales; usually time-bound ad campaigns driving clicks and purchases |
| **Brand Marketing** | Marketing that builds awareness and long-term equity (pull) — creates demand rather than converting it |
| **Attraction** | Getting traffic to your digital shop (website / app / brand page) — the first job of performance marketing |
| **Conversion** | Turning that traffic into a transaction — the second job |
| **Retention** | Keeping customers coming back; run separately via CRM; measured by CLV |
| **ABO Framework** | Website-analytics structure — **A**cquisition, **B**ehavior, **O**utcome |
| **CTR (Click-Through Rate)** | Clicks ÷ impressions — a core **attraction** signal |
| **CPM (Cost per Mille)** | Cost per 1,000 impressions |
| **CPC (Cost per Click)** | Spend ÷ clicks |
| **CPA (Cost per Acquisition)** | Spend ÷ purchases — the cost to win one customer |
| **Add-to-Cart Rate** | A proxy for **conversion** rate — where conversion problems show up |
| **ROAS (Return on Ad Spend)** | AOV ÷ CPA; **below 1 = loss-making → stop the campaign** |
| **AOV (Average Order Value)** | Average revenue per order |
| **Remarketing / Retargeting** | Showing ads to people who already interacted with you — converts best, but risks ad fatigue |
| **CLV (Customer Lifetime Value)** | Total profit from a customer over their lifetime with you — the measure of retention |
| **Channel Interaction** | When one channel's traffic assists another's conversion — don't cut a channel on cost alone |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Riya's ready-to-eat brand had a dashboard that looked alive — thousands of impressions, clicks ticking up every hour, four campaigns running across Instagram. And yet, at the end of the month, the bank balance said the opposite of "alive." Money was going out faster than product was going out the door.

Her instinct was the founder's reflex: *add a bigger discount.* But a mentor made her do something slower first — read the funnel one row at a time, like a shopkeeper counting who actually walked in. Campaign 2 had the cheapest reach she'd been proud of, but almost nobody clicked — she was shouting her preservative-free story to people who didn't cook from scratch at all. That wasn't a discount problem; it was an **attraction** problem. She killed it that night, because its ROAS had quietly dropped below one and every rupee was a loss.

Campaign 3 was the opposite and more painful. People *clicked* — they were curious, they came to the shop — and then they stalled at the cart. More discount wouldn't fix that either; they liked the idea but couldn't find their flavour, or balked at the price, or hit a checkout that demanded a signup before they could pay. That was a **conversion** problem, hiding one row lower in the funnel than attraction. And her best performer, the remarketing campaign, was quietly tiring out — the same loyal faces seeing the same ad until they stopped noticing.

The lesson Riya kept was the kirana-shop one. A shop doesn't grow by shouting "50% off" louder. It grows by getting the right people to the door, making it effortless to buy once they're inside, and giving them a reason to return — and by knowing, when sales dip, *exactly which of those three broke.*

**Performance marketing isn't about spending more. It's about reading the funnel honestly — attraction, then conversion — and fixing the step that's actually leaking, not the one that's easiest to blame.**
