## The Project Brief: Insight Is the Currency

Before the case, the professor handed out the group-project guidelines. Each team picks a real startup and builds a mini digital strategy for it:

1. **Introduction + value proposition** of the business.
2. **A few relevant micro-segments** (use judgment — no formal VFF analysis required).
3. **Two detailed personas**, built from **at least five interviews** with prospective customers.
4. **One customer journey map** for one persona, with **friction points** and the **four design levers** applied.
5. **Content marketing strategy** (brand point of view + content pillar canvas).
6. **Search backbone** (intent → keywords → asset → CTA).

The interviews are the heart of it: 20–25 minute **open, free-flowing conversations** (not a structured questionnaire) with people who actually buy in that category. Talk to five, and two or three personas will naturally emerge from the patterns. Use AI to transcribe and find patterns — but start from real, original insight.

> **Professor:** "Insight is the currency in the digital world. The niche customer insight you have — and others don't — is what makes whatever you create feel personalized and customized for that audience."

:::tip[Important]
AI can polish and cross-analyze, but the professor warned that over-polished output loses realism. **Collect original primary data first, then expand it.** Submissions with no original insight lose marks.
:::

## The Case: Sell 5,000 Tickets Before the Clock Runs Out

Now the main event. The case drops us into a familiar startup pressure cooker: a professional **summit (Synapse)** needs to sell **5,000 tickets in ~72 days** with a **paid budget of \$10,000**. Three very different audiences buy tickets:

- **Students** (looking for internships, learning, exposure)
- **Entrepreneurs** (looking for networking, visibility, deals)
- **Corporations** (bulk buyers, highest ticket value)

The professor used the same case-cracking frame from the last session:

| Element | This Case |
|---------|-----------|
| **Protagonist** | The summit's marketing lead |
| **Goal** | Sell 5,000 tickets in ~72 days |
| **Decision** | How to design and split a \$10,000 **paid** social campaign |

Three decisions sit inside that one: **(1) refine the content strategy, (2) allocate the budget, (3) choose the ad placement.** With a hard deadline and a hard target, organic won't deliver the speed or control — so paid carries the load.

---

## Decision 1 — Content Strategy: Intent → Asset → Engagement

A student's instinct was to list everything the summit could say — leverage partnerships, run giveaways, showcase event value — and start posting. The professor pushed for structure instead. Where does content *anchor*?

**Answer: the persona and their journey.** This maps directly onto the search backbone (Intent → Asset → CTA), now upgraded for social media with one new ingredient — **engagement**.

| Layer | What It Means | Synapse Example (Students) |
|-------|---------------|----------------------------|
| **Intent** | The job-to-be-done at each funnel stage (TOFU / MOFU / BOFU) | "Help me land an internship"; "Is this worth \$299?"; "Give me a reason to apply *now*" |
| **Asset** | What you use to answer that intent | Partnerships + event value — **and the other segments themselves** |
| **Engagement** | Social media's special power — make them like, comment, share, tag | Tag-a-classmate ticket giveaway; a live Q&A on "how to pitch yourself" |

### The clever twist: segments are each other's assets

A student (Priyansu) spotted the nicest insight of the session. The three audiences are not just targets — they are **assets for one another**. Students want internships; corporates want interns. So corporates become the "event value" you advertise *to students*, and students become the draw you advertise *to corporates*.

> **Professor:** "If students are looking for internships, that is their job to be done — and corporates looking for interns is the outcome. So for corporates, your students become your assets; for entrepreneurs, your corporates become your assets."

So for each persona, at each funnel stage, you ask: *What is their intent? Which asset answers it? How do I wrap it in something engaging (a freebie, a Q&A, a tag-a-friend offer) so the algorithm spreads it further?*

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The social-media content formula = Intent → Asset → Engagement.** It is the search backbone (intent/asset/CTA) plus engagement. Intent comes from the **persona's journey map** — which is exactly why accurate journey mapping earlier in the course matters so much. Don't create content randomly; align every post to a stage-specific intent.
:::

## Decision 2 — Budget Allocation: More Than One Way to Slice \$10,000

The case offers three ways to split the budget **across segments**:

| Option | Logic |
|--------|-------|
| **Same proportion as ticket targets** | e.g., 15% students / 30% entrepreneurs / 55% corporations |
| **Solely corporates** | Chase the highest-value bulk buyers |
| **Equal split** | One-third each |

But the professor's real point: **segment is only one axis.** The class surfaced several more:

- **By platform** — how much to Instagram vs Facebook vs LinkedIn vs Twitter (match the platform to where each segment lives).
- **By funnel stage** — what % to awareness, consideration, decision.
- **By time-phasing** — awareness spend is **front-loaded** in the first weeks; consideration and decision ramp up as the deadline nears.
- **Weighted average** — blend revenue-per-ticket and volume into a weight per segment (Ashok's idea).
- **By cost efficiency** — CPC / CPM per platform (Manish's idea).
- **By experimentation** — A/B test variants and move money to the winner (Maitri's idea).

These are **not mutually exclusive** — real plans nest them (segments within stages within platforms within weeks).

:::tip[Important]
A/B testing on social is fast. You don't wait days — **you see click-through rates within hours** and reallocate budget in near real time. Budget allocation is a living decision, not a one-time setup.
:::

## Decision 3 — Ad Placement: The Right Format for the Job

"Placement" means picking the ad **format** that fits the objective. A quick working vocabulary:

| Funnel Stage | Objective | Good Formats |
|--------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Awareness** | Capture attention, spark interest | Video ads, carousel ads, story ads, reels |
| **Consideration** | Drive traffic to the website | Lead-gen ads, in-feed ads, **retargeting** |
| **Decision** | Convert leads into buyers | **Retargeting** + promo codes / discounts |

Format cheat-sheet the professor flagged (Instagram examples): **story ads** → engagement & brand recall; **reels** → awareness & discovery; **shopping ads** → e-commerce; **explore ads** → reaching new audiences. He was clear this is an introduction — picking formats well is a specialist skill in itself.

### Retargeting — the superpower of paid

This is the piece that makes paid special. You run awareness ads; a slice of people engage. You **retarget only those engagers** with consideration ads; a smaller slice engages again. You retarget *them* with decision-stage offers. Each round narrows to warmer prospects, so conversions are almost engineered.

> **Professor:** "The secret sauce, the superpower of paid social advertising, is that it enables retargeting. That is what differentiates paid from organic."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Paid social media is the heart of performance marketing.** Its logic is pay-to-play plus funnel retargeting: it guarantees a steady supply of the *right* customers even for a brand nobody has heard of yet. You pay — the conversions keep coming.
:::

## Putting It Together: The Nested Campaign Template

Stack the three decisions and you get a plan, not a guess. Worked example:

- **Awareness stage** gets 40% of budget → \$4,000.
- Split that by segment (15/30/55) → **\$600 students, \$1,200 entrepreneurs, \$2,200 corporations.**
- Within each segment, choose the **platform** (e.g., 100% Instagram, or spread).
- Within the platform, choose the **ad format** (reels, stories, boosted posts).
- The **content and CTA** are already decided from Decision 1.

Then layer **time-phasing** on top: awareness is front-loaded early; consideration and decision budgets grow as the event approaches.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Stage, Segment, Platform, Format — then phase it over time"]
Budget flows **top-down**: pick the funnel stage, split by segment, drop into a platform, choose the format — and phase the whole thing across the weeks you have left.
:::

## Paid vs Organic: Two Engines, Two Fuels

The professor compared the two before running out of time (organic's detailed principles move to the next session):

| Dimension | Paid Social | Organic Social |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Primary objective** | Expand reach, **drive conversions**, retarget, accelerate growth | Drive **engagement** and **credibility** |
| **What drives reach** | **Payment** — pay more, reach more | **Engagement** — more likes/comments/shares, the algorithm shows it to more people |
| **CTAs** | Conversion-focused: "Shop now," "Sign up now," "Download now" | Interaction-focused: like, comment, share, tag |
| **Credibility** | Lower — everyone knows it's sponsored | Higher — virality signals that real people found value |
| **Control** | High — you control spend and targeting | Lower — depends on whether people actually engage |
| **Mindset** | Quant / analytics-driven optimization | Psychology / insight-driven |

> **Professor:** "Paid social media is not for getting engagement. Paid social media is for getting conversions. Organic is what spreads your word of mouth — and because people are interacting, it carries credibility."

The hinge for organic, then, is a single question: **how do you make content engaging enough that people share it?** That answer — the five principles of driving engagement — was deferred to the next session.

## Two Closing Q&As Worth Keeping

### Content snacking (the Netflix "clips" question)

Why does Netflix show short clips to people who already pay? Because it's **retention, not acquisition.** Attention spans have shrunk; people crave a quick dopamine hit every few minutes — the professor called it **content snacking**. Netflix adds clips so you snack *on Netflix* instead of drifting to Instagram or TikTok, and so you discover shows to watch later.

### Customer heterogeneity and the monetization balance

Why do Netflix, Amazon Prime, and YouTube charge *and* show ads? Because a customer base is **heterogeneous** — some binge for hours, most watch little. Heavy users are almost addicted, so they'll pay the **highest** price to avoid ads; light users tolerate ads, so you **monetize them through ads**. Different monetization for different segments.

But copy this blindly and it backfires (a student raised Jio Hotstar's ad backlash).

> **Professor:** "If you just blindly copy Netflix, it won't work. For each case you have to understand what your customer base is comprised of, what each segment values — then calibrate. Otherwise monetization overweights retention, and people leave."

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Snack to retain, segment to monetize"]
Short clips keep attention (retention); tiered ads-vs-subscription milks heterogeneity (monetization). Both only work if you've understood your customer first.
:::

## The Real-World Takeaway

Devika had forty-eight hours before the budget meeting and a number that would not shrink: 5,000 tickets, 72 days, ten thousand dollars. Her first instinct was the one everyone has — pick the prettiest ad, boost it to "everyone 18–45," and pray. Her co-founder had already drafted three such posts. They looked great. They would also have burned half the budget on people who were never going to buy.

She stopped and did something slower. She pulled up the three personas the team had built weeks earlier: the student hunting an internship, the entrepreneur hunting a room full of the right people, and the corporate L&D manager buying ten seats at once. Then she asked a different question for each — not "what do we want to say?" but "what do *they* want, at the exact moment they'd see this ad?" The student wanted a foot in the door. The corporate wanted their juniors to meet talent. And in that gap she saw it: the student *was* the thing the corporate wanted, and the corporate *was* the thing the student wanted. Each audience was the other's advertisement.

So she built a ladder instead of a billboard. Awareness ads went out first and wide — front-loaded, most of them reels, cheap attention. But she didn't chase conversions from strangers. She watched who leaned in — who clicked, who tagged a classmate on the "win two free passes" post — and she retargeted only them with the next rung: a live Q&A, a highlight reel from last year's summit, the 75 speakers and 200 exhibitors laid out like proof. The warm ones got the final rung: a code, a countdown, a reason to buy tonight.

The budget wasn't one decision; it was a nested one. Forty percent to awareness, split 15/30/55 across the three audiences, poured into the platform each one actually lived on, in the format that fit the moment — all of it phased so awareness spent itself early and the conversion dollars waited for warm leads. When a student-targeted variant underperformed by lunch, she moved its money to the corporate carousel by dinner. No ceremony. Just CTR and a steady hand on the dial.

She hit 5,000 with a week to spare. Not because the ads were clever, but because every dollar followed a person with a known intent down a funnel that got narrower and warmer at each step. Organic would have been cheaper and more credible — but organic can't be summoned on a deadline. Paid can.

**When the clock is real and the budget is finite, you don't buy attention — you buy a funnel. Spend wide to find who leans in, then spend narrow on the ones who do.**
