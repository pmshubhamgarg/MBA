# Session 6: Making Friends with the Librarian — SEO, SEM, and the Art of Being Found

*Why every marketing decision you have made so far — persona, journey, content — is pointless if nobody can find you when they search, and how to befriend the "librarian of the internet" so you show up at exactly the right moment*

## Quick Recap: Where Content Marketing Left Us

Before diving into search, the professor rewound to the previous session on **content marketing** — because search sits right on top of it.

The five steps were:

1. **Identify the audience jobs** — what is the audience trying to do at each stage of the journey?
2. **Craft your brand's point of view** — your opinion, your *reason* for doing things the way you do (Zerodha's "teach first, give clean do-it-yourself tools" belief).
3. **Build content pillars** — jobs + point of view + brand assets, so your content stays focused and *authentic*.
4. **Draft the content plan** — stage-wise (top / middle / bottom of funnel), choosing educate vs. persuade vs. convert, and the right format per channel.
5. **Distribute** — spend *more* time on distribution than on creation.

And always end content with a **micro-CTA** — a small next step (save, follow, "check your charges") shown immediately so the nudge lands while the content is fresh.

> **Professor:** "Everything that we will do in this course will be anchored on these two ways of understanding the customer — persona and the journey map. Both of them together form the backbone, even of content marketing."

## The Big Idea: Search Marketing Is About *Being Found*

The professor opened with the simplest possible framing. Strip away the jargon — SEO, SEM, PPC, GEO — and all of it serves one goal.

> **Professor:** "Search marketing is about being found. If you want to be found, then you have to make friends with the search engine."

Here is the logic. People use search engines to find whatever they are looking for. If that is how they find things, that is where you must be present. And "making friends" goes two ways: either you **pay** the search engine to recommend you, or you **design yourself** so the engine thinks you are high-quality enough to recommend for free.

That split gives you the three types of search marketing:

| Type | Full name | In plain English |
|------|-----------|------------------|
| **SEO** | Search Engine Optimization | Earn your way to the *organic* (unpaid) results |
| **SEM** | Search Engine Marketing (also PPC / pay-per-click) | *Pay* to appear at the top as a sponsored result |
| **GEO** | Generative Engine Optimization | The *emerging* game — being found inside AI/generative answers |

This session (Search Marketing 1) covers SEO and SEM. GEO comes next session.

### Who Google Actually Works For

A subtle but exam-worthy point. When you see Zerodha, Groww, and NSDL ranked organically for "open demat account," **none of them paid** for that. Google chose to show them because it believed those links would help the searcher.

> **Professor:** "Ultimately, who is Google's consumer? Google's consumer is the end consumer — the person who is searching. What is in Google's best interest is to help out that person with high-quality, relevant results. Only if they find what they are searching for will they keep coming back."

Remember this: everything the algorithm does is in service of its *own* user. Your job is to be genuinely useful to that user — then the engine rewards you.

## SEO: Earning the Librarian's Trust

The session used a classic explainer analogy: a search engine is a **librarian for every book in the world**. To recommend the right book, the librarian needs to know what is inside every book and how books relate to each other — and every engine has a secret **recipe (algorithm)** for turning that into results.

So **SEO is the process of optimizing your website's content and structure to improve its ranking in the organic results.** The professor boiled the whole thing down to three things the "librarian" checks:

| Pillar | Question the engine asks | How you earn it | SEO type |
|--------|--------------------------|-----------------|----------|
| **Relevance** | Does this page match what the user typed? | Right keywords in the right places | **On-page SEO** |
| **Reputation** | Why you over the other relevant pages? | Backlinks from other (reputed) sites | **Off-page SEO** |
| **Ease** | Can I crawl it, and will it load well for the user? | Fast, mobile-friendly, crawlable site | **Technical SEO** |

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**SEO rests on three pillars: Relevance, Reputation, and Ease.** Relevance is signalled by a *match* between the search words and the words on your page. Reputation is measured by *backlinks* — how many (and how reputed) the sites linking to you are. Ease is how easily the engine can crawl your site and how smoothly it loads for the user. Map these to **on-page, off-page, and technical SEO** respectively.
:::

### On-Page SEO (Relevance)

On-page SEO is about *communicating relevance*. You do that by placing the words people search for where the engine reads them: the **page title, the URL, meta tags, meta descriptions, alt tags, internal links**. If the searched words and the page's words match, the engine infers the page is relevant.

### The Meta Tags Detour

A student asked what meta tags actually are. The class answer: meta tags are **HTML tags in the code** — usually in the header — that describe the page to the search engine but are **not visible to the user**. They give the engine extra context.

Two practical points surfaced:

- **Don't over-generalize.** One student asked: can I stuff a specific product page's meta tags with every broad term so it catches all searches? The professor pushed back — that *confuses* the engine, so your page may not show at the right time.
- **Indexing control.** Meta tags also tell the engine whether a page should be **indexed**. Operational pages — login, signup, "thank you for your order" — have no useful information, so you mark them *no-index* to keep users (and your bounce rate) off dead ends.

### Off-Page SEO (Reputation) and Backlinks

Many sites are relevant — so why you? Reputation breaks the tie, and its proxy is the **backlink**: another website linking to or citing you.

The professor's analogy: in a new city you ask around for a good neurologist. A recommendation from someone you trust carries more weight than one from a stranger — and the more people who recommend the same doctor, the higher your trust. The search engine follows exactly that logic.

Key nuance: **not all backlinks are equal.** A link from a high-authority site is worth far more than one from a low-authority site. Ways to build them: request links early on, write guest blogs, get listed in directories (Yellow Pages, Yelp, your Google profile), and earn social shares. But the *sustainable* path is to create genuinely useful content that others feel compelled to cite — which loops right back to content marketing.

### Technical SEO (Ease)

The quietest pillar: how easily the engine can **crawl and understand your whole site**, and whether the page **loads fast and works on mobile**. The professor flagged this lightly ("I'm not a technical expert") but counted it as one of the three.

:::tip[Important]
Keyword matching is a *starting point*, not a strategy. Simply cramming keywords into your pages — "keyword stuffing" — is superficial.
:::

> **Professor:** "If this approach is being used as a strategy, it is a very superficial approach. Don't take this as the strategy. That does not mean you do this much and SEO is done — no, that is not the way it is."

## SEM: Buying Your Way to the Top — Efficiently

While reputation takes months or years to build organically, you cannot sit idle waiting to be ranked. **SEM (pay-per-click)** lets you appear at the top *today* by paying for ads triggered by specific keywords.

The beauty of PPC is efficiency. A class video made the point sharply: with a newspaper ad you pay whether or not anyone reads it. With PPC, **you pay only when an interested person clicks.** So SEM is almost always a **conversion-focused** play — the goal is to catch people who are *close to taking an action* (buy, subscribe, hand over an email lead) and move them over the line.

### Anatomy of a Search Ad

When you "write an ad copy," you are crafting these pieces: the **headline(s)**, the **description**, the **display URL**, and optional **site extensions** and **snippets** (extra links, a phone number, etc.).

### The Three Steps of a PPC Campaign

1. **Keyword research** — find keywords that signal a user is *ready to act*.
2. **Ad copy and design** — write the headline(s) and description; choose extensions.
3. **Landing page optimization** — the page must match the ad, meet the user's expectation, and carry a clear call to action.

> **Professor:** "If your keywords are not identified correctly, whatever else you do after that goes for a toss."

### The Heart of SEM: Multi-Level Alignment

Keywords are grouped into **ad groups** — a bundle of similar keywords all pointing to the same action. When any keyword in a group is searched, that group's ad fires. But firing is not enough; the *whole chain* must line up.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**SEM lives or dies on multi-level alignment:**

**search words → ad group keywords → headline & description → landing page**

Every link must share the same words and intent. If a user searches a running shoe, your ad group, your ad copy, *and* the page you send them to must all be about that exact shoe — not your homepage. A mismatch at any level means you pay for the click and still lose the customer.
:::

A running-shoe searcher who lands on your homepage has to hunt all over again — so their effort rises and they bounce. The landing page must answer their question (specs, reviews, proof) and end with a clear CTA.

### Match Types: How Much Freedom You Give the Engine

For each keyword you set a **match type** — the degree of freedom the engine has to interpret it. Take the keyword *running shoes*:

| Match type | Freedom | Also triggers on | Trade-off |
|-----------|---------|------------------|-----------|
| **Broad** | Highest | "tennis shoes for men," "socks for running" | Great *discovery* tool for new keywords, but attracts non-converters |
| **Phrase** | Moderate | "men's running shoes," "best shoes for running" | Balanced — catches real variations, drops distant ones |
| **Exact** | Lowest | "running shoes" (or exact-meaning equivalents) | Highest conversion; used once you *know* which words work |

The practical wisdom: keep a **mix**. Broad match early to discover unexpected winning keywords; move proven, high-converting words into exact match to lift efficiency.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Broad discovers, Exact converts"]
Broad match is your scout — it finds search terms you never imagined. Exact match is your sniper — it fires only on words you already know will convert.
:::

### Bidding and the Real-Time Auction

For each keyword you set a **maximum CPC** (cost per click) you are willing to pay. But the highest bidder does not automatically win. The engine runs a live auction on **two** parameters:

**Ad Rank = Max CPC (your bid) × Keyword Quality Score**

Whoever scores highest on that multiplication wins the top spot. So a brand that bids *lower* can still outrank a higher bidder if its quality score is strong.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Ad position in paid search = Max CPC × Keyword Quality Score**, decided by a real-time auction every time someone searches. Quality Score is the engine's safeguard so users still land on something useful — and it is a proxy for *reputation* in the paid world.
:::

What drives **Keyword Quality Score**? Alignment of your ad copy with the keyword, alignment of your landing page, your **account structure and history**, your historical **click-through rate** (clicks ÷ impressions), and page load speed. A well-known, well-aligned advertiser can bid *below* average CPC and still show; a new or poorly aligned one must bid higher just to appear.

:::note[Good to Know]
The **average CPC** for a keyword (visible in Google Keyword Planner) doubles as a signal of how close that keyword is to a purchase decision. "Open demat account" is very close to conversion, so every broker bids hard on it and its average CPC runs high (think ₹200+). The quality score, meanwhile, is transparent to *you* for your own account.
:::

This same auction logic runs everywhere there is a search bar — Amazon, Zepto, Blinkit, YouTube — not just Google. Search advertising on quick-commerce portals is now a very big deal.

## A Worked Example: Zerodha's "Charges" Ad

The professor tied the whole SEM chain together with one clean example. Suppose the targeted keyword is **"zerodha charges"**:

| Element | Content | The alignment |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Keyword** | zerodha charges | What the user typed |
| **Headline 1** | Zerodha Charges & Brokerage Calculator | "charges" repeated |
| **Headline 2** | Live Calculator, No Hidden Fees | Adds reassurance |
| **Description** | Transparent fees for equity, F&O, currency. Check DP, AMC, taxes. Break even with our live calculator. | Promises the answer |
| **Landing page** | zerodha.com/charges | "charges" in the URL, not the homepage |
| **CTA** | Check your charges | The clear next step |

Notice the word "charges" threading through the keyword, headline, description, URL, and CTA — and the landing page goes straight to the charges page with a prominent calculator button. That is multi-level alignment made real: the searcher instantly sees that clicking will get them exactly what they wanted.

## SEO or SEM First? A Student's Practical Question

A student from a technical background asked the natural question: as a business owner, which do I invest in first?

The professor's answer was firm on the first half and nuanced on the second.

> **Professor:** "You should always do SEO. There are no two thoughts about it. If you have a website, it should be found. If you don't want it to be found, then don't have it in the first place."

But SEO **takes time** to kick in — months of building authority and backlinks. A business needs customers from day one, not after two years. So if search is an important acquisition channel, you **advertise (SEM)** to buy immediate visibility while SEO matures underneath.

He also corrected a common myth: SEO is **not purely technical**. Yes, technical SEO needs technical skill — but deciding *what content* should live on your site (the thing that actually lifts rankings) is mostly about **understanding your consumer**. Which is exactly why persona and journey sit at the foundation.

:::tip[Important]
And before you hand the whole campaign to automation, learn the fundamentals yourself. A lot of SEM is automated now, but before you give Google that freedom you need to understand these basics — otherwise you won't know what you are doing or why.
:::

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|---------------------|
| **SEO** | Optimizing a site's content and structure to rank in *organic* (unpaid) search results |
| **SEM / PPC** | Paying for ads that appear on top for specific keywords; you pay only per click |
| **GEO** | Generative Engine Optimization — the emerging practice of being found inside AI-generated answers |
| **On-page SEO** | Signalling *relevance* via matching keywords in titles, URLs, meta tags, alt tags, internal links |
| **Off-page SEO** | Building *reputation* via backlinks from other (ideally high-authority) sites |
| **Technical SEO** | Making the site *easy* to crawl and fast/mobile-friendly to load |
| **Backlink** | A link from another site to yours; a proxy for reputation, weighted by the linking site's authority |
| **Meta tags** | HTML tags describing a page to the engine (invisible to users); also control indexing |
| **Ad group** | A bundle of similar keywords that triggers a particular search ad |
| **Match type** | The freedom the engine has to interpret a keyword — broad, phrase, or exact |
| **Max CPC** | The most you are willing to pay for one click on a keyword |
| **Keyword Quality Score** | Engine's rating of ad/landing-page alignment, account history, CTR and speed |
| **Ad Rank** | Max CPC × Quality Score — decides paid position in a real-time auction |
| **Multi-level alignment** | Search words → ad group → ad copy → landing page all sharing words and intent |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Devansh had built a beautiful website for his running-shoe D2C brand, Stride. Great product photography, a founder story, a clean checkout. Three months in, traffic was a trickle and his co-founder kept asking the same question: "If the site is this good, why can't anyone find it?"

He did what felt obvious first — stuffed every page with "running shoes," "best running shoes," "buy running shoes online." Nothing moved. What he had missed was the professor's warning: keyword stuffing is not a strategy, it is noise, and the librarian sees right through it. Relevance was only one of three doors; he had ignored reputation and ease entirely. No other site linked to Stride, and his product pages took six seconds to load on mobile — where most of his buyers actually shopped.

So he split the problem the way the session taught. For the **long game**, he started earning reputation honestly: a genuinely useful guide on choosing shoes for flat feet that two running blogs linked to, a Google business profile, faster image loading. That was SEO — slow, compounding, but durable. For the **short game**, he ran a tight PPC campaign. One ad group for "cushioned running shoes for beginners," an exact-match set around the few terms he knew converted, and a broad-match bucket just to discover phrases he had never considered — which is how he found "shoes for first 5K," a term that quietly became his best performer.

The turning point was alignment. His early ads had sent every click to the homepage, and his money evaporated. Once he pointed the "cushioned beginner" ad to a page about exactly that shoe — specs, a size guide, two reviews, and a single "Find your fit" button — his conversion rate tripled on the same spend. He had bid less than a bigger competitor on one keyword and still outranked them, because his quality score was higher: his ad, his page, and the search term all said the same thing.

Six months later, paid search was still bringing customers in on day one, but his organic traffic had crossed it — people were finding Stride for free, because by then the librarian trusted him.

**Being found is not luck. It is the reward for being genuinely relevant, genuinely reputed, and genuinely easy — and, while that reward compounds, for paying to stand exactly where your customer is already looking.**
