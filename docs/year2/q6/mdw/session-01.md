# Session 1: Welcome to a Different World — The Indie Threads Dilemma

*Why "marketing in the digital world" is not the same as "digital marketing" — and what an artisanal kurta brand's channel dilemma reveals about the decisions that actually matter*

---

## Setting the Stage: Who Is Teaching and Why It Matters

The professor joined IIM Udaipur in 2022, fresh out of a PhD from IIM Ahmedabad. His research area? Consumer psychology — how emotions shape decisions, how perceptions drive buying behavior. He is, at his core, a consumer researcher.

When the institute decided that digital marketing should be taught in-house rather than by visiting faculty, he was the new hire who inherited the course. He had no prior digital marketing background. So he did what any good academic would do: he leaned into what he knew best (the consumer) and built outward from there.

Four years and multiple program iterations later, the course has taken its current shape — and this year brings a new experiment. The 20 sessions are split into two modules:

| Module | Sessions | Focus | Faculty |
|--------|----------|-------|---------|
| **Module 1** | 1-14 | Conceptual foundations — how to decide | The professor |
| **Module 2** | 15-20 | AI tools and execution — how to do | Prof. V. Narayan Vantam (marketing leader at Chargebee, ex-Microsoft) |

> **Professor:** "A tool is only as powerful as the person using it. If the person understands the basics, they can be much more effective with the tools. But without basics, even the fanciest tools won't give you the best results — because you won't know what you are doing or why."

## The Name Matters: "Marketing in Digital World" is NOT "Digital Marketing"

This is the single most important idea the professor wants you to internalize before anything else.

When most people hear "digital marketing," they think: *Okay, so there are physical channels and digital channels. I take my existing marketing strategy and push it through digital channels. Done.*

That is a **partial and suboptimal** understanding.

The professor's thesis is more fundamental: **the digital world changes your value proposition itself.** It is not just a new set of pipes to push the same water through — it is a different kind of water entirely.

> **Professor:** "You cannot continue doing the same things as you are doing in the non-digital world, replicate them in the digital world, and expect to be successful. That would be a very suboptimal use of the digital world."

Think about it through an example he brought up: Unilever took decades to build a brand like Reen to a revenue level of, say, 5,000 crore. Digital-first brands like Mamaearth and boAt reached similar revenue levels at roughly **5x the speed**. They clearly followed a different process — not just the same process on different channels.

Even Colgate Palmolive — a company with decades of marketing expertise — recently offloaded its entire D2C (direct-to-consumer) channel to Bombay Shaving Company. If digital were just "traditional marketing on screens," why would Colgate need outside help? The fact that they admitted "we don't have the expertise for D2C" tells you everything: the digital world operates on fundamentally different principles.

## What IS Marketing, Then?

The professor opened this up to the class, and the responses revealed a common pattern — most people think of marketing as **communication**. Getting the word out. Reaching people.

That is part of it, but only part. The professor pointed to the Kotler definition that one student cited:

> **Marketing is creating, communicating, and delivering value to a target market for a price.**

Notice the three verbs: **create**, **communicate**, **deliver**. Communication is sandwiched between two equally important activities. Marketing begins with *what value you create* (the product, the experience, the service) and ends with *how you deliver it* (pricing, placement, the full journey). The 4Ps — product, price, place, promotion — are all marketing, not just the promotion part.

And here is the kicker for this course: **in the digital world, the "create" part changes.** The nature of what you offer as value shifts. That shift cascades downstream into how you communicate, deliver, and price. We will unpack this in the sessions ahead.

## The Course Roadmap: A Decision-Maker's Journey

The first module follows a logical flow that builds on itself. Think of it as a pyramid:

### Layer 1: Understand the World

- How is the value proposition different in the digital world vs. the traditional world?
- What has fundamentally changed?

### Layer 2: Understand the Customer

- Customer personas — who are they, really?
- Customer journey mapping — what path do they walk?
- Customer experience (CX) — how do you design something so intuitive they fall in love?

### Layer 3: Understand the Channels

Each channel gets its own treatment, focused not just on "what is it" but on "what decisions do I make and how?"

| Channel | Key Decision Questions |
|---------|----------------------|
| **Content Marketing** | What content do I create? How do I decide? |
| **Search Marketing** | How does search work? Which keywords? How does this connect to customer understanding? Includes generative engine optimization (GEO) |
| **Influencer Marketing** | What role do influencers play? How do I pick the right one? What types exist? |
| **Social Media** | Organic vs. paid — when to use which? What psychological principles drive engagement? |
| **Email Marketing** | Basics of how email works as a channel. What purpose does it serve? |

### Layer 4: Measure and Analyze

- **Metrics vs. KPIs** — these are NOT interchangeable terms (more on this later)
- Effectiveness analysis — did the campaign work?
- Efficiency analysis — did it work *well* relative to cost?

### Layer 5: Achieve the Goals

- **Customer acquisition** — performance marketing vs. brand marketing, and when to use each
- **Customer retention** — loyalty programs, CRM fundamentals
- **Sustainable growth** — unit economics, balancing acquisition and retention

> **Professor:** "Analysis for the sake of analysis will just lead to paralysis. Analysis for what purpose? For understanding effectiveness, efficiency, and sustainability."

## The Indie Threads Case: Your First Decision Dilemma

This is the case study that anchors Session 1 (and carries into Session 2). Read it like a story, because it is one.

### The Setup

Under the pink bougainvillea that shades their tiny Jaipur studio, **Aditi** hand-mixes natural dyes while **Rohan** stamps a lotus block onto fresh cotton. Their label, **Indie Threads**, makes artisanal kurtas using eco-friendly fabric and indigenous craftsmanship. The work is done by women artisans from nearby tribal villages who carve the blocks, stitch the linings, and share in every sale.

For four years, sales have flowed through **Instagram DMs**. A reel goes viral, customers pay by UPI, Shiprocket prints the label, a courier heads out. It works — about **300 kurtas a month**. But the late-night DM juggling is draining, and growth has plateaued.

### The Two Offers

Two offers land the same week:

| | **Etsy India** (Digital Marketplace) | **FabPlus** (Retail Chain) |
|--|--------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **What they are** | Niche platform for handmade, artisanal products | 400-store nationwide chain that loves the "slow fashion" vibe |
| **Reach** | Global discovery, niche audience | Nationwide physical presence |
| **Commission** | 13% take rate (only when something sells) | 22% retail margin |
| **Payment** | Immediate (built-in payments) | 45 days after sale |
| **Investment needed** | Low — extension of existing digital presence | High — massive inventory for 400 stores |
| **Brand story** | Preserved — Etsy's platform matches artisan values | At risk — mass shelves may bury the niche backstory |

Rohan fears mass shelves will bury their niche and strip away the artisan backstory. Aditi pictures customers actually touching the fabric in a mall. With limited cash, limited production capacity, and a promise to keep village women at the heart of the value chain — they must choose one path for the next year.

### What the Class Thought

The discussion was lively, and almost everyone leaned toward Etsy — but for different reasons. The professor kept playing devil's advocate for FabPlus to sharpen the thinking. Here is what emerged:

**Arguments for Etsy India:**
- **Asset-light model** — no upfront investment in store infrastructure (Satish's point)
- **Extension of current strength** — they already know digital, this is a natural next step (Priyanku)
- **Working capital** — Etsy pays when it sells; FabPlus holds your money for 45 days while you front inventory for 400 stores (Anish, Gourav)
- **Brand alignment** — Etsy's platform matches the artisan ethos; FabPlus might dilute it (Maitri)
- **Pre-order flexibility** — digital platforms allow pre-orders to manage demand; physical stores require stock-or-stockout (Maitri)
- **Real-time insights** — digital gives you immediate data on what is selling; FabPlus analytics come with a delay (Anish)

**Arguments for FabPlus (mostly raised by the professor as counterpoints):**
- **Nationwide recognition** — this could be a dream opportunity for a small brand
- **Touch and feel** — for niche artisanal products, physical contact may build trust and connection (Manish)
- **Growth push** — if you are stuck at 300 kurtas and want to break through, maybe you need a bold move

**The Third Option (Anish's contribution):**
Why not both? Go with Etsy for the core business, but negotiate with FabPlus for just 30-40 stores near Jaipur where logistics are manageable. A phased approach.

### The Criteria That Surfaced

The professor did not give an answer. Instead, he collected the **decision criteria** that emerged organically from the discussion:

| Criterion | What It Asks |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Capacity** | Can you actually fulfill orders at this scale? Where are your constraints better managed? |
| **Stage** | At what stage is your business? Early-stage startups may not survive the capital demands of a retail rollout |
| **Inventory & Working Capital** | Can you afford to stock 400 stores and wait 45 days for payment? |
| **Insights** | How fast do you get data about what is working and what is not? |
| **Trust & Connection** | Does the customer need to touch the product? Can digital channels build equivalent trust? |

> **Professor (on the capacity point):** "The chance of unpredictable order volumes is actually higher with FabPlus than with Etsy. On a digital platform, demand is partly under your control — how much you spend on advertising drives how many orders come in. But with FabPlus and its 400 stores, you don't really know."

This case continues in Session 2, where the professor will dig deeper into what makes digital and physical channels fundamentally different — and whether the assumptions the class made actually hold up.

## Metrics vs. KPIs: A Preview

The professor dropped this distinction without elaborating fully (it comes later), but flagged it as important:

> **Professor:** "Many of you might be familiar with what metrics and KPIs are, but some of you might be unclear about the difference. Usually we use these terms interchangeably, but they are NOT interchangeable. Metric means something different. KPI means something different."

We will learn the characteristics of a good KPI and how to use both for analyzing campaign effectiveness and efficiency. For now, just know: every number you track is a metric, but not every metric deserves to be a Key Performance Indicator.

---

## Engagement vs. Retention: They Are Not the Same

A student asked whether customer engagement and retention are synonymous. The professor was unequivocal:

> **Professor:** "No, not at all. Engagement is when the customer is reacting to you, talking to you, responding to you on social media. Retention is when they keep buying from you — they continue to be your paying customer. Engagement might lead to retention, but they are very different."

This is a distinction worth nailing down early. A brand can have sky-high engagement (likes, comments, shares) and abysmal retention (nobody comes back to buy). The two require different strategies.

---

## Customer Lifecycle: The Goals That Tie Everything Together

The entire course is ultimately in service of a lifecycle:

```
Acquire --> Retain --> Grow --> Sustain
```

- **Acquire**: Get customers through performance marketing or brand marketing (different strategies, different contexts)
- **Retain**: Keep them buying through loyalty programs and CRM
- **Grow**: Scale the business — but is the growth sustainable?
- **Sustain**: Check your unit economics. Are you spending more on acquisition than you are earning? Is your balance between performance marketing and brand marketing correct? Is it lopsided?

> **Professor:** "Ultimately everything boils down to your unit economics. We will learn how to scale our digital marketing in such a way that it is sustainable and our unit economics are positive."

## Recipe Chain

**Session 1 lays the foundation:**
The digital world is fundamentally different from the traditional world — not just a new channel but a new playing field with different rules. Marketing is about creating, communicating, and delivering value, and all three change in the digital world. The Indie Threads case introduces the real decisions founders face: choosing channels based on capacity, stage, inventory, insights, and trust. The course will build from understanding the customer, to mastering channels, to measuring effectiveness, to achieving sustainable growth.

**Next session:** The Indie Threads debate continues. The professor will unpack the criteria that emerged — capacity, stage, insights, trust — and use them to explore what truly differentiates digital from physical channels. Expect the assumptions from this class to be tested.

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **Marketing** | Creating, communicating, and delivering value to a target market for a price |
| **Digital World vs. Digital Channel** | Digital is a fundamentally different world that changes your value proposition — not just another medium for existing strategies |
| **Value Proposition** | What you are offering to the consumer — this itself changes in the digital world |
| **Customer Persona** | A detailed profile of who your customer is — drives all channel and content decisions |
| **Customer Journey Map** | The path a customer walks from awareness to purchase to loyalty |
| **Metrics vs. KPIs** | Every number is a metric; a KPI is a metric that is specifically tied to a key business objective |
| **Engagement vs. Retention** | Engagement = customers reacting to you; Retention = customers continuing to buy from you |
| **Unit Economics** | The revenue and costs associated with a single unit of your business model — the ultimate sustainability check |
| **D2C (Direct-to-Consumer)** | Selling directly to customers without intermediaries — a model that requires fundamentally different expertise than traditional retail |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Kavya had been selling hand-painted silk scarves through Instagram for three years when two emails arrived on the same Thursday morning. The first was from a curator at a boutique marketplace called CraftLoop, a platform that specialized in artisan goods and had a small but devoted following among design-conscious buyers in twelve countries. The second was from a procurement manager at StyleNation, a fast-growing fashion chain with 280 stores across India. Both wanted her scarves. Both sounded thrilled.

She called her older brother Nikhil, who had spent a decade in retail buying. "Take StyleNation," he said immediately. "You have been whispering into your phone for three years. This is your chance to shout. Two hundred and eighty stores, Kavya. Imagine walking into a mall and seeing your scarves on a rack."

Kavya imagined it. She also imagined the order: StyleNation wanted a minimum of fifteen scarves per store per quarter. That was 4,200 scarves every three months. She was making 180 a month. Each scarf took her and two artisans a full day. The math was not complicated — it was impossible. She would need to hire, train, lease a bigger workspace, buy fabric in bulk on credit, and wait 60 days for StyleNation to pay. One bad quarter and she would be borrowing money to keep the lights on.

She took CraftLoop instead. The platform charged a 14% fee, handled payments and international shipping, and gave her a dashboard showing exactly which designs sold, which were clicked but not purchased, and where her buyers lived. Within four months she learned that her peacock-feather pattern outsold everything else three to one, that her biggest cluster of customers was in Berlin, and that scarves priced between 3,800 and 4,200 rupees hit a sweet spot where conversion rates spiked. She adjusted her production accordingly, introduced two new patterns inspired by the data, and her monthly sales climbed from 180 to 340 without a single additional employee.

A year later, a friend from business school asked her if she regretted passing on StyleNation. Kavya thought about it honestly. "I regret the version of it where I could have walked into a mall and touched my own product on a shelf," she said. "But I don't regret the version of it where I would have had to become a completely different business overnight to serve a channel I didn't understand, with money I didn't have, selling to customers I couldn't see. CraftLoop didn't just give me a storefront — it gave me a classroom. Every sale taught me something. StyleNation would have given me volume, but volume without insight is just inventory waiting to become a problem."

The channel you choose is not just a distribution decision — it is a decision about what kind of business you are ready to be, what you can learn, and whether the pace of growth matches the pace at which you can actually grow.

**The best channel is not the one with the most reach — it is the one that teaches you the most while you can still afford the lesson.**
