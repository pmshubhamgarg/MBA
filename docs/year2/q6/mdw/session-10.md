# Session 10: Nobody Posts for Your Brand — The Five Selfish Reasons People Engage, and How to Measure a Campaign While It's Still Running

*Why every like, share, and comment is secretly about the person doing it — and how a veteran analyst's "keep it simple" framework turns a sea of dashboard numbers into decisions you can act on today*

## Picking Up: Paid Is Done, Organic Is Half-Finished

Last session finished **paid social media** — structuring campaigns, refining content, ad placements, budget allocation. The one-line takeaway: paid is **analytics-driven and pay-to-play**. It runs as long as you keep paying, it is optimized for clicks, leads, and sales, and its CTAs are "Shop Now," "Sign Up Now," "Download Now." Whoever you are, however new you are, paid buys you conversions if your analytics are solid.

Organic is a different animal. Where paid is quantitative, organic is **qualitative and insight-driven**. You do not pay for reach — you earn it by making content people *want* to spread. Organic is for **engagement and credibility**, and its CTAs are "Comment below," "Share this," "Tag someone."

| | Paid Social | Organic Social |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Conversions, retargeting | Engagement, credibility |
| **Nature** | Quantitative, analytics-driven | Qualitative, insight-driven |
| **Economics** | Pay-to-play (stops when you stop paying) | Free to run — if content spreads |
| **Optimized for** | Clicks, leads, sales | Likes, shares, comments |
| **Typical CTA** | Shop Now, Sign Up, Download | Comment, Share, Tag a friend |

So the whole question for organic becomes: **what makes content engaging enough to spread on its own?** That is the first half of this session.

## The Big Reframe: People Are Not There for Your Brand

The professor ran five real ads past the class and asked the same question each time — not "what does the brand want?" but **"why will a real person like, comment, or share this?"** The answer is never "to help the brand."

> **Professor:** "People are not working for Zomato. People don't want to spread Zomato's message. People don't want to make Zomato the market leader. People want something else."

This is the engine of all organic social media:

> **Professor:** "If you help people to get what they are wanting from being on social media, they will help you get what you are wanting. You have to help people get what they want — then automatically they will help you get what you want."

So the designer's job is not to beg for engagement ("please like and share"). It is to build content that lets people pursue **their own** goals. There are exactly five such goals.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Look good, feel better, learn, connect, convert"]
The five engagement motives in plain English: people engage to **look good** (impression management), **feel better** (emotion regulation), **learn** (information acquisition), **connect** (social bonding), or **convert others** (persuasion).
:::

## The Five Principles of Organic Engagement

### 1. Impression Management — "Make me look good"

People share content that manages how others (or they themselves) see them. Two flavours: **self-enhancement / status signaling** (look funny, smart, trendy, expert) and **identity signaling** (express beliefs, values, who I am).

**Class example — Zomato's "200 funniest comments win."** Why do thousands comment? Not to promote Zomato. They want others to see them as funny — and sometimes just to see *themselves* as funny. Validation can be **social** (others' eyes) or **self** (your own eyes); both are real motivators.

### 2. Emotion Regulation — "Help me feel better"

People manage their feelings through content — amplifying positives and venting negatives. **Positive emotion sharing**: something sparks joy, you share to prolong and spread it. **Venting / seeking support**: something touches a stored frustration, you share to release it and get empathy.

**Class example — Always "Like a Girl."** The ad evokes strong emotion around gender stereotypes; people share to express a stance and to let bottled-up dissatisfaction out.

### 3. Information Acquisition — "Teach me something"

People are on social media to learn — seek advice, get insights, aid a complex decision (think Reddit, Quora). If engaging gets them knowledge, they engage.

**Class example — NYT Cooking's orange cake: "Comment ORANGE or DM us to get the recipe sent to you."** Roughly 2,800 people commented "orange." The payoff is pure information — a free recipe.

### 4. Social Bonding — "Connect me with people"

Content that fosters shared identity and belonging. Two drivers: **reconnect / rebuild** relationships, and **reduce loneliness** (often unconsciously — you just "feel good").

**Class example — Swiggy "Tag someone who matches your food freak."** Tagging a friend isn't generating leads for Swiggy in the user's mind — it strengthens a friendship, revives an old bond, and quietly earns a friend's attention.

### 5. Persuasion — "Let me change others"

The subtlest one. People don't just **declare** a stand — they want **others to adopt it too**. Sharing becomes an act of converting your followers.

**Class example — Nike "For Once, Don't Do It."** People engaged not merely to show identity but to **mobilize others** — "I stand here, and I want you to stand here with me."

> **Professor:** "It is not just showing their support. It is not just showing their identity. It is also wanting to modify others' behaviour."

Persuasion thrives on **polarizing content**, because both camps fight to be heard.

> **Professor:** "Polarizing content is a very sure-short way of getting engagement, because people from both sides will engage with you. People who oppose will strongly engage; people who support will strongly engage."

But polarization is a double-edged sword — the same post that rallies supporters can trigger boycotts. The professor's caution: it is a **calibrated choice**, consistent with your values, not a cheap engagement hack.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The five organic-engagement principles are the whole answer to "what content goes viral."** For any high-engagement post, one (or more) of these is doing the work: Impression Management, Emotion Regulation, Information Acquisition, Social Bonding, Persuasion. Design content so the user pursues *their* goal — engagement with *your* brand is the by-product. The five example ads map one-to-one: Zomato (impression), Always (emotion/identity), NYT Cooking (information), Swiggy (bonding), Nike (persuasion).
:::

:::note[Good to Know]
These principles overlap — a single ad can hit several. Always touches identity signaling *and* emotion *and* social bonding (people relate to each other in the comments). Don't force clean boxes; ask which motive is **central**.
:::

## Second Half: Measuring a Campaign — Metrics vs. KPIs

The class switched to analytics with a scenario: *You are a digital marketer for Indie Threads. You've launched a paid social campaign to drive traffic and sales to your website. How do you measure performance?*

Students rattled off reach, sales volume, user traffic, CTR. The professor split the question: **which of these is a metric, and which is a KPI?** Are they even different?

The class converged on the right intuition: **all KPIs are metrics, but not all metrics are KPIs.** KPIs are a *subset*. The sharp question is — what makes a metric graduate into a KPI?

> **Professor:** "Metrics are quantitative measures that provide data on various aspects of a campaign. KPIs are specific strategic metrics that align directly with the campaign's key goals."

| | Metric | KPI |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Any quantitative measure of some aspect of the campaign | A strategic metric aligned *directly* with the campaign goal |
| **Scope** | Many — a dashboard may show 100+ | A focused basket of 3–4 |
| **Test** | Measures *something* | If it moves up, you are (confidently) moving toward your goal |
| **Examples** | Total visits, unique visitors, avg. session duration | Conversion rate, average order value, CAC, cost per acquisition |

### Don't Confuse Goal, KPI, and KPI Target

A student offered "100% customer satisfaction" as a KPI. The professor untangled three different things:

- **Goal** — *what you want to achieve* (e.g., 100% customer satisfaction, or "sell more").
- **KPI** — *the measure* that tells you if you're getting there (e.g., customer satisfaction score, conversion rate).
- **KPI Target** — *the number on that measure* you aim for (e.g., 95%, or 5% conversion).

Later in the session, a student confirmed this from real life: his team's **goal** is 100% satisfaction, but the **KPI target** they must not breach is 95%.

> **Professor:** "No metric is always a KPI. What your KPI is depends on what is your goal. If a KPI moves, then I'm definitely moving towards my goal — that direct alignment needs to be there."

:::tip[Important]
A good KPI passes three tests — hold them together:

1. **Goal-focused** — directly tied to the campaign goal (if conversion is the goal, conversion rate qualifies; if awareness is the goal, it doesn't).
2. **Outcome-focused** — reaches past the output toward the bottom line (sales volume → revenue → profit; average order value signals revenue).
3. **Efficiency-focused** — tracks *at what cost* (CAC / cost per acquisition tells you if you're within budget).

No single KPI covers all three — which is why you carry **a basket of 3–4 KPIs** per campaign.
:::

## Why Measure at All? Because Digital Is Real-Time

In the traditional world, data arrives *after* the campaign ends — you analyze, learn, and apply it to the *next* one. In the digital world, every channel streams performance data **live**. That changes the job from *post-mortem* to *in-flight course-correction*: spot an underperforming piece hour-by-hour and fix it before the budget burns.

> **Professor:** "That is the goal of measuring anything — so that you can manage it. You measure something to manage it, in such a way that ultimately your goal is achieved."

## The Campaign Analytics Framework (Avinash Kaushik)

Just picking 3–4 good KPIs is **not enough**. You need a structure that tells you not only *whether* you're on track but *which part* of the campaign is dragging you down. The professor's framework comes straight from **Avinash Kaushik**, a veteran web analyst, via his blog **"Occam's Razor"** and his book **Web Analytics 2.0** — real practitioner wisdom, not textbook theory.

Build it **before** the campaign starts, then monitor against it:

| Step | What You Decide | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Marketing Objective** | The broad business aim | e.g., increase online sales for a new product |
| **2. Campaign Goal** | A quantified, time-bound target *with budget* | e.g., sell 10,000 units in Q1 — **within budget Y** |
| **3. KPIs** | The 3–4 metrics aligned to the goal | CPA, revenue per click, conversion rate, AOV |
| **4. KPI Targets** | A number for each KPI | CPA = 20, RPC = 50, CR = 5%, AOV = 50 |
| **5. Segments for Analysis** | How you'll slice the campaign to compare | ad groups, product categories, platforms, post types |

**On the goal (Step 2):** always bolt a **constraint/budget** onto the number. "Sell 10,000 units in Q1" is easy if you're allowed to overspend — so the goal is really "10,000 units in Q1 *within budget*."

**On targets (Step 4):** set them using past campaigns, industry benchmarks, or the simple rule — *if the target is hit, the goal is hit.* Keep them **feasible and realistic**; an impossible target makes the whole dashboard useless because everything looks "below target."

**On segments (Step 5):** this is where diagnosis happens. Break the campaign into parts and score each against the same KPI targets.

### Worked Example — "Addicted" (US fast-fashion, SEM paid search)

- **Objective:** increase online sales for a new product.
- **Goal:** sell 10,000 units in Q1 (within budget).
- **KPIs / Targets:** CPA = 20, revenue per click = 50, conversion rate = 5%, AOV = 50.
- **Segments:** product categories (dresses, tops, jackets) and ad groups within them (lace, floral, mini, spring dresses).

Now you can see that denim jackets or mini dresses are pushing CPA *above* 20 (higher CPA = worse) while other groups beat the 5% conversion target. You then **deep-dive the weak segment and fix it, or pull the money** and shift it to a category that's working.

### Worked Example — Travel Agency (goals change, so KPIs change)

Two campaigns, two goals:

- **Campaign 1 — awareness & engagement** ("showcase, inspire, get interested"). KPIs: **social media reach, impressions, share of voice, amplification rate.** Goal: reach 500,000 followers and 50,000 interactions in Q1. Segments: platform (Facebook / Instagram / Pinterest), post type (video / static / infographic), topic, destination category.
- **Campaign 2 — conversions** ("book their trips"). Because the goal is bookings, the KPIs shift toward sales metrics.

The lesson the professor wanted: **the same metric is a KPI in one campaign and noise in another — it all depends on the goal.** Reach is a perfect KPI for awareness and a poor one for bookings.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Campaign Analytics Framework = Objective → Goal → KPIs → KPI Targets → Segments.** KPIs tell you *if* you're on track; **segments** tell you *where* the problem is; **targets** tell you *how much* is good or bad. Set it up *before* launch, then course-correct in real time — invest more in winning segments, fix or cut losing ones. "Measure so you can manage."
:::

:::note[Good to Know]
**Occam's Razor** (the blog's namesake) is the "keep it simple" principle — when alternatives exist, pick the simplest; strip away the unneeded. The professor tied it to the engineering maxim **KISS — "Keep It Simple, Stupid."** Kaushik "applies Occam's Razor to analytics," cutting through hundred-metric dashboards to the few that matter.
:::

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | One-Line Definition |
|---|---|
| **Impression Management** | Sharing to shape how others (or you) see you — self-enhancement/status signaling and identity signaling |
| **Emotion Regulation** | Sharing to prolong positive feelings or vent/release negative ones |
| **Information Acquisition** | Engaging to gain knowledge, advice, or insight |
| **Social Bonding** | Engaging to build community, reconnect, or reduce loneliness |
| **Persuasion** | Sharing to change others' opinions — often via polarizing content |
| **Polarizing Content** | Content that provokes both camps — a sure-shot but risky way to earn engagement |
| **Metric** | Any quantitative measure of a campaign; many exist |
| **KPI** | A strategic metric aligned directly with the goal; a 3–4 member subset of metrics |
| **KPI Target** | The specific number a KPI should reach (e.g., 5% conversion) |
| **Good KPI (3 traits)** | Goal-focused, outcome-focused (bottom line), and efficiency-focused (cost) |
| **Campaign Analytics Framework** | Objective → Goal → KPIs → KPI Targets → Segments; set before launch, monitor live |
| **Segments for Analysis** | Slicing a campaign (by ad group, category, platform, post type) to find weak and strong spots |
| **Share of Voice** | Your share of brand mentions/conversations in the category — an engagement/awareness KPI |
| **Amplification Rate** | Shares/reposts as a percentage of your follower base |
| **Occam's Razor** | "Keep it simple" — the name of Avinash Kaushik's analytics blog |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Two weeks after launch, Neha stared at a dashboard that wouldn't stop blinking. Her Indie Threads campaign had great numbers on top — 90,000 impressions, a healthy follower bump, a viral-ish Reel. Her founder was thrilled. Neha was not. Sales were flat.

The problem was that she had fallen for the oldest trap in digital marketing: counting everything and measuring nothing. The impressions felt like progress, but her **goal** was website sales, and nothing on that giant dashboard told her whether she was getting there. So she did what the framework demanded. She wrote the goal down in plain words — *drive 1,200 purchases this quarter, within a fixed ad budget* — and chose four KPIs that would actually move with it: conversion rate, average order value, cost per acquisition, revenue per click. One number each, a target for each, taped above her monitor.

Then she sliced the campaign. Not "how is the campaign doing" but "which **part** is doing well." By product category, by ad group, by platform. The viral Reel that everyone loved? Wonderful reach, a conversion rate of almost nothing — it was pulling in people who wanted entertainment, not kurtas. Meanwhile a plain carousel of three bestsellers, running quietly on a fraction of the budget, was beating her conversion target by half. The dashboard had been screaming the answer for ten days; she just hadn't known where to look.

She moved the money. Pulled spend off the applause and poured it into the carousel. Within a month, sales climbed — not because she created anything new, but because she finally measured the thing she was actually trying to manage.

And on the organic side, her lesson was the mirror image of the same truth. She stopped writing captions that begged — "Please like and share!" — and started writing ones that gave people a reason that was about *them*: a styling tip they could show off (impression management), a question about their favourite festival outfit (social bonding), a behind-the-scenes clip of a village artisan that people shared to express what they believed in (identity and persuasion). Engagement stopped being something she asked for and became something people did for their own reasons.

**Nobody shares for your brand, and no dashboard manages itself. Give people a selfish reason to engage, measure only what ties to your goal, and fix the weak segment while the campaign is still breathing — that is the entire game.**
