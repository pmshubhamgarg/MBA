# Session 3: Four Lenses, Competitive Advantage, and the Polman Gamble

*Even after double materiality, a company is still staring at 8–9 "important" issues. Session 3 adds the final filter (four lenses), shows how sustainability becomes a moat, and tests it all on the most famous case in the course: Paul Polman and the Unilever Sustainable Living Plan.*

## Recap, and the Problem Session 3 Solves

Sessions 1–2 took us from *why* (strategy) to *how to choose* (materiality + stakeholders). But the professor opened Session 3 with a sobering data point from his own study:

> **Professor Gpati:** "I looked at 100 BRSR reports. On average companies list **8.43 material topics** — Tata Steel had **14**, Infosys had **9**. Across 100 companies there were **34 unique material issues**. But any company can realistically work on only **3 to 6**."

**Why this is dangerous:** too many material issues = scattered attention = **the *impression* of greenwashing** ("you state everything, you do nothing"). So Session 3's job is a sharper filter.

He also previewed the decision-maker's reality — which is the mood of the whole session:

> **Professor Gpati:** "As you rise to leadership, what matters is **decision-making under bounded rationality** — you don't have full information, so you go with judgement, a thumb-rule, some gut — and stick with it. That's what Polman did at Unilever."

## The Four Lenses (Distilling 8–9 Issues Down to 4–5)

Take each material issue and pass it through **four lenses**. If it survives, it's genuinely core.

### 1. Purpose Lens
**"Does this fit *why we exist*?"** Mission/vision must be **lived, not just framed on a reception wall.**

- **USLP / Unilever** — purpose baked into strategy.
- **Novo Nordisk** — purpose is fighting **diabetes**; even if that market dips, you don't abandon it, because it *is* your purpose.
- **Mahindra "Rise"** — sustainability built into the program, extending to employees, not just energy mix.

> **Professor Gpati:** "If the market dips for a quarter and you drop the issue, it was never your purpose. Purpose is long-ingrained — it goes beyond a quarterly earnings report."

### 2. Stakeholder Lens
**"What do stakeholders actually *do* — not just *say*?"** Watch **actual behaviour, not stated preference.**

- **The organic-produce study (the professor's own):** door-to-door, *everyone* said they'd buy organic. He returned with organic tur dal priced ~**₹5/kg more** — **nobody bought it.**
- **EV parallel:** people *want* an EV, but will they pay more / give up their petrol car? Tesla and BYD show demand *is* shifting — but you must verify, not assume.

> **Professor Gpati:** "Stakeholders have endless wish-lists. Identify your **definitive stakeholder**, see their *actual* behaviour, and be strategic — don't chase every wish."

### 3. Science / Data / Technology Lens
**"What does the data actually say?"** Don't decide on gut or herd belief. Use **life-cycle assessment (LCA)**.

- **GMO / Bt cotton:** villages *believe* it's bad, yet ~**99.5% of cotton is now genetically modified** — it raised yield and cut water and pesticide use. The *data* tells a different story than the gut.
- Don't mark carbon "material" just because **95% of companies do** — check *your* measurements.

### 4. Business Value Lens
**"Does this actually move my P&L?"** Compliance, cost, revenue, willingness-to-pay. Some value is **invisible but real** — e.g., **talent retention** (critical in AI and pharma).

> **Professor Gpati:** "If customers won't pay the premium — organic, EV — it can't stay material for long. But some things, like talent, don't show up clearly yet are decisive."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**The four lenses: Purpose · Stakeholder · Science/Data · Business Value.** They cut a bloated list of 8–9 material issues down to a focused **4–5**. The professor's warning: most companies *only* use the **business-value lens** (which quietly collapses back into single materiality) — and **almost nobody uses the purpose lens**. A top answer insists on using *all four*.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "PS-BS" / "Please Stop Being Shallow"]
**P**urpose, **S**takeholder, **B**usiness value, **S**cience. Four lenses to go deeper than a checklist. *"Please Stop Being Shallow."*
:::

## The Sticky Test: Sustainability as Competitive Advantage

Why bother distilling? Because sustainability only pays off when it's **sticky** — i.e., it creates a **competitive advantage rivals can't easily copy**.

- **Indigo** — saving fuel, time and energy *is* competitiveness. Even cabin waste (seat covers, etc.) is recycled — "everything is value addition, everything is money." Notice the **behaviour difference**: passengers queue an hour early for Indigo; dawdle for Air India. Operational discipline *is* the sustainability edge, and it **survived even Indigo's bad patches** — because it's embedded, not bolted on.
- **Patagonia** — "responsible apparel" doesn't change when governments change. That's genuine, embedded purpose.
- The failure mode — **rebrand without strategy change:** **Fair & Lovely → Glow & Lovely.** New name, *same* product and value chain → **no competitive advantage**, easily copied (Emami did similar). Changing the label isn't changing the business.

**The three strategic postures (learn this triad):**

| Posture | What it is | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost of compliance** | Bare minimum — "I won't pay a fine, nothing more" | Most of India sits here |
| **Corporate theatre** | Lots of PR/ads, nothing behind it | This is where **greenwashing** lives |
| **Competitive advantage** | Embedded in the value chain, hard to copy | The goal |

> **Professor Gpati:** "In most companies the first budget cut is CSR, the second is sustainability, while marketing goes up. If your sustainability is a true competitive advantage, it survives the cut."

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Ask of any initiative: **is it embedded in the value chain and hard to replicate?** If yes → competitive advantage (sticky). If it's just a renamed product or a PR campaign → corporate theatre / greenwashing. This "sticky test" is the spine of the Unilever case answer.
:::

## THE CASE: Paul Polman and the Unilever Sustainable Living Plan (USLP)

This is the centrepiece of the course. Know the arc cold.

### The Setup
- **2009:** **Paul Polman** becomes Unilever CEO — pointedly an **outsider from P&G** (Unilever vs P&G is a Coke-vs-Pepsi rivalry). Unilever's growth had **plateaued**; the **global financial crisis** was raging.
- Polman's instinct: **"Never waste a good crisis."** A crisis creates the *urgency* to do something radical (the professor's parallel: COVID pushing through hybrid/WFH changes nobody expected).

### The Bold Bet — USLP (2010)
Three audacious goals, built into Unilever's 10-year "Compass" vision:
1. **Improve the health & well-being of 1 billion people.**
2. **Halve the environmental footprint** of products.
3. **Enhance the livelihoods of millions** across the value chain.

What made it radical:
- It stretched across the **entire value chain** — suppliers, factories, transporters, middlemen — not just Unilever itself. (The professor: the value chain is the *hardest* place to drive change, because you influence but don't *own* it.)
- Polman **integrated marketing with sustainability** and refused to publish **quarterly reports**, and refused **hedge-fund money** — explicitly managing for **stakeholders, not just shareholders**.
- This was a **BHAG — Big Hairy Audacious Goal** — with *no precedent*. P&G wasn't doing it.

### How It Showed Up in Brands
- **Lifebuoy** (the red soap): a massive **"handwashing with soap"** health program. Regulators wouldn't let them push the product name, so they promoted the **red soap + the habit**, linking to the government's **Swachh Bharat** mission — and a **billion-litre water-saving** program.
- **Axe** — ditched the absurd "spray and women chase you" ads for humour; a genuine mindset shift in product stewardship and brand messaging.
- **11 of Unilever's ~30 brands** became **"Sustainable Living Brands"** — and they **grew faster** than the rest.

### The Results (2015) — A Genuinely Mixed Scorecard
Of **67 targets** across **9 pillars**:
- **12 met, 48 on track, 7 missed.**
- The hardest misses: **GHG emissions** and **water** — because pushing them **through the value chain** (suppliers *and* consumers) is brutally hard.
- **Financials:** Unilever's shareholding performance **beat P&G**, but overall **growth plateaued** versus **Nestlé**. Great brands, reputation, talent retention — but the numbers flattened (and 2008 was a crisis year, 2015 wasn't, flattering the comparison).

### The Crossroads (the exam's favourite question)
By 2015 Unilever stands at a fork: **do more / scale back / pivot & refocus?** 

:::tip[Hindsight is 20/20 — the trap to avoid]
In the exam, don't just say "approve, it worked." The professor's point: in **2008, with no precedent**, would a board *really* back a 52-year-old P&G outsider's unprecedented plan? They did — but only because **plateaued growth created urgency**, and Polman then **institutionalised** it. Show you understand the decision as it looked *then*, under bounded rationality.
:::

## Shareholder vs Stakeholder (and Milton Friedman)

The case forces this distinction:

- **Shareholder** — owns equity; cares about **dividends, growth, bottom line**.
- **Stakeholder** — anyone who affects or is affected by the business (the Session 2 definition).

**Milton Friedman's doctrine** (cite it): *the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits* — the manager is an agent for shareholders. From a **pure shareholder view, USLP looked bad**: no quarterly reports, no clear near-term financial payoff. That's precisely why Polman had to *reframe* Unilever around **stakeholders**.

**And employees?** Also uneasy — in FMCG, incentives are tied to **sales and market share**; stretching returns over 6–7 years delayed bonuses, and **9 pillars / 67 indicators** made it unclear how they'd even be *measured*. Roughly **a third of leadership left**. (This is why employee wellness and DEI surveys later became part of the picture.)

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Shareholder = owns stock, wants profit now (Friedman). Stakeholder = anyone affected, wider and longer-term.** Polman's whole gamble was shifting Unilever from a **shareholder** frame to a **stakeholder** frame — no quarterly reporting, no hedge-fund money. Name-drop **Friedman** for the counter-argument and you've nailed the tension of the case.
:::

## Institutionalising Sustainability: Kotter's 8 Steps

How did Polman make it *stick* rather than fade? The professor mapped it onto **Kotter's 8-step change model** (he said "Kotler K" — he means **Kotter**). It applies to *any* big strategy, not just sustainability:

1. **Establish urgency** (plateaued growth, the crisis).
2. **Build a coalition** (restructure; give the right people the roles).
3. **Create a vision** (USLP / the big bet).
4. **Communicate the vision** (relentless meetings, town halls).
5. **Empower people to act.**
6. **Generate short-term wins** (prove it's actionable).
7. **Consolidate** gains.
8. **Institutionalise** — bake it into KPIs, SOPs, culture.

**Indian parallel:** **Tata** did this via the **Tata Sustainability Management Group (TSMG)** — vision → SOPs across Tata companies → training → institutionalised (CSR first, then sustainability). Big firms often hire **McKinsey/Bain/BCG** to run exactly this playbook.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Polman's 8-step ladder"]
Urgency → Coalition → Vision → Communicate → Empower → Short wins → Consolidate → Institutionalise. If you can't make change *stick* (step 8), it dies with the next budget cut.
:::

## The Market-Mover Effect

One more point that scores well: Unilever didn't just change itself — it **moved the whole FMCG market**. **Marico** (the "₹2 of this goes to CSR" ad) and others followed, later building real programs. Because a genuine, embedded advantage **can't be copied quickly**, Unilever got a real head start — the very "stickiness" the four lenses are designed to find.

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 3 is the payoff. Materiality tells you what *could* matter; the **four lenses** tell you what *truly* matters; **competitive advantage** tells you whether it's worth doing; and **Kotter's 8 steps** tell you how to make it last. Paul Polman's Unilever is the living exam case — bold vision, real brand wins, honest misses in the value chain, and a genuine shareholder-vs-stakeholder tension. If you can argue *both* sides of "should the board have backed USLP?" using the four lenses and the sticky test, you can answer almost anything this course throws at you.
