# Session 1: The Risks Beyond Your Visual Range

*Why the biggest threats to a business are the ones sitting outside the CEO's line of sight — and how "sustainability" is really just the lens that lets you see them.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** Sustainability as a **business strategy / non-market strategy** (not CSR charity); the **risk ↔ opportunity** duo; the **Brundtland definition** (meeting present needs without compromising future generations); **Triple Bottom Line** (People, Planet, Profit) and how **ESG** maps onto it; the shift from **voluntary codes → mandatory disclosure**.

**Important (supporting):** Why the course is called **EGS, not ESG**; the WEF short-term vs long-term risk split; **license to operate**; **BRSR** (250 → 1000 companies); the five levers (competitive advantage, cost, license to operate, talent, bottom line).

**Context (colour for case answers):** Coca-Cola water, HUL/Surf, Budweiser, Tata "we also make steel", BHP/Vedanta mining, single-use plastic & paper straws, Delhi diesel scrapping, Larry Fink's U-turn.
:::

## Setting the Stage: Who's Teaching, and Why It's "EGS"

Your professor is **Vijay Gpati**, who runs a sustainability and CSR consulting firm called **Thinkru Consulting** (~100 people). He's spent **30 years** in this space — starting in grassroots NGOs, then UN agencies, then consulting at **KPMG** and **Ernst & Young**, before founding his own firm in 2015. So when he gives an example, it's usually a real project he worked on, not a textbook box.

His promise for the course:

> **Professor Gpati:** "Most of my insights will be practical — on what is happening, how exactly decisions are made, how strategies around sustainability are made, and the key terms you need to remember going forward."

**Why "EGS" and not the famous "ESG"?** A student asked this directly. The answer matters for the exam because it frames the whole course:

> **Professor Gpati:** "ESG is Environment, Social and Governance — but in many cases ESG gets **taken over by the E**. We wanted more around society and governance as well. The overall rubric is **sustainability**. ESG is one strategic option within that, not the whole thing."

So the mental model is: **Sustainability is the big umbrella. ESG is one lens under it.** The course keeps zooming out to strategy, not down to compliance checklists.

:::tip[The One-Line Thesis of the Whole Course]
Sustainability is a **non-market strategy** — a way to manage risks and capture opportunities that don't show up on a normal P&L or a competitor map, but can still make or break the business. Treat it as **strategy**, not charity.
:::

### How You'll Be Graded

- **Two announced quizzes**, 15 marks each — held at the **start of class on Saturdays** (around the 5:10 pm slot), so people can join in.
- **~70% interterm** exam.
- Format is **MCQ + case-based**. The professor deliberately avoided surprise quizzes after students pushed back, so quizzes are announced and sit at the beginning of a session.

**Exam takeaway:** expect questions that give you a company situation and ask you to *apply* a concept (materiality, a lens, a risk) — not just define it. Memorising definitions alone won't carry you.

## The Opening Scene: A Risk You Can't See Coming

Picture a mid-sized Indian IT services firm. Comfortable margins, big US client, nothing to worry about. The CEO's strategy deck is all about profit and the next quarter.

Then, somewhere far outside that deck:
- The US slaps a **tariff** on a category of exports.
- A **government changes** in a partner country (Bangladesh) and a running project is disrupted overnight.
- Delhi suddenly **scraps 10-year-old diesel vehicles**, and a secondary car market collapses.
- A **gaming law** redefines "game of chance" at 50% — and an entire fantasy-sports industry's fate flips.

None of these appeared on the competitor map. The professor has a phrase for exactly this:

> **Professor Gpati:** "These risks are there **beyond their visual range**. Companies at times are unable to perceive them, yet they have a lot more impact on the business. Understanding sustainability and ESG helps us navigate these things."

That's the emotional core of Session 1: **the dangerous risks are the invisible ones**, and sustainability is the lens that brings them into focus.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**No company is immune.** Sustainability is not about being "green and nice." It is about seeing **environmental, social and geopolitical risks that sit outside a company's normal field of vision** — and realising they still hit the bottom line. If a case asks "why should this company care about sustainability?", your first move is: *because these risks are real, financial, and beyond its visual range.*
:::

## Big Idea 1: The World Economic Forum Risk Map (Short-Term vs Long-Term)

The professor ran a live poll based on the **World Economic Forum (WEF) Global Risks** report — the view of **13,000+ global experts**. He split it into two questions:

| | **Short-term (next 2 years)** | **Long-term (next 10 years)** |
|---|---|---|
| **What dominates** | "Flavour of the month" — geoeconomic confrontation, misinformation/disinformation, state-based armed conflict, societal polarisation | **Environmental, societal and geopolitical** — extreme weather, biodiversity loss, critical change to Earth systems |
| **Example** | Iran, Gaza, US–Canada tariffs, India–China friction | Climate change, water stress, ecosystem collapse |
| **Why it shifts** | Trump "may become lame-duck in 2 years"; today's headline fades | These are structural and compounding — they don't go away |

**Concept → Why it matters:** Short-term risks *change* constantly; the long-term list is remarkably stable and is **environmental + societal**. A company that only reacts to this quarter's headline keeps getting blindsided. Sustainability forces you to plan for the slow, big risks too.

> **Professor Gpati:** "In the short term it's geoeconomic confrontation. But in a 10-year horizon, the much bigger challenge is around environment, society and technology. Preparing yourself for that shift is what sustainability gives you — a lens, a view."

**The interconnection point (easy to miss, loved in exams):** The risks are **all interrelated**. The professor flagged that **inequality** barely registered in the poll (only ~10% of 59 people picked it) — yet it's linked to almost every other risk (license to operate, social polarisation, unrest). Lesson: don't score a risk in isolation.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Short list shuffles, long list stays green"]
The 2-year risks reshuffle with the news cycle. The 10-year risks are almost always **environmental + societal**. If an exam asks what risks a board should *really* plan for, point at the long list.
:::

## Big Idea 2: Every Risk Has a Business Transmission Line

The professor's real skill here was showing **exactly how** a far-away risk reaches a specific company. Keep these paired examples — they're perfect case-answer ammunition:

| Risk (abstract) | How it hits a real business |
|---|---|
| **Extreme weather / water stress** | Plant shutdowns — **Coca-Cola**; **HUL** builds an entire brand story around *saving water* |
| **Geoeconomic confrontation** | Tariffs, sanctions, export controls — a Walmart project disrupted; India's goods exporters hit |
| **Misinformation / social backlash** | **Budweiser's** trans brand-ambassador campaign → market-share hit → had to withdraw |
| **Inequality / license to operate** | Mining and extractive firms blocked by communities; the famous **Tata "we also make steel"** ad built goodwill |
| **Policy / regulation shift** | **Delhi diesel scrapping**, **BS6** norms, ethanol, EV rules — whole markets swing |

> **Professor Gpati:** "Each of these seems pretty big, but each of them impacts business. That's where the profit and loss gets challenged."

**Why it matters:** this is the move that turns "sustainability" from a feel-good word into a **strategy conversation**. Every environmental or social issue is really a *business exposure* in disguise.

## Big Idea 3: Risk is Only Half the Story — Sustainability Creates Opportunity

The second half of the session flips the mood. Sustainability isn't only defence; it opens **new business**.

**The star example — Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) & single-use plastic:**

India banned single-use plastic and put the **responsibility to recover plastic back on the producer**. That sounds like pure cost. But watch the opportunity appear:

> **Professor Gpati:** "A ₹10 Maaza tetra-pack has a plastic straw. They can't use it anymore. Put a plastic cap instead and it becomes costly — now you can't sell at ₹10. So what happened? **Paper straws.** Almost every company in India moved to paper straws. A constraint created a whole new product."

More opportunity examples to bank:
- **Catalytic converters** — not just cars; cement plants, thermal plants (tightening emission norms = new tech market).
- **Water treatment / STPs** — textile dyeing units must now recycle water instead of dumping it in rivers.
- **Recycling** — plastic and paper recycling as a genuine business line, especially for EU export markets with stricter norms.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Sustainability = Risk management AND Opportunity creation.** Weak answers only talk about avoiding fines. Strong answers show *both* sides: the risk it defuses *and* the new revenue, cost saving, or competitive edge it unlocks. The EPR → paper-straw story is the cleanest example of a constraint becoming a business.
:::

## Big Idea 4: What Does "Sustainability" Even Mean? (The Definitions Arc)

There is **no single agreed definition** — and the professor says that's itself a core problem (it makes measurement, rating and disclosure messy). But one definition anchors everything:

**The Brundtland definition (1987) — learn this verbatim-ish:**

> Using the resources you have **without compromising the ability of future generations / future stakeholders to meet their own needs.**

This is **intergenerational equity**. For a mining company it's existential; with AI it's resurfacing (energy and water use, "guardrails").

The timeline the professor sketched (1972 → today):

| Milestone | What it added |
|---|---|
| **1972 Stockholm** | Environment enters the global agenda |
| **1987 Brundtland Report** | The future-generations definition; "common planet" |
| **1997 Elkington** | **Triple Bottom Line** — perform on economic prosperity, environmental quality *and* social equity (this is where ESG grows from) |
| **Rio Summit / UN Global Compact** | Voluntary global commitments |
| **Today** | Mandatory disclosure, EU directives, stringent standards |

**The "going concern" analogy (nice for answers):** In accounting, a *going concern* is a business expected to keep operating. The professor's point: if you keep polluting and ignoring society, **people will eventually demand accountability** and you may *stop* being a going concern. Sustainability protects the license to keep existing.

## Big Idea 5: Triple Bottom Line, the 5 Ps, and ESG

Start with **Triple Bottom Line = People, Planet, Profit** (the 3 Ps). The world then added two more, giving the **5 Ps**:

**People · Planet · Profit · Prosperity · Peace (+ Partnership)**

These 5 Ps map directly onto the **UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** framing.

**ESG** then slots in as the corporate measurement version:
- **E** — Environment (emissions, water, waste)
- **S** — Social (labour practices, gender & diversity, human rights, product responsibility)
- **G** — Governance (board diversity, ethics, transparency)

:::tip[Memory Hook — "3 Ps grow to 5 Ps"]
**People, Planet, Profit** (Elkington's triple bottom line) → add **Prosperity, Peace, Partnership** → the 5 Ps of the SDGs. ESG is just the corporate scoreboard that tracks the E, S and G of all this.
:::

**SDGs in numbers:** 17 goals, 231 indicators. India ranks roughly **94th** — "way back," the professor admits, but NITI Aayog and others are pushing. Covers everything from poverty and hunger to responsible consumption, climate action, and life below water.

## Big Idea 6: The Big Shift — Voluntary Codes → Mandatory Disclosure

This is a theme the whole course returns to. The direction of travel is **one-way**:

**Voluntary code → Mandatory code → Directive**

> **Professor Gpati:** "Nowhere can you impose these things on corporates in one go. Every idea starts somewhere, becomes a voluntary code, then mandatory, then a directive. It has to be gradual."

Concrete markers of the shift:
- **BRSR** (Business Responsibility & Sustainability Reporting) in India: expanded from the **top 250 → top 1000** listed companies.
- **EU CSRD** (Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive) and **CSDDD / CS3D** (Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive) — making reporting *and* supply-chain due diligence mandatory.
- **Carbon credits:** a real market, but the price has fallen; and crucially, people now demand **real local action over offsetting** — if you pollute in Kerala, don't "offset" in Telangana. (This is why India's CSR law nudges work *near the factory premises*.)
- **CBAM** (EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism): export more carbon-heavy steel to the EU and you pay — so what *wasn't* material for Tata Steel suddenly *becomes* material.

:::info[Priority Map — The Acronym Shortlist]
**BRSR** (India reporting), **CSRD** (EU reporting), **CSDDD/CS3D** (EU due diligence), **CBAM** (EU carbon border tariff), **EPR** (extended producer responsibility). If you can define these five in one line each, you're covered for the "regulation" MCQs.
:::

## Big Idea 7: The Five Levers — Why Boards Actually Buy In

When the professor says "sustainability is strategy," he means it pays off through **five concrete levers**:

1. **Competitive advantage** — a differentiator rivals can't easily copy.
2. **Cost advantage** — less water, energy, waste = lower cost.
3. **License to operate** — communities and governments *let you* run (vital for mining/extractive: **BHP**, **Vedanta** in Zambia/Zimbabwe, **Adani–Rio Tinto** opposition in Australia).
4. **Talent attraction & retention** — people want to work for credible firms.
5. **Bottom line / market share & investability** — ESG ratings now gate foreign investment in sensitive sectors.

> **Professor Gpati:** "I look at sustainability from a strategy point of view, a competitive-advantage point of view, and a bottom-line point of view. It's not just 'good to have.'"

**Real cascade example — L&T:** Larsen & Toubro had to get **~250 big vendors** reporting on sustainability. Why? Because large firms don't operate in isolation — **~90% of CSR money sits with the top 1000 companies**, and governments pressure the big players, who then **cascade** requirements down their supply chain. You may be an MSME today, but your big client will drag you in.

## The Honest Debate: Greenwashing, Green-Hushing and "Tick the Box"

The professor is refreshingly candid — this field is **contested**:

- **Greenwashing** — claiming more than you do.
- **Green-hushing** — staying quiet to avoid scrutiny.
- **Virtue signalling / corporate theatre** — saying, not doing.
- **"Tick the box"** — a student (ex-**ITC**, ~2015) recalled benchmarking Infosys, HDFC, Nestlé but feeling it "lacked spirit" — lots of compliance cost and jargon, missing the core. Especially true for MSMEs, who get long timelines ("bigger firms today, smaller firms tomorrow, smallest maybe never").
- Even **Larry Fink (BlackRock)** once called sustainability critical, then **walked that position back** — proof the targets are still moving.

**But the conclusion is firm:**

> **Professor Gpati:** "No big company of repute can go back and say sustainability is something we're not looking at. There's greenwashing, green-hushing, everything — but they cannot ignore it and say it doesn't matter."

**Exam nuance:** the sophisticated answer acknowledges the debate (yes, greenwashing is real; yes, there's a compliance-cost burden) *and still* concludes the direction is unavoidable because regulation, investors and social media keep tightening the screws.

## Real-World Takeaway

Sustainability, done right, is just **good strategy with a wider field of vision.** It sees the tariff, the water crisis, the community protest and the EU directive *before* they hit the P&L — and it turns some of those threats into products (paper straws), cost savings (water recycling) and reputation (license to operate).

The companies that treat it as a tick-box chore keep getting ambushed by risks "beyond their visual range." The ones that treat it as strategy see the ambush coming — and occasionally sell tickets to it.
