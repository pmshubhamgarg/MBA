# Session 2: Four Approaches, and the Art of Choosing What Matters

*When every ESG issue screams "I'm important," how does a company decide what to actually work on? Session 2 hands you the filters: four approaches to sustainability, plus the two master tools — materiality and stakeholders.*

---

:::info[Priority Map — What to Focus On]
**Must know (exam-critical):** The **four approaches** — System, Science-based, Circular, Shared Value; **Materiality** (and "don't boil the ocean"); **Single vs Double Materiality** (the 2×2 matrix); **Stakeholder salience = Power + Legitimacy + Urgency**; **Scope 1 / 2 / 3** emissions.

**Important (supporting):** The four **drivers** that push companies to act; **planetary boundaries** (9 boundaries, 7 crossed); why materiality is **dynamic** and context-dependent; silent/intergenerational and emerging (gig-worker) stakeholders.

**Context (colour for case answers):** Volkswagen Dieselgate, palm oil, Coca-Cola/data-centre water, Tetra Pak & Tata recycling, ITC e-Choupal, HUL Project Shakti, Amul/NDDB, Infosys & Tata Steel materiality maps.

## The Four Drivers: Why Companies Move at All

Before choosing *what* to do, know *what forces* them. The professor ranked them for India:

1. **Regulation** — the biggest push (BRSR, EU laws). A live example: **IRDAI proposing to cap insurance commissions** rattled PolicyBazaar's stock — regulation hits profit directly.
2. **License to operate** — community/government acceptance (mining, energy, cement).
3. **Investor concern** — foreign money needs ESG/sustainability ratings before it will invest in sensitive sectors.
4. **Customer demand** — weaker in India, strong globally. Example: **Patanjali's** Ayurveda surge *forced* **HUL** to launch Ayurvedic products it had ignored for decades.

:::tip[Memory Hook — "R-L-I-C"]
**R**egulation, **L**icense to operate, **I**nvestor concern, **C**ustomer demand. In India the order is roughly that; globally, customer demand climbs higher.
:::

## The Four Approaches (Lenses) to Sustainability

These are **not mutually exclusive** — they're four angles you combine to build a strategy. This is a classic MCQ/short-note target, so learn all four with one example each.

### 1. System-Based Approach
**Concept:** Economy, environment and society are **one interconnected system**. You must *optimise all three*, not let one grow while the others collapse. Look for **feedback loops** and supply-chain interdependencies.

- **Example (growth gone wrong):** Bangalore/Delhi boom — economy grew, but environment and social systems (water, housing, migration) buckled. Karnataka even asked **Wipro** for campus land to build roads; it refused — a snapshot of unsustainable development.
- **Example (supply-chain loop):** India imports **palm oil**; plantations in Malaysia/Indonesia clear forests → environmental *and* social damage (people lose forest livelihoods).
- **The signature case — Volkswagen "Dieselgate":** chasing market share, VW installed a **defeat device** to fake emission tests (showing legal NOx limits while polluting far more). The system view was ignored — profit optimised, ethics and environment trashed. Cost: a **US$14.7 billion** US settlement.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**System thinking = optimise the whole, not one part.** Volkswagen is the textbook failure: maximising one variable (sales) while betraying the system (ethics + environment) created a catastrophic, interconnected loss. If a case shows one metric winning at everything else's expense, name the **system-based approach**.
:::

### 2. Science-Based Approach
**Concept:** Let **data and scientific limits**, not gut feeling, set your targets. Anchor: the **nine planetary boundaries** — the limits that keep Earth liveable (fresh water, land-use change, biosphere integrity, CO₂ concentration, novel entities like plastics/chemicals, ozone, ocean acidification…). **Scientists say we've already crossed 7 of the 9.**

- Countries take targets (e.g., **limit warming to 1.5°C**) → push them down to companies → companies must build strategy around them.
- **Example:** fresh water — a mid-size **data centre** can consume ~**20 million litres** of water; Coca-Cola must recycle and treat water on-site. Land-system change is a mining company's core headache (compensatory afforestation).

:::tip[Memory Hook — "9 boundaries, 7 already broken"]
The science-based approach starts from a scary fact: of the **9 planetary boundaries**, we've blown through **7**. That's why governments keep tightening rules — your strategy should aim *ahead* of them.
:::

### 3. Circular Approach
**Concept:** **Cradle-to-cradle**, not cradle-to-grave. Design products with a "**life after life**" — reuse, recycle, co-process. Because consumption isn't falling fast enough, **reuse/recycle** becomes the realistic lever.

- **Coca-Cola** — t-shirts from recycled PET bottles.
- **Tetra Pak** — desks, tables and waste-baskets from used cartons.
- **Tata** — recycling car bumpers back into production.
- **Textiles** — recycled yarn into mats and runners; **IKEA** uses recycled materials; **cement plants** co-process single-use plastic as fuel.
- **E-waste** — old laptops refurbished for government schools; incoming rules for **battery tracking** (unique IDs to trace end-of-life).

### 4. Shared Value Approach
**Concept:** From **Porter & Kramer** — create value for the *business* by creating value for *stakeholders*. It looks **beyond philanthropy and compliance**: grow the ecosystem and you grow yourself.

- **ITC e-Choupal** — gives farmers price information and a fair route to market.
- **HUL Project Shakti** — trains rural women (self-help groups) as retailers.
- **Nestlé** — supports dairy farmers with veterinary care, fodder and knowledge → assured quality milk.
- **Amul / NDDB (Mother Dairy)** — the entire cooperative model *is* shared value: not just procuring milk, but giving farmers information on cattle, fodder and veterinary care.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
Memorise the four approaches as a set: **System · Science-based · Circular · Shared Value.** A strong strategy answer uses *more than one* — e.g., "a circular packaging programme (circular) that also lifts waste-picker incomes (shared value), targeting a planetary-boundary issue (science-based), across the whole value chain (system)."
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "Systems Science Cycles Share"]
**S**ystem → **S**cience-based → **C**ircular → **S**hared value. Say it as *"Systems Science Cycles, everyone Shares."*
:::

## Master Tool 1: Materiality — "Don't Boil the Ocean"

You can't fix everything. **Materiality** is the discipline of choosing the handful of issues that *actually matter* to your business and stakeholders.

> **Professor Gpati:** "In corporate meetings people say *don't boil the ocean*. Materiality is exactly that — there are endless issues, but a company can realistically work on only a few. It forces you to say: these I'll do, these I won't."

**Context is everything — materiality is not one-size-fits-all:**
- What's material for **Tata** may not be material for **Mahindra**; what's material for **Infosys** may not be for **Wipro** or **TCS**.
- For an **IT firm**: data privacy, energy, water (for data centres), occupational health, maybe AI.
- For a **miner**: land use, water, community.

**Why it even exists (great exam line):** In India, CSR is often **owner-driven** (the professor's blunt example: individual promoters' pet projects). Materiality **forces a scientific, business-logic process** instead of the "whims and fancies of individuals."

**The materiality process:**
1. Understand the **business model** and value chain.
2. List all topics → build an **impact / risk / opportunity register** (using planetary-boundary and ESG lenses).
3. **Talk to stakeholders** (customers, clients, regulators, sometimes media) — honest voices, not a copy-paste.
4. **Score and validate** → place on the matrix.
5. Build **strategy, KPIs and KRAs** around the critical few.

:::tip[Important — Materiality is Dynamic]
Revisit materiality **every 6–12 months**. What wasn't material can *become* material: **CBAM** made carbon suddenly material for **Tata Steel**; the **new labour code** will make **gig-worker welfare** material for e-commerce. A warning sign of laziness: companies that just **rehash last year's** material list and only redesign the report cover.
:::

## Single vs Double Materiality (The 2×2 Matrix)

This is the heart of Session 2 and a near-certain exam question.

- **Single materiality (old view):** only **how your business/products impact the outside world** — e.g., how your steel plant affects nearby communities. *(Impact going one direction: company → world.)*
- **Double materiality (EU/CSRD view):** *also* **how those issues impact your business financially.** Both arrows.

**The clean example — water for HUL:**
- *Impact side:* taking water hurts the community.
- *Financial side:* if the government later **charges** for water you currently get free, it hits you financially.
- Water is material **both ways** → it lands high on the double-materiality matrix.

**The matrix:**
- **X-axis = impact on society/environment**
- **Y-axis = financial impact on the enterprise**
- The **top-right quadrant** (high on both) = your most material issues → where you concentrate.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Single materiality = impact on the world. Double materiality = impact on the world + impact on your finances.** EU rules (CSRD + ESRS) now require **double** materiality. In a 2×2, the **high-impact *and* high-financial** corner is where strategy goes. Draw the matrix if the question allows — examiners love the diagram.
:::

**The live Infosys exercise (worth remembering):** The class debated whether **climate change** was material for Infosys. Intuition said "low — an IT firm emits little." But Infosys' own report placed climate change **high**, because its energy/fuel/emissions footprint and stakeholder expectations made it so. Lesson: **context + stakeholder input can override your gut.** Meanwhile **data privacy (DPDP)** is low on *impact* but very high on *financial* materiality for an IT firm.

**Tata Steel twist:** they marked **water consumption as *not* critical** — shocking for a steel plant — because they'd *already* solved their water security earlier, so in *their* current context it's less material. Materiality is always relative to where you already are.

## Scope 1, 2, 3 Emissions (The Decarbonisation Map)

When you tackle emissions, you split them three ways:

| Scope | What it covers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope 1** | Direct — within your own factory/premises | On-site combustion |
| **Scope 2** | Indirect — energy/fuel you **purchase** | Bought electricity |
| **Scope 3** | Your entire **supply/value chain** | Vendors, logistics, customers |

**The killer stat:** for **Mahindra, ~85% of GHG emissions sit in Scope 3** — the supply chain. So decarbonising means dragging *every vendor* along. That's why **US/EU clients audit your whole supply chain**, and why a firm can lose business because its *suppliers* aren't sustainable. (Traceability is hard, so companies start with **estimates + self-declaration**, then audit a few big suppliers — e.g., UK firms getting **human-rights audits** done on their Indian BPOs/KPOs.)

:::tip[Memory Hook — "1 = Me, 2 = my Power bill, 3 = my Posse"]
**Scope 1** is you directly, **Scope 2** is the energy you buy, **Scope 3** is everyone in your value chain. Scope 3 is usually the biggest — and the hardest.
:::

## Master Tool 2: Stakeholders — Who Can Make or Break You

**Stakeholders** = anyone who benefits, is harmed, or whose rights are affected by what the company does.

- **Internal:** employees, managers, owners.
- **External:** suppliers, government, creditors, shareholders, customers, media, NGOs, local community.
- **Silent / intergenerational stakeholder:** the **environment** — you don't treat it as a stakeholder directly; its impact flows through people and communities (and future generations).
- **Emerging stakeholder:** **gig workers.** Five years ago, nobody listed them. Now an Uber driver is a critical stakeholder — a commission change can make or break the business. (The professor even spotted an **"Amazon Ashray"** rest-stop for delivery workers.)

**Why NGOs & local government matter (mining/extractive):** they've blocked or delayed major projects — **Vedanta**, **Sterlite**, the **Kudankulam** nuclear plant, **Tata's Singur** exit. For extractive firms, the local NGO is often the *most* powerful stakeholder.

### Stakeholder Salience: Power + Legitimacy + Urgency

How do you prioritise among stakeholders? Score each on three attributes (this is **Mitchell, Agle & Wood's salience model**):

- **Power** — can they produce an effect on you (harm or help)?
- **Legitimacy** — is their claim valid/appropriate?
- **Urgency** — does their claim demand immediate attention?

**The gig-worker illustration:** a Zomato/Rapido rider's claim ("₹35–40 per delivery is too little") is **legitimate** and **urgent** — but they lack **power** (no collective bargaining, by design they're fragmented). So they are a **dependent stakeholder**: they rely on others (or the firm/regulator) to act for them.

:::danger[Must Know — Exam Critical]
**Stakeholder salience = Power + Legitimacy + Urgency.** All three = *definitive* stakeholder (act now). Legitimacy + urgency but no power = *dependent* stakeholder (e.g., gig workers). Being able to classify a case's stakeholders on these three axes is a high-value exam move.
:::

:::tip[Memory Hook — "PLU: the stakeholder price tag"]
**P**ower, **L**egitimacy, **U**rgency. Like a "PLU" barcode at the till — it tells you how much a stakeholder "weighs" in your decision.
:::

## Real-World Takeaway

Session 2 is where sustainability stops being a slogan and becomes a **decision discipline**. Pick your approach(es), run honest **materiality** (single *and* double), map emissions across **Scope 1–3**, and rank **stakeholders** by power, legitimacy and urgency. Do this well and you focus on the vital few instead of drowning in a long, box-ticking list — which, as Session 3 shows, is itself a form of greenwashing.
