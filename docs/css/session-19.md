## Group 3: GCPL vs. Dabur — Buying Belonging vs. Being Family

### The Origin Story

> "In 1987, a young lawyer named Adeshar Godrej walked away from his practice to start making locks. He wasn't very good at law. He turned out to be rather good at locks."

A century and a bit later, that company sells soap in Nigeria, insecticide in Indonesia, and hair extensions across Africa — Godrej Consumer Products Limited (GCPL). The comparison firm is Dabur India, a 140-year-old house. Both chase the same Indian consumer, but ask them the three corporate-strategy questions — **what business should we be in, how much of the chain should we own, how far from home should we go** — and you get completely different answers.

### The Numbers at a Glance

| Metric | GCPL | Dabur |
|---|---|---|
| **FY25 revenue** | Roughly 14,364 crore | -- |
| **Countries / plants** | Over 200 countries, 27 plants | -- |
| **Domestic revenue share** | 61 percent (39 percent international) | -- |
| **Herfindahl index (diversification)** | 7,406 | 3,174 |
| **Vertical integration (value-added/sales)** | 54.1 percent | 47.5 percent |
| **International revenue share** | 39 percent | Roughly 26.5 percent |

GCPL's business rests on **three core pillars** — home care, personal care, hair care — plus a newer fourth: pet care.

### Group Belongingness: Better-Off and Ownership Tests

GCPL sits inside the broader **Godrej Properties** business group — a real multi-business group where **Godrej Agrovet supplies palm oil** and **Godrej Properties lends land and engineering muscle**. The **better-off test is a clear yes**. The **ownership test is moderate** — group overhead and a loss of autonomy come with the benefit.

Dabur is completely different: the **Burman family holds 67 percent**, but there's no sister company down the corridor to trade favors with. What the family gives instead is **patience capital that doesn't demand results next quarter** — 130 years of being custodians of the brand. So the **better-off test is partial**, but the **ownership cost is low** because of lean governance with little bureaucracy.

> **Formula for the exam: GCPL's advantage is built on resources (a business-group ecosystem). Dabur's advantage is built on governance (patient family capital, low overhead).**

### Diversification: The Herfindahl Gap

> "Don't put all your eggs in one basket — the oldest advice in finance. The Herfindahl index counts the basket."

GCPL scores **7,406**, Dabur scores **3,174** — a real gap worth sitting with, because **Dabur pulls 81 percent of its revenue from one segment** (consumer care), with food at 17 percent as the only other pillar worth naming. GCPL runs **four buckets, none of which crosses 32 percent**.

A BCG-style read of GCPL's portfolio: **Good Knight is the star** (leading, still-growing category), **soaps are the cash cow** (great share, mature market, steady cash), **hair care and pet care sit as question marks** (promising but hungry for investment). This is **related diversification done deliberately**.

> "There's a line in Hindi — *ek lakdi se chulha nahi jalta* (one grain can't break the oven / one log alone can't light the stove)."

On their own, each category would carry its own cost base; together they split the bill. **One salesforce reaches 6.28 million outlets** and carries all three categories down the same road. **One R&D team** works on fragrance and active ingredients that end up in soap, hair color, and insecticides. And the **Godrej name itself** — every new launch borrows trust it never had to pay for. This is what it looks like when the corporate parent actually earns its keep.

### Vertical Integration: Selective vs. Deep

GCPL follows **selective/quasi backward integration** — sourcing palm oil through Godrej Agrovet, investing heavily in in-house R&D and formulation, but using a wide **external retail network** rather than owning stores. Dabur follows **deeper backward integration**, especially in **medicinal herbs and food ingredients**, reducing supply risk on highly specialized inputs. Both create value through vertical integration — GCPL focuses on manufacturing capability and innovation; Dabur focuses on securing critical raw material.

### Internationalization: Buy vs. Build

> "An African proverb fits almost too well: if you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together. GCPL decided to go far, and it went by buying its way there."

GCPL **didn't** try to sell Godrej soap in Nigeria — it **bought Tura**. It didn't build a hair brand from scratch in Africa — it **bought Darling**, already the number-one hair brand on the continent. On the AAA framework, GCPL is **Adaptation first** (buy the local brand, the local name, respect local hair and skin), **Arbitrage second** (Indian manufacturing and R&D serving markets abroad), with **Aggregation barely featuring** beyond backend systems.

> "GCPL doesn't globalize its brand — it localizes its ownership."

For GCPL, **39 percent of revenue is international**; for Dabur, **roughly 26.5 percent** — but the percentage hides the philosophy. GCPL used **12-plus acquisitions**, buying shelf space, brand, and customer relationships. Dabur used **three major acquisitions** plus greenfield plants and exports — **slower, cheaper, far more organic**. On the AAA row: GCPL is **Adaptation plus Arbitrage**; Dabur is **Adaptation plus Aggregation**, because "the Dabur name means the same thing in Dubai as it does in Delhi." GCPL adapts by buying the local identity; Dabur adapts by **exporting the Indian identity**.

### Recent Moves (Last 3 Years)

| Move | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Raymond Consumer Care acquisition** | Roughly 3,000 crore, brands like Park Avenue and Kamasutra — horizontal push into premium male grooming |
| **Pet care entry** | New category, 500 crore committed over a 5-year period in a market compounding at 20 percent |
| **Must Mustache acquisition** | A digital-native grooming brand, bought not for revenue but for D2C capability GCPL was lacking |
| **Latin America exit** | Walked away from Argentina and Chile (entered 2010) due to macroeconomic volatility and forex risk |

> "Good corporate strategy isn't just the discipline to add — it is also the discipline to walk away."

### Final Scorecard and Recommendations

Diversification: GCPL 7,406 vs. Dabur 3,174 (broader portfolio). Vertical integration: 54 vs. 47 (Dabur goes deeper exactly where it has to). Internationalization: GCPL wins. Corporate group advantage: GCPL wins on sheer breadth of what the group gives.

**Recommendations if sitting across from GCPL's CEO:**
1. **Horizontal scope** — build healthcare/OTC into a fourth pillar; Dabur earns roughly 4,800 crore there, GCPL earns nothing.
2. **Vertical backward** integration.
3. **Vertical forward** integration.
4. **Geographic** — make South and Southeast Asia (Bangladesh, Nepal, Vietnam) a formal fourth cluster, and this time **build, don't just buy**.
5. **Synergy** — tighten it across the portfolio.

> "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — the world is one family. That's more or less what GCPL has spent 20 years building: a family of local brands held together by one Indian parent. Dabur went the other way — one Indian identity carried out into the world. Neither is wrong. But only one of them is earning 39 percent of its revenue abroad."

### Q&A Highlights

- **Is there a real advantage to being part of the Godrej group, or should GCPL be treated as standalone?** The group's answer: strong brand equity and financial backing from the group; the better-off test shows it does add value, creating belongingness and a strong parent-brand impact.
- **What organizational structure supports GCPL's mix of segments, categories, and geographies?** The professor helped sharpen the question — how does GCPL balance **functional, geographical, and product/divisional dimensions**, and manage the tension between **centralized R&D in India** and **localized brands abroad** (aggregation vs. adaptation)? The group was honest that detailed organizational-structure data isn't easily available from public sources — the professor accepted this, noting that without a published case specifically covering that, the data is genuinely hard to get.

## Group 6: HUL vs. Nestle India — Diversification and Shared Capability vs. Focused Specialization

### The Setup

Both are subsidiaries of global FMCG parents, operating in the same market, yet with very different corporate strategies. The analysis focuses on the same three dimensions: diversification, vertical integration, internationalization.

| Metric | HUL | Nestle India |
|---|---|---|
| **FY25 revenue** | Roughly 61,500 crore | Roughly 20,200 crore (HUL is ~3x larger) |
| **Business segments** | Five (home care, beauty and personal care, food, etc.) | One (food) |
| **Retail outlets reached** | 9 million | 5.1 million |
| **Ad spend (% of revenue)** | 9.8 percent | 4.8 percent |
| **Herfindahl index** | 7,304 | 0 (single reported segment) |

**The most important difference isn't size — it's business structure.** HUL's broad portfolio gives it the opportunity to share resources across businesses; Nestle focuses on specialization.

### Economies of Scope vs. Specialization

HUL creates economies of scope because the **same distribution network, salesforce, and marketing platform** support multiple categories — the same retailer who sells Surf Excel can also sell Dove or Lakme. Nestle's approach is different: all its products belong to the food industry, letting it **share manufacturing facilities, procurement systems, and R&D** deeply within one industry. **HUL gains business diversification; Nestle gains from specialization.**

HUL's strongest capability is its **9 million-outlet distribution network** supported by a digital platform, plus far higher ad investment (9.8 percent vs. 4.8 percent) backing its brand portfolio. Nestle's advantage comes from **deep manufacturing expertise, direct milk sourcing, and access to global R&D**. As a pattern: HUL's resources create value across several industries; Nestle's capability strengthens leadership within one.

Nestle scores **zero on the Herfindahl index not because it lacks products, but because it reports only one business segment** under accounting standards. HUL's businesses share manufacturing, procurement, marketing, and distribution resources — so HUL follows **related diversification**, creating economies of scope while maintaining strategic focus.

### The GSK Consumer Healthcare Acquisition: A Case Study in the Better-Off Test

HUL's acquisition pattern shows a clear strategic logic: it enters businesses where existing capabilities (distribution, branding, marketing) add value. The headline example: the **31,700 crore acquisition of GSK Consumer Healthcare**, bringing brands like **Horlicks and Boost** into HUL's portfolio. At the same time, HUL has **exited businesses that don't fit its strategy** — showing disciplined capital allocation rather than uncontrolled expansion.

Applying the **better-off and ownership tests** to the GSK deal:
- **Better-off test: clearly passes.** Horlicks and Boost benefited from HUL's nationwide distribution and healthcare/marketing muscle.
- **Ownership test: not yet fully proven.** The health-food-drinks segment currently delivers **lower return on assets** than HUL's other segments — the operational synergies are evident, but stakeholder value creation will need more time to materialize.

### Vertical Integration: A Moderate, Asset-Light Model

| Metric | HUL | Nestle India |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-added/sales, FY23** | 47.3 percent | -- |
| **Value-added/sales, FY24** | 51.5 percent | -- |
| **Value-added/sales, FY25** | 50.7 percent (slight decline) | Consistently 52 to 57 percent |

HUL follows a **moderate level of vertical integration**; Nestle relies more on internal (in-house) manufacturing. HUL's approach is supported by **27 factories** including contract manufacturing units, plus a strong supplier network for raw material sourcing. This **asset-light strategy** offers lower capital investment, greater manufacturing flexibility, and faster capacity expansion — but it also increases supply dependency, reduces operational control, and creates potential supply-chain risk. Overall, HUL balances cost efficiency, flexibility, and operational resilience, letting it respond quickly to changing market demand.

### Internationalization: Adaptation Through Subsidiaries, Not Direct Export

HUL's **standalone business generates only 1.2 percent of revenue from exports**. On a **consolidated basis**, overseas revenue rises to **4.1 percent in FY25** — slightly higher than Nestle India's **3.9 percent**. This indicates HUL's international revenue is driven mainly through overseas subsidiaries like **Unilever India Exports Limited**, rather than direct export. HUL follows an **adaptation strategy**, customizing products to local consumer preference rather than a standardized global approach, and has expanded internationally through subsidiaries and acquisitions (including GSK Consumer Healthcare). Nestle India follows a **relatively more export-oriented model**, though both still generate most revenue from the Indian market.

### Recent Strategic Moves and Recommendations

HUL has focused on **premiumization, capability acquisitions, and portfolio optimization** — for example, acquiring **Minimalist and Oza (D2C/premium brands)** and **separating (demerging) its ice cream business, Kwality Wall's**. Nestle, in contrast, has invested in **manufacturing expansion, premium products, and adjacent food categories**. This reflects HUL strengthening its portfolio across categories, while Nestle deepens its competitive position within food.

**Recommendations:**
1. **Continue related diversification** while exiting businesses with limited strategic fit.
2. **Maintain the asset-light manufacturing model**, but selectively increase integration where premium products require greater quality control.
3. **Expand exports through existing export platforms** rather than investing heavily in overseas manufacturing.
4. **Greater sharing of digital capabilities, R&D, procurement, and consumer insight** to further strengthen corporate advantage.

> "HUL creates value through diversification and shared capabilities across multiple businesses; Nestle creates value through specialization, manufacturing excellence, and deep expertise within the food industry. Neither approach is universally better — each aligns with the company's own resources, capabilities, and long-term strategic objectives."

### Q&A Highlights

- **Why is HUL demerging its ice cream business (Kwality Wall's) and selling off Pureit, while also acquiring Minimalist and Oza?** This is a **strategic-fit test**. Ice cream requires a specialized, dedicated cold chain and an entirely different distribution channel — low FMCG synergy with the rest of HUL's business. Pureit is a durable-goods business, also with low FMCG synergy. Minimalist and Oza, by contrast, perfectly fit HUL's synergy logic — capturing the high-growth, digital-first, premium personal-care segment.

## Group 8: Apollo Hospitals vs. Max Healthcare — The Vertical Integration Paradox

### The Setup

Two giants of Indian healthcare: **Apollo** (focal firm) is divided into three segments — **healthcare services, digital health and pharmacy, and diagnostics and retail health** — totaling roughly **25,229 crore** in revenue. **Max Healthcare** reports only **one business segment** (hospitals), at roughly **10,538 crore** revenue, with **16 percent revenue growth**, **14 percent EBITDA margin**, and **34 percent PAT growth**.

Same industry, same country — **opposite answers to the scope question**. Apollo is much more diversified; Max is not.

### The Corporate Advantage Triangle

**Apollo's key resources:** its **brand value**, which Apollo itself says "transcends healthcare delivery formats" — allowing it to translate trust into pharmacy, diagnostics, and other businesses. Apollo brought the **first MRI and CT scan machines to India**, and the **first proton therapy in South/Southeast Asia**. It also **owns two medical colleges and 13 nursing colleges**, securing a steady talent supply instead of depending on an erratic professional pipeline. Its scope includes the **Apollo 24/7 app** (47 million registered users), three reported segments covering the **full patient journey**, and **57 percent of beds in metro cities**. Organizationally, Apollo is **promoter-led** — Dr. Reddy and his three daughters head the overall business and its different segments.

**Max's key resources:** a string of good acquisitions — **BLK, Nanavati, Saroj, Alexis** — brought to the group's profitability. Max is focused mostly on **premium metro cities**, with an **ARPOB (average revenue per operating bed) of 78,000**, actually **higher than Apollo's**. Its only reporting segment is hospitals; adjacent businesses are limited to **Max Care (home care)** and diagnostics.

### Diversification: Two Ways to Measure It

Mapping both companies onto the **patient journey** (prevention, diagnosis, treatment, pharmacy, chronic care, home care): **Apollo is present at every single stage**; **Max is concentrated almost entirely at treatment**, with two small extensions (Max Lab in diagnostics, Max at Home) together under 5 percent of revenue.

| Metric | Apollo | Max |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue share of largest segment** | 49.8 percent (no single segment dominates) | 95.6 percent (essentially one business) |
| **Herfindahl index (reported segments)** | 5,633 | 0 (falls below 10 percent disclosure threshold) |
| **Herfindahl index (business-line basis)** | -- | Roughly 851 (about a seventh of Apollo's) |

**Neither firm is a conglomerate and neither is a business group.** Apollo monetizes the patient journey; Max monetizes the hospital bed.

### The Better-Off Test: Apollo Pharmacy Deep Dive

The group went one step further — not just how diversified the firm is, but whether a **specific business** is actually better off for belonging. They picked **Apollo Pharmacy**, which gets four things from membership:

1. **Brand transfer** at near-zero marginal cost.
2. **Patient flow** — discharge prescriptions from Apollo's own ~29,000 annual inpatients.
3. **Procurement scale** across **7,289 stores**, pushing private label to **15.2 percent of sales**.
4. **Data** — 47 million registered users on Apollo 24/7 driving roughly **51,600 medicine orders a day**.

**The evidence it works:** offline pharmacy revenue is **9,528 crore**, margin is only **7.7 percent**, but **return on capital employed is 57.3 percent** — that's the number that matters. Judged on margin alone, pharmacy looks weak next to hospitals' 24.4 percent margin — but **it's actually a capital-turn business, not a margin business**. On that basis, **the better-off test passes decisively.**

### Vertical Integration: A Genuine Paradox

| Metric | Apollo | Max |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-added/sales ratio** | 48.1 percent | 75 percent |

By the numbers, **Max looks far more vertically integrated than Apollo**. But look at what each firm actually does: **neither owns much of anything.** Apollo is **backward integrated into medical and nursing colleges** (a thin, specific clinical-talent market) and into **Keimed**, its pharmaceutical distributor. Max mostly **contracts three hospitals on 30-to-45-year operations-and-management (O&M) agreements**, plus partner-built/leased assets, rather than owning them.

> "Why does a firm which owns more of the chain score lower? Because Apollo Pharmacy resells 8,557 crore of stock-in-trade every year — high-turnover trading mechanically depresses value-added as a share of sales. The ratio measures manufacturing depth, not ownership breadth."

**Neither firm integrates for its own sake — both own only where a contract would genuinely fail.**

### Internationalization: Exporting Talent, Not Assets

Neither firm owns a hospital abroad. Apollo runs **management contracts and franchises in Bangladesh and Dubai** — about **6 percent of inpatient revenue** comes from international patients. Max has **no overseas facilities at all**, just **15 patient-assistance centers across 14 countries** — international patients are a **larger share for Max (9 percent of hospital revenue)** than for Apollo.

Both exploit **arbitrage**: India treats a heart bypass at roughly **one-tenth of US cost**, so the patient travels to the low-cost location — neither firm exports the asset. Apollo has **one extra channel Max doesn't**: it **exports talent** — over **4,000 nurses deployed to the UK, Middle East, and Germany**, plus **300-plus radiographers into the NHS** — the colleges Apollo built to solve its own staffing problem now export a business of their own.

### Recent Strategy: Narrowing vs. Deepening

**Apollo is narrowing:** its board approved a composite scheme (June, prior year) demerging **pharmacy distribution, Apollo 24/7, and telehealth into a new listed entity alongside Keimed** (India's largest pharma wholesaler, connecting drug manufacturers to over 70,000 distributors worldwide) — targeting a listing in **FY27 Q4**. Apollo Health & Lifestyle's maternity/fertility business is combining with **Cloudnine**; insurance was already sold to **HDFC Ergo** a year earlier.

**Max is deepening:** it degrouped from Max India in 2020 and went on to acquire **Saroj (Lucknow)** and **Alexis (Nagpur)**. **Apollo is unbundling; Max is buying more of the one thing it's in — hospitals.**

### The Three Corporate Advantage Tests

1. **Does membership add value?** Apollo Pharmacy earns 57.3 percent ROC; Max's acquisitions get lifted onto the network's margins.
2. **Does value-added exceed the cost of ownership?** Max **passes cleanly at 26.2 percent operating margin**.
3. **Is the firm the best possible parent for each business?** Max **plausibly passes**; Apollo's answer is **partial, and it's actively fixing it** — insurance sold, maternity combined into Cloudnine, pharmacy/digital about to be spun off.

> "Corporate advantage comes from fit, not from scope."

### Recommendations to Apollo's CEO

- **Horizontally: scope narrower** — complete the new-co separation on schedule, apply the same logic to any remaining subscale AHL (Apollo Health & Lifestyle) format.
- **Vertically: hold, with one extension** — the current integration is correct, but extend backward integration further only in **clinical intent**.
- **Geographically: widen but contractually** — scale management contracts and franchises in the Gulf and East, where the brand travels but capital shouldn't.

### Q&A Highlights (the richest, most professor-driven Q&A of the session)

- **What was the "digital cash loss" of 163 crore mentioned in one slide, and how does that square with claiming a better-off pass?** The group explained: when segments acted as one company, overall profitability averaged out — healthcare services runs a **24 percent EBITDA margin**, while digital health and pharmacy runs just **4.5 percent**. The **weak digital-health leg dragged the blended average down**, which is exactly why the segments were **demerged** — so each business can be assessed and managed on its own.
- **What does "digital health" actually cover?** The Apollo 24/7 app, offering **online consultation / telemedicine** with Apollo's own doctors.
- **The professor's own diagnosis:** "My belief is that the cost of customer acquisition would be higher compared to the value capture — that is the reason why they got the negative margin there." The group confirmed this was exactly right.
- **Wouldn't separating medical stores from the digital part make the stores far more profitable?** The group clarified: medical stores are also **asset-heavy**, so their margin is thin too — that's exactly why the two were clubbed, and why the whole 24/7-plus-pharmacy business is now being unbundled into Keimed (whose competency this actually is).
- **The professor pushed back on a common student mistake:** "Just because there's a negative margin or a loss, that is not the reason they should separate it — it may just be a different kind of business. My hunch is that they want to scale up the hospital business more because they see scale advantage there." Confirmed by the group: Apollo's annual report shows plans to add **2,500 more beds**.
- **The professor's own hypothesis on why spin-offs happen here:** "Private equity firms are more interested in investing in the pure hospital business, not the other businesses. My hunch is that in the near future one of the PE firms will invest into this separated part of the business... spin-off is required because you can value each of the businesses properly."
- **Closing compliment:** "I'm very happy with your explanation of why that vertical integration number is mysterious" — the professor singled out the Apollo/Max ratio paradox (Max scoring higher despite owning less) as the highlight insight of the presentation.

## Group 11: Bajaj Auto vs. TVS Motor — "The Executive War Room"

### The Framing

Titled "the executive war room," this presentation compares **Bajaj Auto** and **TVS Motor Company** across diversification, vertical integration, and internationalization for FY 2025-26. **Bajaj dominates profit margins and global scale through its KTM partnership; TVS is winning the EV race by building high-tech capability through serial acquisitions like Norton.**

### The Numbers at a Glance

| Metric | Bajaj Auto | TVS Motor |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | 65,870 crore | 16 percent lower than Bajaj |
| **PAT (net profit)** | 10,744 crore | Roughly 3.5 times smaller |
| **EBITDA margin** | 20.5 percent | 12.9 percent |
| **Herfindahl index** | Roughly 6,000 (both firms score similarly — "constrained related diversifiers") | Roughly 6,000 |
| **EV market share (E-scooters)** | 25 percent | 34 percent (TVS leads the future) |

> "In terms of net profit, Bajaj generates nearly 33.5 times that of TVS... in short, Bajaj is currently the industry's cash machine while TVS is the quality and EV leader."

Both companies "stick to what they know — vehicles" — sharing factories and research teams lean across brands to save costs.

### Industry Context: Four Reshaping Trends

1. **Premiumization** — market shift toward motorcycles larger than 200cc and high-margin lifestyle brands.
2. **Electrification (EV)** — rapid adoption in the scooter segment, with legacy incumbents reclaiming share from startups.
3. **Export dominance** — India is now the primary global manufacturing hub for Africa, Latin America, and ASEAN markets.
4. **Captive finance** — in-house credit arms (Bajaj Auto Credit Limited) create closed-loop demand cycles.

**Company histories:** Bajaj was founded in 1945, pivoted to the **Pulsar in 2001**, and achieved **sole control of KTM in 2025** with an approximately 800 million dollar (roughly 7,765 crore) investment, focused on three-wheelers, premium bikes, and exports. TVS, with roots to 1911, won the **Deming Prize for quality in 2002** and is on an M&A wave acquiring **Norton and Swiss E-Mobility Group (SEMG)** to build brand, mobility, and EV capability.

### Parenting Advantage: Scale-and-Cash Machine vs. Capability System

**Bajaj is the "scale and cash machine"** — low-cost manufacturing combined with **18,137 crore in surplus funds**; its one big bet was taking control of KTM to gain a premium global brand and advanced technology. **TVS is the "capability system"** — strong manufacturing standards plus multiple acquisitions (Norton, SEMG, Ison) to add specialized skills. **Bajaj wins through scale and financial strength; TVS wins through shared expertise and capabilities.**

### Horizontal and Vertical Scope

Both firms have similar mathematical diversity, but different market skew: **Bajaj's revenue skews 57 percent to motorcycles/premium bikes and 20 percent to commercial/three-wheelers**; **TVS is more evenly split — 46 percent motorcycles, 41 percent scooters.**

| Metric | Bajaj | TVS |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-added/sales ratio** | 30 to 33 percent | 24 to 27 percent |
| **Vertical integration style** | Deeper in-house — retains control over **design, engines, final assembly**; integrates forward through PACL, Bajaj Auto Credit, and Chetak stores | Leans more on an external supplier ecosystem, but more aggressive **downstream integration** via TVS Credit and DriveX |

### AAA Framework and Internationalization

- **Adaptation:** TVS adapts its Western/European test through Norton and local ASEAN models.
- **Aggregation:** Bajaj uses a **single Indian hub to serve over 100 countries**, leveraging the KTM global network for scale.
- **Arbitrage:** Both use Indian cost and labor advantage — Bajaj is the export leader, with **34 percent of turnover from international markets**.

### Parenting Advantage in Practice

A parent company must prove it makes its subsidiaries better off. **Bajaj** uses its cash reserves to protect the **KTM and Bajaj mobility supply chain**, and launched **BSA**, which became profitable in its first year by feeding off the parent's dealer network. **TVS** provides **patient capital and manufacturing discipline** to a distressed asset like **Norton**, and uses **SEMG** to gain EV retail access in the EU.

### SWOT and Synthesis

**Bajaj:** strength = fortress balance sheet and three-wheeler monopoly; weakness = currently lagging in EV market share. **TVS:** strength = quality engineering and EV leadership; threat = execution risk of managing multiple disparate European acquisitions.

Four key takeaways: **synergy is tangible** (Bajaj's 20.5 percent margin proves shared platforms/R&D cut costs); **transaction-cost logic** (both firms integrate only when contracts fail — Bajaj bought KTM to avoid supply-chain holdup risk); **diverging global paths** (Bajaj scales via aggregation from one hub, TVS scales via adaptation with local Western capabilities); **the EV collision** (both firms racing for premium EV leadership, paths now colliding).

### Recommendations to Bajaj's CEO

- **Horizontally** — win back EV share by extending into e-motorcycles using KTM's technology.
- **Vertically** — integrate into production-linked-incentive (PLI) government manufacturing schemes to de-risk the supply chain.
- **Geographically** — use KTM as the global distribution engine for Indian-manufactured premium EVs.
- **Synergy** — create a formal group organization for EV architecture so synergy is actively measured.

> "Within a single industry, Bajaj has built a scale-and-cash aggregation machine while TVS has built a capability-driven adaptation system. Electrification is their final frontier."

### Q&A Highlights (the liveliest, most debated Q&A of the session)

- **What is "KD" mentioned on the adaptation-strategy slide?** KD stands for **knockdown / complete knockdown** — a Bajaj strategy where, instead of shipping a fully assembled bike, Bajaj **manufactures parts in Pune, packs them in boxes, and ships them to Africa**, where they're **assembled locally**. This is cheaper to ship and attracts **lower tariffs**, and is a big reason Bajaj gained so much ground in the African market — with good margins.
- **Is the KD strategy sustainable long term, given that other Indian companies use it too and governments may crack down?** A student's answer: "Bajaj was the first to implement it, and by the time others started adopting it, Bajaj had already gained a lot of market in Africa" — its edge was **quality plus fuel efficiency**, beating both Chinese and Japanese competitors who competed mostly on price. Tariff structures in African and Asian markets supported the strategy; notably, it **did not work in Brazil**, where Bajaj opened an assembly line that didn't pan out.
- **Does KD survive the shift to EVs?** A student flagged real doubt: "Bajaj itself is lagging in EV at home, so I'm not very sure it can capture the African market very well" with the same playbook — it was designed for internal-combustion bikes. The professor added that **China is way ahead in EV and very strongly present in Africa already**, making this transition genuinely uncertain.
- **A live class debate broke out** over whether Bajaj or TVS leads in EV two-wheelers — students corrected each other in real time (initial confusion between "overall two-wheeler exports," where Bajaj leads, and "EV specifically," where **TVS leads**) until the professor had them go back to the actual slide to settle it: **Bajaj leads in overall export/IC business; TVS leads in EV.**
- **A macro aside:** Bajaj faced one prior period of trouble due to macroeconomic volatility in African markets (a big hit for a major exporter there) but later revived — "EV remains a question," a student summarized.

## Group 14: Asian Paints vs. Berger Paints — Big-and-Digging-Backward vs. Small-and-Cheap

### The Central Question

Does being part of the bigger **Asian Paints group** actually make its core paint business a stronger player? All data used is from 2025-2026, none older than 2023.

### Four Headline Findings

1. The group **does help** the core paint business through shared dealers, shared brand, and shared research — roughly **87 percent of Asian Paints' business runs through homes** via this shared engine.
2. The **home-decor business does not pay its way** — kitchens, bath, lighting, and windows are just **2.6 percent of sales even after 10 years of ownership**.
3. **Going backward into raw materials** is now a bigger help than adding new products.
4. **Berger runs a smaller, cheaper business — and it is winning market share.**

### The Numbers at a Glance

| Metric | Asian Paints | Berger Paints |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue (this year)** | Roughly 35,500 crore (up 5 percent) | Roughly 11,900 crore (Asian Paints is roughly 3x bigger) |
| **Herfindahl index** | Roughly 2,363 | Roughly 2,157 (spread not very different) |
| **Vertical integration (value-added/sales)** | 43.7 percent (rising) | -- |
| **Gross margin (last quarter)** | 44.8 percent | 42.3 percent (on the same raw materials) |
| **International revenue share** | 9.4 percent, across 13 countries with different local brands | Smaller, mainly Nepal and Poland |

Both firms earn **almost all their profit from the same business** — selling paint in India through dealers. The real question: does Asian Paints' extra size buy the paint business anything Berger cannot get?

### Industry Disruption: The Backdrop That Matters

For years the paint market was calm, held by a few big firms, prices stable. Then two things shook it up: **Birla Group put 10,000 crore into a brand-new paint business, Birla Opus**, entering with several large plants and thousands of dealers almost at once; and **JSW Paints bought Akzo Nobel India**, creating a new number-four player overnight. Because of Birla Opus, **Asian Paints' share of decorative paint sales fell from about 59 percent to about 52 percent in just one year**. On top of this, the **Competition Commission of India started a review of dealer-based paint sales** after a complaint from the Birla side. **The old playbook of low price and wide dealer reach is no longer safe on its own.**

### The Better-Off Test: A Clear Pass for Decorative Paint

The group shares **one large dealer network** — the same shop counter sells paint, waterproofing, and even bath fittings, so adding a new product costs very little. It also shares **one research team, one brand, one design program**, and a **full painting service reaching over 650 towns** that turns a simple paint sale into a finished job. All of this makes the business harder for a rival to beat — **the better-off test passes for decorative paints.**

### The Ownership Test: A Fail for Home Decor

**Passing the better-off test is not enough — the business also has to be worth owning.** Home decor (kitchen, bath, lighting, windows) has been fully owned for **almost 10 years** but contributes only **2.6 percent of total revenue**. Compare this to Asian Paints' **industrial paint business — a 50/50 partnership with PPG** that grows by double digits and **costs Asian Paints nothing**.

> **Formula for the exam: decor may fit strategically, but it should not be fully owned — a partnership would have been the smarter choice.**

### Vertical Integration: Digging Into Raw Materials

Instead of adding more products, Asian Paints is going **backward into making its own raw materials**. Its **value-added/sales ratio is about 43.7 percent and still rising**; last quarter's gross margin was **44.8 percent vs. Berger's 42.3 percent** on the same inputs. Asian Paints is building its own plant for a **key chemical at Dahej**, and has started a **white cement plant in the Middle East**. The goal: make its paint **cheaper to produce than any price-cutting rival can manage.**

### Internationalization: Local Brands, Not Linked Markets

Overseas sales are **9.4 percent of total** for Asian Paints, spread across **13 countries with different local brands per market** — strong in each market individually, but the markets aren't linked together well, and 9 percent is too small to offset the share lost at home. The interesting new move: the **Middle East white-cement plant uses cheaper local materials to feed the Indian business.** Berger is much smaller abroad (mainly Nepal and Poland) with no equivalent move.

### Putting It Together

On **product range and cost control, Berger is better placed** — leaner, cheaper, gaining share. On **raw materials and global reach, Asian Paints is far ahead.** Both use partnerships well. This year, **Berger gained share but profit dipped slightly; Asian Paints lost share but grew profit.**

### Recommendations to Asian Paints' CEO

1. **Stop fully owning the weak decor business** — keep only what sells through the paint counter, turn the rest into partnerships.
2. **Push harder on raw materials** so the firm becomes the cheapest-to-supply player.
3. **Grow the overseas business**, using the Middle East plant as an export base.
4. **Make the painting service the one thing rivals cannot copy** — treat it as a real business, not just marketing.

> **The simple test behind all four: for every business, is it worth more inside the group than the cost of owning it?**

> "The strength of Asian Paints sits in its core paint business, and its future edge must come from its supply chain — not from adding more products."

### Q&A Highlights (an extended, professor-driven strategic discussion)

- **How can Asian Paints stay profitable in its core business without diversifying into decorative solutions, given Birla's aggressive entry?** A student clarified that decorative-solution services (digital home-visualization tools) **are relatable to the core business, not unrelated diversification** — closer to vertical integration, since Asian Paints already has the paint business and is simply helping customers curate/decorate around it, adding value like matching craftsmen to apply the design.
- **The professor asked the group directly for its own stand** on whether Asian Paints should stay in core paint or venture into supporting businesses. The group's answer: Asian Paints has **clearly shown intent to move into adjacencies** (home decor, adhesives) because it wants to **capture the entire ecosystem** — painters, plumbers, interior designers, engineers — even before a single bucket of paint is sold. The Middle East plant is direct evidence of this vertical-integration intent. By contrast, **Berger is "all over the place"** — making coatings for "One Bharat," coatings for nuclear power plants, and acquiring Saroj (2019) — without having decided where it ultimately wants to go, whereas Asian Paints has already decided and is **a decade into execution.**
- **The professor's own bet:** "My bet is that Berger will get acquired at some point in time... most likely it should be JSW" (which has already acquired Dulux/Akzo Nobel). "There will be three fiercely competitive players" eventually.
- **Is the unprofitable home-decor segment actually a deliberate long-game — invest now, dominate later since no competitor has that category?** The professor's response was more cautious/nuanced: home decor **was** a very profitable business for Asian Paints due to its own dominance, but the **market structure is now eroding** because of Birla's aggressive entry and JSW's acquisition — turning a once-comfortable space into a genuinely competitive one. His prescription: Asian Paints needs **both an aggressive competitive counter-response and diversification away** (new businesses or new export markets) to preserve overall corporate profitability. His long-term hunch: **Asian Paints' market share will eventually stabilize around 40 to 45 percent**, and could fall further if Berger gets acquired.
- **Could geopolitical volatility and oil-price swings erode this sector's attractiveness?** The professor agreed it **could and should** in the short run, but held that the **long-term trend for oil is downward** — so if oil corrects meaningfully, that's beneficial for the paints business in the short run, and shouldn't be a long-run problem regardless of near-term geopolitical noise.

## Group 18: Crompton Greaves Consumer Electricals vs. Havells India — Focused Challenger vs. Diversified Incumbent

*(The final presentation of the day.)*

### The Setup

Comparing India's electrical/consumer-durable companies through the lens of **"focused challenger vs. diversified incumbent."** **Crompton** is the focal firm — a focused player in fans, pumps, small appliances, lighting, and kitchen appliances (via subsidiary **Butterfly**). **Havells** is roughly three times Crompton's size, spanning six segments including **Lloyd air conditioners**.

### The Numbers at a Glance

| Metric | Crompton | Havells |
|---|---|---|
| **FY26 revenue** | Roughly 8,900 crore (up 2.9 percent) | Roughly 22,500 crore (up 3.5 percent) |
| **Net result** | Net **loss** of about 231 crore (headline) | Profit grew 14.5 percent (partly helped by a 283 crore gain on its stake in Goldi Solar) |
| **Herfindahl index** | Roughly 446 | Roughly 7,600 (nearly double, more diversified) |
| **Vertical integration (value-added/sales, using revenue minus COGS)** | 31.9 percent | 33 percent (minimal difference) |
| **Segments** | Three (ECD alone is 75 percent of the mix — real concentration risk) | Six (a genuine mix of infrastructure-linked business like cables/switchgear and consumer products like ECD/Lloyd) |

Crompton is pushing into two new bets: **wires (under a new "Crompton Armor" brand) and solar.**

### Diversification: A Reminder That It's Not a Perfect Hedge

By the Herfindahl index, **Havells is clearly more diversified**. But FY26 results are a good reminder that **diversification alone is not a perfect hedge**: Havells' **cable business and Lloyd (AC) moved in exactly opposite directions** — Lloyd saw a huge loss, while cables performed well and compensated for it.

### Vertical Integration: Trading Model vs. Manufacturing Depth

Crompton runs a **leaner, more outsourced model** — its purchased/traded-goods cost is **2.4 times its raw-material spend**, meaning it's less a manufacturer and more a trading-and-assembly operation. Havells **owns far more of its manufacturing** for cables, switchgear, and lighting components, and is notably **debt-heavy**, having funded capex that roughly doubled in FY26. Interestingly, the ownership advantage shows up more in **margin stability than in higher margins.**

### Internationalization: Neither Is Export-Driven

Neither company is meaningfully export-driven today. Crompton's growth bets (wires, solar) are domestic, not new geographies. Havells has some **residual international know-how** from its former **Sylvania lighting business**, giving it more headroom to go global if it chooses — but for now, this is a **domestic growth story for both.**

### Pulling It Together

Havells is **larger, more diversified, and slightly more vertically integrated**, with cables driving structural growth. Crompton is **smaller and more concentrated**, but its **underlying FY26 performance was actually resilient — pre-exceptional profit of 507 crore, up nearly 30 percent**. The headline loss came from **one acquisition write-down, not operational weakness.** Important nuance: concentrated bets carry their own risk, but so does relying on a segment like Lloyd within an otherwise diversified portfolio.

On the **AAA framework**: both companies score high on **Adaptation** (deeply localized for India); Havells has a slight edge on **Aggregation** through its six-segment shared platform and the new Goldi Solar stake; **neither does much Arbitrage** — both are overwhelmingly domestic.

### Parenting and Corporate Advantage

**Crompton** is independently, professionally managed, with a **narrower but focused advantage**. **Havells** is **promoter-led by the Gupta family**, with **long-term capital allocation** across a genuinely synergetic six-segment portfolio — "a clear case of real corporate advantage." The group's framing: **Crompton's job is to diversify and de-risk; Havells' job is to sustain cable momentum while fixing Lloyd's structural profitability.**

### Recommendations to Crompton's CEO (closing the loop on all three scope dimensions plus synergy and AAA)

- **Portfolio scope** — diversify beyond ECD by scaling wires and solar; deepen vertical integration selectively to close the one-point gap with Havells.
- **Synergy** — extend Butterfly's shared distribution, service, and R&D platform to the new wires and solar businesses so they ramp up faster and cheaper.
- **Geographic scope** — pilot low-risk exports of fans, appliances, and lighting to South Asia and Africa **through distributor alliances, not acquisition.**
- **AAA** — raise Aggregation with one shared "Crompton" go-to-market model, and build real Arbitrage by turning India's manufacturing cost edge into an export engine.
- **Timeline:** near-term = shared-platform extension and export pilot; medium-term = backward integration and the "One Crompton" rollout.

### Q&A Highlights

This presentation drew **no questions** — the professor noted good time management and moved to close the class.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Better-Off Test** | Whether a business unit is genuinely stronger for being part of the parent — GCPL/Godrej, HUL/GSK, Apollo Pharmacy, and Asian Paints' decorative-paint business all passed it this session |
| **Ownership Test** | Whether the parent still needs to *own* the business to deliver that benefit — Asian Paints' home decor and Apollo's digital/pharmacy segment both showed partial or failing results, triggering restructuring |
| **Herfindahl Index** | A diversification score measuring revenue evenness across segments — can be zero simply due to accounting disclosure thresholds (Nestle India, Max Healthcare) rather than a true lack of diversity |
| **Value-Added-to-Sales Ratio** | The standard vertical-integration metric — this session's key lesson (from Apollo vs. Max) is that a **high** ratio can mean **less** manufacturing depth if it's driven by low resale/trading volume rather than ownership |
| **Vertical Integration Paradox** | When a firm with less actual asset ownership (Max, via O&M contracts) scores a *higher* value-added ratio than a firm with more owned infrastructure (Apollo), because high-turnover trading (like Apollo Pharmacy's stock resale) mechanically depresses the ratio |
| **Knockdown (KD) Strategy** | Bajaj Auto's export approach: manufacture parts in India, ship them unassembled (lower shipping cost, lower tariffs), and assemble locally in the destination market (Africa) |
| **AAA Framework** | Adaptation (local responsiveness), Aggregation (global scale/standardization), Arbitrage (cost/market exploitation) — used to classify every company's internationalization style this session |
| **Corporate Advantage Formula** | Portfolio times Capabilities times Organization — the standard three-part lens for judging whether a diversified structure creates real value |
| **Spin-off / Demerger Logic** | Separating a business so each piece can be valued and managed properly — explicitly used to explain Apollo's pharmacy/digital demerger and HUL's Kwality Wall's demerger |
| **Return on Capital Employed (ROC) vs. Margin** | A business can look weak on margin (Apollo Pharmacy's 7.7 percent) but be a genuinely strong capital-turn business on ROC (57.3 percent) — margin alone can mislead |
| **Asset-Light Strategy** | Relying on external manufacturing/contract partners instead of owning factories — lowers capital investment and increases flexibility, but raises supply dependency and reduces control (HUL, Crompton) |
| **Operations and Management (O&M) Agreements** | Long-term contracts (30-45 years) where a company runs a hospital or facility it does not own — Max Healthcare's primary growth vehicle, versus Apollo's mix of owned and backward-integrated assets |
| **Holdup Problem / Transaction Cost Logic** | Backward integration only where the market for an input is unreliable or could hold the firm hostage on price/supply — Bajaj buying KTM to avoid supply-chain holdup, Asian Paints backward-integrating into raw-material plants |
| **Business-Group Parenting Advantage** | Whether belonging to a larger group delivers real economic benefit beyond just size — Godrej Agrovet/Properties for GCPL, KTM/BSA for Bajaj, Gupta-family long-term capital for Havells |
| **Open-Book, Fresh-Case Exam Format** | The professor's explicit final-exam structure: a mix of MCQs and case-based questions, applied to a brand-new case, testing framework fluency over memorized facts |
