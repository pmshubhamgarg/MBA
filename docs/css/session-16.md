# Session 16: Finishing Corning -- Four Decisions, One Painful One

*Tonight the professor closes out the Corning case for good. ## Part 1: Recap -- Why Hybrid Solutions Exist At All

Before touching Corning specifically, the professor re-anchors the whole topic in one sentence:

> "When the benefits of being together are there -- so the better-off test holds -- but acquisition is very difficult, not possible, not desirable, or the cost of organizing/ownership is very high, in those situations you need to look at a hybrid solution. The pure market kind of transaction would lead to very high transaction cost. So you need a hybrid solution in which neither full acquisition nor pure market."

**The toolbox of hybrid mechanisms**, restated as a list:
- Long-term contracts
- Cross-holding of shareholding
- Cross-appointments of directors on each other's boards
- Licensing of intellectual property rights
- Joint ventures

Think of these as **different strengths of "engagement ring" short of marriage** -- each one binds two companies a little more tightly than a handshake deal, without going all the way to a single household.

**Four reasons companies reach for these tools, restated with fresh examples:**

1. **Technology transfer.** A company has patents it can't or doesn't want to commercialize in every possible product-market. It enters that market itself where it can, and licenses out elsewhere.
2. **International expansion.** A company entering a new country may not understand local policy or distribution -- so it ties up locally.
3. **Complementary capabilities.** The professor's fresh example this time: **OpenAI has ChatGPT; Infosys or TCS has customer-specific IT implementation and offshoring capability.** The two come together to deploy AI solutions inside client organizations -- a strategic relationship that avoids outright acquisition on either side.
4. **Economies of scale via specialization.** One company focuses on R&D and scale in one part of the industry value chain; the partner focuses on another part. Each gets specialization advantage in its own domain, then the two cooperate to bring the pieces together.

> "Corning happens to be one of the best examples of that. Its entire corporate strategy is built on that model."

Corning has one deep, specialized capability -- **glass-making technology** -- with many potential product-market applications. Rather than enter every application itself (which it lacks the know-how to do well), it enters each one as a **50/50 JV** with a partner holding complementary capability, technology, or resources.

**Corning's own internal discipline, restated:** it tightens portfolio management to keep its own corporate strategy priorities aligned with each JV's priorities; it has strong culture and leadership support; it sends **high-quality people and resources** into JVs to make them succeed; and it is very careful about **personal-level comfort at the very top** between the two companies' leaders (the Ciba example: deep interpersonal trust and rapport at the top).

> "When something doesn't get solved, you can use your personal rapport to solve those things. And the second thing is that many such things might even be completely avoided."

**Important caveat on opportunism:** JV-level trust reduces opportunism risk compared to a pure market contract, but it doesn't erase it completely -- **only 100 percent acquisition removes opportunism entirely.** A partner can still cheat by under-allocating resources or capital, or by selling its stake to your competitor. Good personal rapport is a hedge against that risk, not a guarantee.

## Part 2: The Evaluation Framework, Restated Before Diving In

Before evaluating any of the four decisions, the professor restates the **external vs internal** criteria one more time, with slightly richer language than Session 15:

**External criteria (opportunity attractiveness):**
- Economic benefits: revenue growth potential from demand/market growth
- Strategic benefits: high profit potential from industry consolidation, acquiring high market share and its associated power, and competitive advantage from complementary/specialized resources

**Internal criteria (feasibility and fit):**
- Consistency with existing corporate strategy, portfolio, and in-house resources/capabilities
- Required investment -- money, people, and management bandwidth
- Expected returns -- amount and timeline
- **Opportunity cost** -- do you have better alternatives? Do you want a different timeline or a higher ROI?

With that settled, the class moves decision by decision.

## Part 3: Decision 1 -- Should Corning Exit the Ciba-Corning JV?

### The Setup

Ciba-Corning makes **medical diagnostic equipment**. The JV exists because neither partner can do this alone: Corning has the glass-making know-how for the equipment; Ciba brings an **established pharma brand, pharma know-how, and pharma distribution networks.** Neither wants to build the other's piece in-house -- for Ciba, developing glass technology from scratch would be a long, risky, capital-intensive diversion from its own core pharma business.

> "So it is advantageous for both of them to be together, but it is not advantageous for either of them to acquire each other or enter into each other's business on its own. So there's a clear case of JV here."

**Was the JV struggling? No.** Was there any partner tension? No. This is a **well-performing JV** -- its valuation had **doubled within three years.**

### So Why Even Consider Exiting?

The professor puts the puzzle directly to the class: *if something is working well, why exit?*

The answer: the business needs **further investment** to fund patient R&D, and the **new timeline to real success stretched to roughly 8 to 11 years.** Corning did not want to commit to that long a horizon, especially because it had a **more immediately attractive alternative** -- the laboratory-testing acquisitions (Decision 2) -- competing for the same capital.

> "The only two issues here for Corning is that the timeline is longer, for which it doesn't want to invest further resources, and it wants to rather invest those resources into quick, beneficial kind of opportunities. So it is the opportunity cost related issue rather than the actual performance of this JV."

**Strategic centrality check:** this business is not far from Corning's core capability -- it's an **adjacent** business, with Corning directly contributing complementary technology. (Contrast this, the professor flags, with the much more distant PCO/junction-box proposal discussed later.)

### Student Q&A Round on This Decision

**Q -- is this a long-term-return problem or a short-term-profitability problem?** A student asks directly whether the concern is poor long-term viability or just slower short-term returns.

> "As far as I remember, the long-term viability was not under question -- it was the short-term. It was taking longer to reach that lucrative position. So it required short-to-medium-term continuous investment of financial resources so that you can build a long-term advantage."

The professor frames it in present-value language: the longer the horizon, the more its present value gets suppressed -- and if a more lucrative short-term opportunity exists, its present value may simply be higher right now.

**Q -- "one bird in hand" (Manish's question):** if the JV is already working and generating returns, why chase a short-term opportunity that "is not sure"?

> "In corporate finance there is a truism: one bird in hand is better than two birds in bush."

The professor's answer is to **park the exit question entirely** until the alternative opportunity has been evaluated on its own merits -- not to decide reflexively based on this truism alone.

**Q -- exit clause details missing from the case; does the seller get a worse deal without one?** A student asks whether the party that wants to sell ends up at a disadvantage if no exit clause is spelled out, since the buyer holds the power.

> "Exit clauses are must. I am very much -- I know for sure that almost all the JVs would have well-defined exit clauses."

The professor lists the typical mechanics:
- **First right of refusal:** you must offer your stake to your partner before anyone else.
- **Competitor exclusion clauses:** you may be barred from selling to a defined set of direct competitors.
- **Arbitration jurisdiction clauses:** disputes go to arbitration before courts -- and many MNCs entering India deliberately set the arbitration seat in **Singapore**, because they don't fully trust the Indian court system and consider Singapore-based arbitration fairer.
- If legal mechanisms in the local jurisdiction are inefficient (the professor's example: India), disputes and enforcement can drag on for a long time.

**The liquidity-discount vs liquidity-premium nuance:** without a clean exit clause, a seller's stake may trade at a **liquidity discount** because it isn't easily sellable elsewhere. But the reverse can also happen -- if the stake is uniquely valuable and not otherwise available in the market, it can carry a **liquidity premium** instead.

**The real-world story the professor tells here -- L&T and Mindtree:** the Cafe Coffee Day owner (who later died by suicide) held roughly 20-plus percent in Mindtree. Mindtree tried to block the sale of that stake, but couldn't. L&T's acquisition of that first ~20 percent stake **triggered a mandatory open offer**, and L&T eventually acquired over 60 percent of Mindtree.

> "For that first 20 percent stake, it must have paid higher than the market rate, because that first 20 percent stake was very critical."

The lesson: negotiating power in a stake sale depends heavily on **who owns the stake and the specific situation**, not just a fixed rule.

**Q -- do bigger companies (more revenue, more geographic presence) always have more negotiating power in a 50/50 JV?** A student pushes this generalization.

> "In India, minority stakeholders also have a lot of power. Our firm has historically represented a couple of minority stakeholders in such situations, and we have ensured that these minority stakeholders get their due value of the stakes in private firms. So not all is lost for minority stakeholders -- you need to play your cards well. But in general what you are saying is right."

**Q -- won't exiting damage Corning's reputation with its other JV partners?** A sharp student question: if Corning exits Ciba-Corning the moment a better opportunity appears, doesn't that send a signal to *every other* JV partner that Corning might abandon them too, the moment something better comes along?

The professor's answer distinguishes **forced, hostile exits** from **amicable, win-win exits**:

> "If they are leaving the JV by keeping the partners unhappy, or if they are kind of forcing... yes, you are giving a very wrong signal to your other JV partners. But if it is a win-win kind of exit which was acceptable to both parties, then it may not necessarily damage your reputation. It may also signal to your partners that if there are beneficial reasons for them, you will not block those exits."

He previews the final case as the cautionary counter-example: **Mondelez** had a European JV whose partner sold its stake to **a bitter competitor** -- forcing Mondelez into an unwanted exit with no good options.

**The Microsoft-OpenAI tangent, used as a "graceful exit" example:** as OpenAI grew more powerful, Microsoft had to cede some control -- but in return it secured roughly a **31 percent stake**. The professor recalls figures along the lines of a combined investment of roughly 13 billion, of which the cash portion was around 4 to 5 billion and the rest was in the form of Azure compute credits, against an eventual valuation on that stake of roughly 131 billion -- **around a 10x jump.**

> "Microsoft would not be as happy as if it had kept very tight control over OpenAI, but both parties got what they wanted. It is not creating a holdup situation for either, and therefore both of them will preserve their own reputation with others."

(Note: these figures are the professor's own recollection in the moment, offered as an illustrative order of magnitude rather than an audited number.)

**Q -- do JVs have an expiry date?** A student asks whether joint ventures are time-bound the way contracts are.

> "The joint ventures themselves are a legally separate entity. As far as accounting and legal treatment is concerned, that entity has got the going-concern assumption -- on paper, that entity will go on forever, until some kind of shareholding/ownership change happens. You may have clauses that after certain milestones or years, one partner has the right to buy back or increase its stake -- so the nature of the JV may change, or it may become a wholly owned subsidiary. But the entity remains a going concern unless it faces bankruptcy and gets wound down."

> **Formula for remembering this: JV entity = indefinite going concern. Only the shareholding inside it can be time-bound.**

### Professor's Tentative Stance

> "Let us park this question till the end. Let us see: can you fund the alternative opportunities through some alternative mechanism? If yes, you are not letting go of those opportunities, and you are still keeping your strategic stake alive in this otherwise attractive JV. If not, then you may think of selling this stake and use that resource elsewhere."

## Part 4: Decision 2 -- The Laboratory-Testing Acquisitions

### The Business Context

This is **not a JV** -- it's a wholly owned Corning subsidiary business. Corning had recently entered laboratory testing in three areas:

- **Clinical laboratory testing** -- entered via acquiring **MetPath**
- **Pharmaceutical testing** -- entered via acquiring **Hazelton**
- **Environmental testing** -- a new, potentially high-growth area Corning wanted to enter

Each of these uses Corning's **porous glass technology** -- but that technology is only a small part of what makes the business succeed. Corning judged that it could **capture more value by owning the business outright** (100 percent of the profit, no sharing) rather than merely licensing the porous-glass technology out to third parties.

### The Three Targets and How to Prioritize Them

| Target | Segment | Effect of Acquiring | BCG-Style Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ICL** | Clinical testing (more mature, established) | Makes Corning **number one** in clinical testing | Becomes a stable **cash cow** once number one |
| **NSCO** | Environmental testing (fast-growing) | Makes Corning **number one** in a fast-growing space | Converts a **question mark into a star** |
| **Basel** | Pharmaceutical testing (already number six-ish player) | **Defends** Corning's existing number-one position in pharma testing | Prevents a rival from becoming number one if it acquires Basel instead |

> "According to me, at least, there is a higher prioritization for ICL. Second priority for NSCO -- because that makes you a number one player in a fast-growing environmental-testing business with great future potential. Third possibility is Basel -- it further consolidates your already number-one position... but if you don't acquire Basel and your number two or three rival does, that entity becomes number one and you become number two."

**Total price tag for all three: roughly 450 million.**

### The Financing Puzzle

Several live options are on the table:
1. Acquire only one or two of the three, not all three.
2. Fund partially by **divesting the Ciba-Corning stake** (roughly 150 million raised there), plus debt for the rest.
3. Use a **cash-plus-equity** structure, issuing equity stakes to the target companies' shareholders.

> "Typically, equity-based acquisitions are costlier in the long run compared to debt or cash-based acquisitions. So if your credit profile is good, and if you are not very worried about the success probability of these acquisitions, you may want to go for a cash-based mode of acquisition rather than equity."

**The credit-rating question (Goro's question):** if Corning raises the entire 450 million as debt without divesting Ciba-Corning, could that seriously hurt its credit rating?

> "If the current debt-equity ratio becomes very high, then your immediate credit rating may get hampered."

The professor connects this directly back to the **Cadbury-Adams case**: Cadbury's own credit rating dropped from roughly AAA/double-A to a lower investment-grade tier (around triple-B) after that acquisition -- and that was considered **acceptable**, because Cadbury was cash-rich, held a strong number-two market position, and could service the debt from the acquired business's own cash flow within a few years.

> "Similar argument can be made here: if these three businesses will give immediate returns compared to Ciba-Corning JV... the Ciba-Corning JV doesn't require very significant investment -- only 25 million. The real big investment is here. If immediate benefits start coming up within two years or so, your credit rating will again improve."

### Strategic Fit -- The Honest Weakness

Despite being financially attractive, the professor is candid that this is a **weaker strategic fit** than Ciba-Corning:

> "The only minor value that you are adding here is your porous glass technology. Apart from that, in all possible other ways this business is very different from your core capabilities and your other portfolio businesses."

Specific mismatches: most of Corning's other businesses are **manufacturing**; this is a **service business** requiring franchisee decisions, different business processes, different growth-rate management, and different control mechanisms. It also has **low R&D intensity**, unlike the rest of Corning's high-R&D-intensity portfolio.

> "Strategic-fit point of view, this is a weaker fit -- more of an unrelated diversification. Now there is no harm in going for unrelated diversification, if it is an extremely lucrative financial opportunity, the cost of organizing is not that high, it will not create negative conflict with the rest of the portfolio, and it should not consume top management bandwidth so significantly that you cannot achieve the desirable financial objectives."

**And crucially:** Corning is *already* in this business and has *not* struggled to make its existing acquisitions (MetPath, Hazelton) successful -- so the sensible move, having already entered, is to **build strong number-one positions**, not to second-guess the original entry decision.

> "Once you have entered, you might as well become a number one player in that business. That is my view. And this is my way of prioritizing the three options. Your view may be different -- that is fine, as long as there is an alternative logic."

### Professor's Final Position on Both Decisions Together

> "You should find out all possible ways to first invest into this business without exiting that JV. If you have got no other option, then you can seriously think of exiting that JV... My core hypothesis here is: why exit a strategically important JV which is working well, which has got positive NPV? The only issue is that the timeline is a little bit longer. Why exit? Preserve that good opportunity, and see if you can work out an alternative way to fund this otherwise lucrative opportunity."

### The Real-World Reveal

A student asks what Corning actually did. The professor -- true to his usual habit of *not* looking up outcomes before teaching a case, so the analysis isn't biased by hindsight -- says he checked after last session out of curiosity:

> "Eventually, the company did not exit Ciba-Corning JV. That I kind of realized after teaching this case for the first time. So I took a stand that why exit that JV, etc. Then somebody looked it up on the net and said that in reality the company did not exit that JV. So I was happy to know that something we are studying in this course is useful in terms of real-life corporate strategy decision-making."

## Part 5: Decision 3 -- Preemptive Optical Fiber Cable Capacity in the US (Core/Siemens)

### The Backstory

Corning had glass-making capability that it turned into fiber-optic technology -- but it never had **cable-making** capability. In the US, **AT&T held roughly 75 percent market share** and had a quasi-vertically-integrated arrangement with specific fiber-optic cable makers, making direct US entry extremely difficult. Corning even tried convincing a couple of copper-cable makers to enter the business, but they were too intimidated by AT&T's market power.

So Corning looked to **Europe** instead, where no equivalent monopoly existed, focusing on **long-loop, between-city fiber cables.**

**Why Siemens as partner:** Europe's protective tendencies meant a local European JV partner was needed, and Siemens was Germany's national cable-making champion with deep, historical credibility:

> "Siemens means the first undersea cable between England and India, laid by Siemens after 1857's mutiny. Siemens is in that cable-making business for that long -- it has deep know-how and a trusted local brand for cable making."

Corning's fiber-glass capability plus Siemens's cable-making capability and trust combined to create **"Core,"** a **50/50 JV**, successful in the European market.

### The US Opportunity Opens -- With a Ticking Clock

Antitrust action eventually broke AT&T's monopoly apart, opening the US market. Core wanted to enter -- but by then, the **long-loop demand had already peaked**, and the **within-city (short-loop) demand hadn't yet exploded**, because the **World Wide Web hadn't happened yet** (the timeline here is roughly 1989-90). Worse: **Corning's patents in this business were due to expire by 1992.**

**The decision:** should Corning **preemptively invest aggressively in US cable-making capacity** before its patents expire, so that when short-loop demand eventually explodes, Corning has a **scale-based cost barrier** to substitute for its expiring patent-based barrier?

> "Earlier you have got the patent protection, which was the entry barrier for others. Now you are creating a scale barrier for others -- and then use that cost advantage to penetrate the market when the eventual short-loop demand kind of explodes."

### Two Real Risks

The professor lays out his own reasoning (declining to poll the class first, so his logic isn't contaminated by groupthink):

1. **Scale advantage may get nullified.** If two or three major players all enter and compete on cost, a price war erodes profitability for everyone -- the whole market structure turns unattractive.
2. **Timing risk / carrying dead weight.** If Corning invests preemptively in 1991-92 but demand doesn't actually explode until 1997-98 instead of 1995, Corning carries years of extra depreciation that its more patient competitors -- who didn't preemptively invest -- would not have to bear. When a rival later enters at scale, Corning's cost structure could actually be *higher*, not lower.

### The Professor's Comparative Verdict

> "Because my pet proposal happens to be the Ciba-Corning alliance, I would compare the risk-return profile here versus the risk-return profile in Ciba-Corning. I personally believe the potential return for the given risk should be higher in Ciba-Corning as compared to this decision... I'm not saying don't invest here. I'm saying that if you have to make a comparison, that looks a more lucrative and less risky opportunity, based on differentiation advantage that will eventually build strong scale and brand -- as compared to this one, which looks a potentially risky investment, and if you commit, there is no way to have a rectification out of that."

Still, this decision has **genuine debate on both sides** -- unlike the next one.

### The Real-World Reveal (Goro's Update)

A student who works in this field checks and reports: Core went into partnership in Europe, and around **1997-2000, Corning acquired Siemens's stake** and bought the entire optical-fiber-cable business outright. Corning has run it as a standalone entity ever since.

The professor builds his own **conjecture** live, in front of the class, rather than simply accepting the fact:

> "My conjecture is that there was a strategic role of Siemens in Europe -- you needed a local European partner. That strategic role is not required in the US. Second, because this JV operated for long, the entire cable-making know-how has already transferred to the JV -- so that part is over. Third possibility: Siemens might not be agreeable to invest further into the US-based business and might have other, more lucrative opportunities elsewhere."

The student adds a striking detail: Corning reportedly paid a **lump sum around 1.3 billion** to acquire the stake, because by then the business had become highly profitable.

> "It became highly profitable **after** the [dot-com] bust... during the bust, all the cable-making companies were considered dogs... We have got the hindsight benefit. Eventually that business became very successful. All right, thank you for the update -- I will use this next time I teach this case."

> Think of this whole arc like buying a house before a neighborhood is fashionable: you carry the mortgage through some quiet, unglamorous years, and only years later does everyone realize you got in at the right price.

## Part 6: Decision 4 -- The IBM "PCO" Proposal -- An Unambiguous No

This is the proposal to enter the **junction-box / cable-peripheral-equipment** business (connecting different pieces of fiber-optic cable) by giving IBM a minority stake, forming "PCO."

The professor's position here has **no debate at all**, unlike the previous decision:

> "This particular investment proposal says: now you are entering into a junction-box kind of equipment for fiber-optic cables, which is way from your core capability. It has nothing to do with glass business. This is a structurally unattractive business, in which IBM has got preferred vendors already. If you tie up with IBM, you will still not become a preferred vendor -- you will become a fallback vendor. And IBM was developing this kind of peripheral device in-house. So if IBM succeeds, IBM would always prefer its own in-house boxes as compared to your JV-based boxes. And this is not a 50/50 JV. The R&D intensity is not that high."

> "I have an unambiguous position here: irrespective of any other decisions that you make, do not enter into this JV at all. It doesn't make sense to enter into this business. There is no strategic justification. There is no financial justification. Structurally weak. And you are not creating too much value for your partner -- your partner is not looking for your value addition significantly. To me at least: do not enter here. No. No. Definitely no here."

> **Formula for the exam: distance from core capability + structurally unattractive + you're the fallback vendor, not the preferred one = walk away, no matter what else you decide.**

## Part 7: Decision 5 (The Hardest) -- Asahi's Bid for 49 Percent of the TV Glass Business

The professor calls this "the final and most painful decision" -- and, unlike the exit decisions above (which are one-time pain), this is described as **ongoing pain** with real cultural and relational stakes, not just financial ones.

### The Backstory -- Decades of Trust

Corning's TV glass business (making cathode-ray-tube-era TV screens, in a pre-LCD world) is **one of Corning's oldest wholly owned businesses**, with manufacturing facilities located very near Corning's own headquarters.

Corning had licensed its glass-making technology to **Asahi**, a large Japanese conglomerate, since **the 1930s** -- Corning as technology provider, Asahi as technology user, paying royalties in return. During **World War II**, when Japan and the US were enemies, all communication between the two companies broke down for six or seven years.

> "What Corning realized was that Asahi had kept meticulous account of their sales throughout that period and paid all the royalty for that period to Corning. This is the strength of a great relationship with a very reputed, trustworthy partner. No cheating."

### The New Proposal

Asahi is now entering the **US market** for TV glass, and wants to buy **49 percent** of Corning's wholly owned US TV glass business (a **51/49** structure, deliberately *not* 50/50) -- carving a JV out of a business Corning has held outright for decades. Corning would use the proceeds to fund entry into **LCD glass** manufacturing.

### Why This Is Internally Painful

- It's one of Corning's **oldest** businesses, physically located near HQ.
- Post-deal, Asahi would take over manufacturing and **rebrand** the plants under its own name -- meaning local employees would suddenly see "Asahi" replace "Corning" on their own building.
- Employees would now work under **Japanese management practices and culture**, very different from US norms -- generating real anxiety and a painful cultural transition.

### Why This Is Also Externally Painful

- **Samsung conflict:** Corning has an existing JV with Samsung, and Samsung is a competitor of Asahi in the TV-screen business. Partnering with Asahi risks making Samsung "anxious" about whose side Corning is really on.
- **Philips conflict:** Corning is a major supplier to Philips in the US, and Asahi is a competitor of Philips in Japan. Would Philips stay comfortable using Corning as a supplier once Corning is partnered with a Philips rival? Would Asahi even tolerate Corning continuing to supply Philips?

> "We don't have the answers. What we are saying is that such questions arise in this sort of strategic stake sale and creating a new JV from your wholly owned subsidiary."

### No Resolution Given -- Deliberately

A student directly asks what actually happened with this deal. The professor is candid that he genuinely doesn't know, and explains **why he deliberately avoids looking up outcomes before teaching a live decision:**

> "If you know the answer, the whole analytical process kind of gets influenced by the answer. I leave that open, because when this decision was being made, the eventual outcome was not clear. That is true for all major strategic decisions anyway."

### Student Q&A on This Decision

**Q -- why would Samsung being "unhappy" even matter as a deciding factor?** A student had wondered why Samsung's discomfort should block the deal at all, and realizes mid-discussion that it depends on how much the acquisition actually damages the competitor.

> "Just because your customer, complementor, or partner will feel unhappy, that itself should not be a reason not to do that. There is a better way to do it and still keep your partner comfortable... Please understand, here we have an existential issue. If right now Asahi is ready to buy out the stake from Corning, if that opportunity is gone, and there is no way that Corning can achieve similar cost competitiveness, it is already under pressure in that business. If it is not able to achieve cost competitiveness, there will be a painful, gradual decline of that business, and it will suffer significantly. So I don't think there is a choice. I think there is a better way to manage the choice with respect to your existing partners."

**Q -- how does supplying two competing customers usually work in practice, when the conflict of interest isn't obvious upfront?** A student (Prasoon) pushes on the practical mechanics: what if a company simply doesn't know that its own supplier is also arming a rival, especially for smaller companies where such deals aren't public?

The professor's answer, in three parts:

> "As far as I understand, most of the time, if you are sourcing something strategic from somebody, you always have the information -- it is very difficult to assume that you don't know if a major strategic decision is made by that entity. If you don't know that, then you don't know how to do your business."

> "Are such issues very unusual? The answer is no. If a capability is very critical or a technology is very unique, typically the same company would be supplying the technology to multiple competitors at the same time. It's not very unusual. Look at Nvidia -- it's supplying GPUs to all the cloud competitors at the same time."

> "What is required is that you ensure none of your customers get a strategic benefit over the other because of you being the common supplier -- or, if you're okay with one customer getting a decisive benefit and another suffering because your eventual value-capture potential from the larger customer is higher, that dynamic is also possible. Sometimes what you need to do is convince your customers -- who are competitors of each other -- that you've created separation of businesses, separate teams, that strategic data doesn't get shared. If you look at our IT service companies, they often serve exact bitter competitors in respective markets, but create strong access control -- they don't allow mobile phones in certain areas, they encourage frequent client visits so clients can verify the access controls themselves. By doing all this, they create trust that the customers' interests are not harmed. And if you've got something extremely cost-efficient or high-tech to offer, customers have no other option but to stick with you."

> Think of this like a chef who cooks for two rival restaurants next door to each other: the chef survives only by proving -- through locked recipe books, separate kitchens, and open-door inspections -- that neither restaurant's secrets leak to the other.

## Part 8: The Overall Learnings From Corning (Professor's Summary)

Before moving on, the professor consolidates what the whole case demonstrates:

1. **This is a great example of a pure strategic-alliance-driven corporate strategy** -- Corning's entire corporate strategy is built on managing a web of alliances, not on owning everything.
2. **Multiple alliances pull a company in different directions.** Each alliance has its own investment priorities and strategic priorities. You must stay disciplined about whether you're drifting away from your core strategic objectives, core capabilities, or corporate strategy -- there's a real risk of being pulled into unrelated businesses or conflicting areas and **spreading too thin**.
3. **The case-specific recommendation:** rather than investing in the PCO business, preserve the Ciba-Corning JV and, if possible, raise financing to fund the lab-testing acquisitions instead. Whether Corning could actually raise enough debt is uncertain -- but the recommended sequence is: **first look for alternative financing, and don't enter PCO under any circumstance.**
4. **Be careful with the fiber-optic capacity investment** -- it turned out hugely profitable eventually, but the **short-term risk was very high**, especially compared to the much safer Ciba-Corning JV.

## Part 9: Preview of the Final Topic -- Spin-Offs and Activist Shareholders

The professor deliberately doesn't start the new topic in depth, but frames it clearly for next time:

> "So far we have only discussed entry -- should you enter a business, should you keep a business in your portfolio. Now in this final case we are more focused on spin-offs. Why would spin-offs happen? That is very much part of corporate strategy. Second thing: often spin-offs are not very attractive for the current top management -- so sometimes spin-offs are forced by activist shareholders."

**The final case: Kraft splitting into two businesses.** The case covers how the **consumer packaged goods** side of the original business acquired the Cadbury part of **Cadbury Schweppes** (linking directly back to the earlier Cadbury-Adams case) -- that piece eventually became **Mondelez.**

**What's *not* well covered in the case, but the professor fills in:** the other half (the grocery-focused business, formed via a later merger) was considered successful when the case was written -- but it later hit serious headwinds, and in **late 2025/early 2026** it split again, merging that grocery business with another company. That later split/merger was pushed by another activist shareholder: **Warren Buffett**, who had acquired a **10 percent stake** in that business.

> "Among the very rare failures of Warren Buffett -- that acquisition of stakes in that grocery business of Kraft is considered to be one of the failures. He had to sell the stake eventually at a loss."

The final case will also feature **Irene Rosenfeld** driving the corporate transformation.

## Part 11: Students' Own Companies -- Corporate Strategy Playing Out in Real Time

Several students spontaneously connect the course material to what's currently happening in their own organizations.

**Story 1 -- a 5-year JV where the foreign partner walked away.** A student describes their own company: a JV running for five years, where the **European partner stopped investing** last year. The **Indian partner then took over sales and marketing entirely**, a rearrangement was made, and **transfer-pricing margins were only given to the JV plants** -- since the underlying material technology still belongs to the European partner, they still get their margin, but the Indian partner captures everything else. **Story 2 -- a 25-year-old JV with no exit clause, and a CEO's regret.** A student shares a case from another organization: successful in the US for around 50 years, this company entered India via JV roughly **30 years ago** (going it alone as a single company everywhere else globally), and was successful in India for **around 25 years.** But margins in the *global* market only became strong in the last 10 years or so -- and the local Indian partner held **51 percent**, the foreign partner only **49 percent**, with **no exit clause.** When the retiring CEO visited India, he called this the **biggest mistake of his career**: without an exit clause, the JV could not be unwound even once the global market became more profitable than the Indian one -- and the JV continues regardless.

## Part 12: Industry-Level JVs and Standard-Setting

A student (Manish) raises a different category of JV he's seen: **industry-level joint ventures / consortiums**, where multiple competitors collaborate rather than pair off one-to-one. His own example: a maritime-industry open standard for exchanging ship-hull inspection data across formats, built as an industry consortium.

The professor confirms this is a real and distinct category, and builds out the logic:

> "There are standard-setting bodies, or industry-level cooperation that might happen -- that is also another kind of strategic network."

**The public-good logic behind standard-setting, explained with a fresh EV-charging example:** four different European EV car manufacturers came together to build **common, compatible charging infrastructure** across Europe.

> "Any public good means any investment that will be beneficial to everyone, but the cost can be private. In such situations nobody will invest into public goods on their own. So how do you encourage investment through a cooperation strategy? You bring everybody together and say: we will pitch in our own respective investments, and together we will capture the benefits of our collective investment."

**Why this matters -- the chicken-and-egg problem:** if each manufacturer had to build its own exclusive charging network, the upfront cost and uncertainty would be enormous (if charging isn't available, EVs don't sell; if EVs don't sell, charging doesn't make money) -- and a competitor building a rival, incompatible network could win the whole race. Instead: **connectors stay compatible, infrastructure cost gets shared, and the manufacturers compete only on selling their own cars.**

**Format-war examples, tied to the same logic:** **VHS beat Betamax** because powerful players deliberately came together and adopted VHS as the shared standard; **HD-DVD lost to Blu-ray** the same way. **2G, 3G, 4G** are all standards created by bringing large numbers of competitors together.

> "Standard-setting -- which is a public good -- reduces uncertainty for content makers (which format will sell), for device makers (which devices will sell), and for customers (which device to buy so they keep getting content). All of these uncertainties are gone when you cooperate on setting a standard."

**Who can set standards -- three possibilities, raised by a student and confirmed by the professor:**

1. **Government bodies** (or multinational government bodies, e.g. ISO, IMO)
2. **Industry bodies**
3. **Private players coming together directly** -- neither government nor industry body, e.g., the four European EV manufacturers.

> "That will require a lot of mutual trust and some facilitation process. European companies have typically got higher trust among each other. Can they develop such standards with Chinese companies or American companies? That would be much more difficult. But the trust deficit is not very high among themselves, and therefore they could come together to create such a standard."

**The monopolistic underside of standard-setting:** the professor is explicit that this cuts both ways.

> "Such activities can also be seen as monopolistic activities, because when you create a standard, that will exclude certain companies which are not complying with the standard. So that is another facet to standard-setting."

**Student example -- Apple's Lightning connector**, cited as a case where a de facto private standard remained monopolistic for years until **government intervention** (in Europe) forced a shift to USB-C.

**Student example -- a live tangent on Chinese-led standards:** a student raises a recent Chinese-developed interface, referred to as **TPMI / GPMI (graphics/media interface)**, positioned as a rival to **HDMI**, combining internet capability with video provisioning. The professor's blunt closing reaction:

> "American and European companies will try their very best that that doesn't become a standard. They will create all possible kind of doubts -- security issues, and this and that -- and they will not allow the broader adoption of those standards for their own [interests]. They will not adopt it. But that is an excuse."

With that exchange, and the clock run out, the professor thanks the class for "a very interesting discussion" and closes, with the final case (spin-offs, activist shareholders, Kraft/Mondelez/Buffett) to be covered next time.

**The meta-lesson of Session 16:** every one of Corning's four decisions gets solved with the *same* external-vs-internal checklist -- but the checklist doesn't always produce a clean answer. A well-performing JV with a long horizon (Ciba-Corning) deserves patience and alternative financing, not reflexive exit. A structurally weak, distant, fallback-vendor business (PCO) deserves an unambiguous no, no matter what else is decided. And some decisions (Asahi) are genuinely painful precisely because the "right" financial answer collides with decades of trust, culture, and triangulated partner relationships -- and real strategic decisions are made without knowing the outcome in advance.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Hybrid Solution / Strategic Network** | Neither pure market nor pure hierarchy; toolkit includes long-term contracts, cross-shareholding, cross-board appointments, IP licensing, and JVs |
| **Opportunity Cost (as exit driver)** | The reason to exit a healthy JV is rarely poor performance -- it's a more attractive, faster-return alternative competing for the same capital |
| **First Right of Refusal** | A standard JV exit clause requiring a selling partner to offer its stake to the other partner before any third party |
| **Arbitration Jurisdiction Clause** | A clause specifying where JV disputes get arbitrated (e.g., Singapore, chosen by MNCs entering India for perceived fairness over local courts) |
| **Liquidity Discount / Liquidity Premium** | A stake without a clean exit mechanism may sell below market value (illiquid) -- or, if uniquely valuable and otherwise unavailable, may command a premium instead |
| **JV as Going Concern** | Legally, a JV entity is presumed to exist indefinitely; only the shareholding/ownership inside it can be time-bound via buyback or milestone clauses |
| **BCG-Style Acquisition Logic (Corning lab-testing)** | ICL = converts entry into a stable cash cow (clinical testing, #1 position); NSCO = converts a question mark into a star (fast-growing environmental testing); Basel = defensive hold of an existing #1 position (pharma testing) |
| **Cash vs Equity Acquisition Financing** | Equity-based acquisitions are typically costlier long-run than cash/debt-based ones; cash/debt is preferred when credit profile is strong and acquisition success probability is high |
| **Credit Rating Risk in Debt-Funded Acquisitions** | High new debt-to-equity can drop a company's credit rating; acceptable if the acquired business's own cash flow quickly services the debt (Cadbury-Adams AAA-to-BBB precedent) |
| **Unrelated Diversification (acceptable case)** | Fine to pursue if extremely financially lucrative, doesn't conflict with the rest of the portfolio, and doesn't overconsume management bandwidth -- even if strategic fit with core capabilities is weak |
| **Scale Barrier (replacing a patent barrier)** | Preemptively building manufacturing capacity/cost advantage to block competitors once a patent-based entry barrier is about to expire |
| **Preemptive Investment Timing Risk** | Investing ahead of a demand curve risks carrying years of "dead weight" depreciation if the demand materializes later than expected, while non-investing rivals avoid that cost |
| **Fallback Vendor (vs Preferred Vendor)** | A JV/minority-stake position that doesn't earn you preferred-vendor status with the partner -- a red flag against entering a structurally unattractive, distant business (the IBM/PCO case) |
| **Common Supplier to Competing Customers** | Managed via strict information separation, access controls, and trust-building (IT services firms restricting client-site mobile phones); acceptable when the supplier's offering is uniquely cost-efficient or high-tech |
| **Spin-off (previewed)** | A form of corporate strategy exit -- splitting a business off from its parent, sometimes management-driven, sometimes forced by activist shareholders |
| **Activist Shareholder (previewed)** | A shareholder who takes a stake specifically to push for corporate strategy change (e.g., a forced spin-off); Warren Buffett's 10 percent stake and eventual loss in Kraft's grocery business is cited as a rare high-profile failure |
| **Public Good / Standard-Setting** | A shared standard (e.g., EV charging connectors, VHS, 4G) reduces uncertainty for makers and customers alike; can be set by government bodies, industry bodies, or trusted private players acting together -- and can simultaneously function as an exclusionary, monopolistic tool |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Priya had four folders open on her laptop and eleven minutes before the leadership sync. She was VP of Partnerships at a mid-size payments company, and this quarter she had to close out decisions on four outside relationships at once -- which was, she'd joke later, either very efficient or a sign that nobody had been managing this portfolio for a while.

The first three took no time at all. Kill the loyalty-rewards tie-up with a regional retailer that had never moved the needle. Say yes to acquiring the small fraud-detection startup her team had been quietly testing for a year -- easy, obvious, the kind of decision that makes you feel good at your job. Expand the co-marketing deal with a travel app that was clearly working. Three folders closed before her coffee went cold.

The fourth folder was named "Meridian," and she left it open on the screen without touching it. Meridian was the processing partner her company had built its entire backend on twelve years ago, back when Priya was an analyst two rungs down and Meridian's founder, a man named Devraj, had personally walked her through their settlement architecture on a whiteboard because nobody else in the building understood it. They had grown up together, her company and his. Now Meridian was quietly building its own front-end product -- one that looked, feature for feature, like it could sit exactly where her company sat.

She called Devraj that afternoon, half hoping he'd deny it. He didn't. "We're not trying to compete with you, Priya," he said, in the same unhurried voice from that whiteboard session years ago. "We're trying to make sure that when the market moves, we're not stuck being just the plumbing for somebody else's app." She believed him completely, and that was the uncomfortable part -- he wasn't lying, and he wasn't wrong to protect himself, and none of that changed the fact that his roadmap now pointed straight at her business.

There was no clean move here. She couldn't fire Meridian -- ripping out twelve years of infrastructure would take eighteen months and cost more than the problem itself. She couldn't out-invest her way clear of him, because the thing that made the partnership valuable in the first place -- how deeply woven together their systems were -- was the same thing that made walking away nearly impossible. She closed her laptop before the sync without writing a recommendation in the folder, because for once there wasn't a clean recommendation to write, only a relationship to keep managing, one quarter at a time, with her eyes open.

**The easy calls tell you whether a deal is good; the hard ones tell you whether a partner is still, deep down, on your side -- and sometimes you have to keep dancing with them anyway, because leaving the floor costs more than staying on it.**
