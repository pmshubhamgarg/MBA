## Part 1: Why Do Firms Internationalize At All?

Think of internationalization as a company simply **outgrowing its own backyard**. The professor gave three distinct reasons firms cross borders, and each has its own logic.

### Reason 1: Your Home Market Is Too Small

At some point, a company's local market becomes too small to sustain further growth, so it has to look beyond its own country's boundaries to find bigger markets. This is the most intuitive reason -- and it is exactly the story Philips (a Netherlands-based company) will illustrate later in this same session.

### Reason 2: You Have a Resource Advantage at Home That Foreign Markets Lack

Sometimes your own country has a resource-based advantage -- something cheap or abundant at home -- while a foreign market has the demand for exactly that.

> Indian IT service companies had the resource advantage of cheap, educated, abundant talent in India, and they found tremendously large markets in developed countries.

### Reason 3: Your Value Chain Is Inherently International

Sometimes the very nature of your business forces you to operate across countries, because no single country can host every stage of your value chain.

> A semiconductor chip: design might happen in one place, the foundry (fabrication) somewhere else, packaging elsewhere, and testing elsewhere again -- purely because of how the business itself is structured.

### The Flip Side -- Internationalization Also Brings Real Trouble

At home, a firm enjoys deep familiarity: it understands its own culture, customers, and regulations, and usually does not face hostility from its own policy environment. Cross a border, and all of that comfort disappears -- different regulations, different regulatory expectations, different norms, different culture, different financing environment, different customer mindset, different technology environment, and much tougher competition.

**The Adani story, told as a cautionary tale:** Adani Group has been accused of paying bribes in a particular Indian state, and is facing a lawsuit in the US -- even though Adani does not even operate in the US. The connection is that Adani had **borrowed money from the US**, and that financial link alone was enough to trigger US legal scrutiny.

> "It may be completely political, geopolitical, whatever. But the fact remains: internationalization can make your business more complex, your compliances more complex, your operations more complex, your risk management more complex."

## Part 2: Imprinting -- The Era You're Born In Never Really Leaves You

Before touching the main framework, the professor introduced a beautiful idea called **imprinting**: the era in which a company internationalizes shapes its organizational DNA for decades afterward -- almost like a birthmark that never fades.

Three historical waves were described.

### Wave 1: The Colonization Era

Many European firms internationalized easily simply by riding along with their countries' colonization of places like India, China, and parts of Africa. Communication in that era was painfully slow -- the undersea telegraph cable between the UK and India was laid only after 1857.

Because headquarters could not communicate quickly with distant subsidiaries, **country managers effectively became independent CEOs**. Home offices had to hand over enormous freedom, and this hands-off philosophy became baked into how these firms operated. Internationalization itself was easy in this era -- entry barriers were low because the home country controlled the colonized country, and there was almost no real competition since European firms were far ahead in technology and production sophistication.

### Wave 2: The Mass-Production / Industrial Revolution Era

Countries like the US and UK could now produce in huge volumes (the steam-engine-to-electric-motor transition), but their own local markets were not big enough to absorb all that output. Because mass production made things cheaper, these firms could compete on both cost and price and "flood" other markets.

Crucially, communication had improved by this point, so this era produced **tighter, centralized control** between home country and host country -- the opposite imprint of Wave 1.

### Wave 3: The Internet / Digital Era

Remote observation and control have become extraordinarily sophisticated -- live video feeds, keyboard-tracking, remote sensors on machines, and the ability to **remotely stop or start an asset**. Modern vehicles, for instance, can be locked remotely by their manufacturers if there is a legitimate regulatory requirement.

> This is producing extreme centralized control -- a genuinely new era in which very different organizational models, structures, and strategies are being deployed to internationalize.

**The takeaway:** different eras produce different internationalization DNA. A firm's control structure today may simply be an inherited imprint of the era in which it first went abroad -- which is exactly what will explain Philips's later struggles.

## Part 3: The AAA Framework -- Aggregation, Adaptation, Arbitrage

This is the one dominant, must-know framework for this course. The professor was explicit that more advanced international-business electives cover many more frameworks -- this course sticks to just three strategies.

> "Any successful internationalization would require at least one of these three. Companies that are becoming more sophisticated, and facing more complex competitive environments, have started adopting two of these together -- or sometimes even all three."

### Strategy 1: Aggregation

Think of aggregation as **one giant, centralized factory serving the whole planet**. There are centralized R&D labs, centralized plants, centralized product divisions, standardized products manufactured in large quantities -- and the entire point is **economies of scale**.

> **Aggregation's core advantage: R&D and manufacturing scale, achieved through centralized locations selling standardized products into world-level markets.**

**Example:** GE's aircraft engines, or GE's high-end medical equipment like CT scan machines -- designed in advanced centralized labs, manufactured in centralized locations, and exported worldwide in standardized form.

**Class Q&A -- is economies of scale the only motivation?** A student asked whether companies might also centralize simply because they don't want to export their technology to other countries. The professor agreed this is a real additional motivation, and used it to explain something important about the original AAA article itself:

> "The era in which this paper was published, the whole world was increasing internationalization and reducing border control -- geopolitics was not a major concern, so the researchers only looked at economies of scale and did not mention geopolitics at all."

He then opened up a rich geopolitics tangent:

- **Weak IP protection abroad can also push a firm toward centralization** -- if you can't protect your intellectual property in a country, you may want to keep production centralized at home instead.
- **But geopolitics can also push in the exact opposite direction.** India, for example, might once have been comfortable importing defense engines from GE -- but now insists those engines be manufactured in India, because if geopolitics turns unfavorable at a crucial moment, India could face a hold-up situation on critical supply.
- As global supply chains shift from **"just in time" to "just in case,"** and countries start using their own resource advantages as leverage, you see two opposite reactions: some firms centralize further (protecting their technology), while markets that don't own the underlying resource **demand localization** instead -- forcing disaggregation. This is exactly why **data localization, data sovereignty, and AI sovereignty** concerns are forcing hyperscalers to open their own data centers inside countries like India, wherever those markets are lucrative enough to demand it.

### Strategy 2: Adaptation

Adaptation means responding to country-specific customer, cultural, and regulatory needs -- needs that differ meaningfully across countries. Instead of chasing economies of scale, the competitive advantage here is **responsiveness**. Sometimes it isn't even an advantage at all -- it is simply a **survival requirement**, the only way to internationalize at all.

**Story bank for adaptation, all straight from class:**

- **McDonald's aloo tikki burger** is unique to India -- nowhere else does McDonald's sell that.
- **Fast food chains in Gujarat** refusing to sell non-veg items during Navratri, driven by local custom and local political pressure.
- Certain regions require **jain food** availability; some countries restrict selling beef, others restrict pork.
- **Content restrictions**: you cannot show India's map in certain ways within India.
- **Accountancy and law firms** may carry big international names, but every country has different accounting standards, norms, and actual regulations, forcing adaptation.
- **Advertising can blend both aggregation and adaptation**: a global brand theme ("selling happiness," for a cola company) can be centrally managed by one global account team, but each country chooses its own local celebrity -- a local cricketer or footballer -- and adapts the message to local sensibilities. Sometimes translating a slogan from one language to another accidentally produces a very negative or pejorative meaning.
- **AI firms** may need highly centralized R&D for economies of scale, but still must adapt to Indian languages -- because many models, undertrained on Indian languages, see accuracy drop below 40-45 percent. Becoming truly relevant in India requires local offices, local model training, and local data chains -- both aggregation and adaptation together.
- **P&G's Tide** is a big brand worldwide, but which fragrance works where differs completely: jasmine or lemon might have great appeal in India but zero appeal in Europe.
- **Haldiram's opening a restaurant in London** faced a genuine dilemma: keep the spice level authentic (satisfying Asian customers) or make it milder (to attract non-Asian palates)? This tension between staying true to the home recipe and adapting to local taste is a live, ongoing balancing act.

### Strategy 3: Arbitrage

Arbitrage means keeping **separate parts of your value chain in separate countries**, because different locations offer different advantages -- cost, capability, or both.

**The iPhone story:** design sophistication sits near headquarters in the US. Manufacturing used to happen almost entirely in China, but a significant part has now shifted to India, and some assembly (likely Samsung-related) happens in Vietnam.

**Traditional arbitrage advantages:** local cost advantages, low energy costs, favorable policy environments, capable workers willing to do dull and repetitive work that developed-country workers would refuse (or demand very high wages for), lower local wages, and favorable exchange rates.

**The Indian IT services evolution -- arbitrage's best case study:** Indian IT firms originally built their advantage purely on exchange-rate benefits and a huge pool of cheap, educated talent. Over time, this **cost arbitrage evolved into capability clusters** -- a genuine knowledge cluster developed in India. Today, even for sophisticated chip designs at nodes like 3 nanometer or 2 nanometer (which India cannot manufacture), the **software and design work for those very chips can be done in India**. This is exactly why so many **Global Capability Centers** now exist in India -- the country is still cost-competitive, but the bigger and newer advantage is this capability-cluster effect.

> "Wherever the dominant capabilities are lying, you locate that part of the activity in those countries -- and then connect all of them through very sophisticated information and communication technology links, very capable globally-movable employees, creating global supply chains or global ecosystems."

**Overlap is possible:** a huge centralized plant in China can give you both arbitrage advantage (cost) and aggregation advantage (scale) at the same time -- but the underlying rationale behind each strategy remains fundamentally different.

## Part 4: Comparing the Three -- The Master Table

The professor built out a comparison across four dimensions: strategic logic, where to locate operations, coordination requirements, and what typically goes wrong (the "extremism" risk).

| Dimension | Adaptation | Aggregation | Arbitrage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic logic** | Local relevance through national focus, while still exploiting some regional economies of scale | Global economies of scale through standardization | Absolute economies -- both learning and cost economies -- via international specialization |
| **Where to locate operations** | Primarily in host countries, because you need to adapt there | Centralized R&D and centralized production plants (mostly home country, though not always -- see Birla's Thailand plant) | Different functions located wherever comparative advantage for that function is highest |
| **Organizational structure typically seen** | Country-level offices, country-level production, **geographical divisions** | Global R&D and global production, standardized products, **product divisions** | **Global functional structures** -- coordination happens across functions, not countries or products |
| **Coordination requirement** | Weak intercountry coordination; strong within-country autonomy -- so transferring a successful product between countries is very hard | Coordination happens within a product group/division, not by country | Very high coordination requirement across dispersed functions (Apple must coordinate R&D, marketing, manufacturing, and chip design across totally different countries) |
| **What can go wrong (the extremism to watch for)** | Too much variety, no scale, and country heads become entrenched "change blockers" | Standardization becomes so extreme that any country-specific deviation is delayed or blocked entirely | Narrowing cost/exchange-rate spreads, geopolitical barriers, or choke points (like the Strait of Hormuz) make the whole model collapse |

**Regional middle-ground example (adaptation plus some scale):** Dell sells to Southeast Asia from a regional manufacturing hub (likely Thailand or Indonesia) -- some economies of scale, some adaptability, both brought together at a regional (not fully global, not fully country-by-country) level.

### The Real-World Failure Stories Behind Each Extreme

**Aggregation gone too far -- Amazon and Disney in India:**

> "Amazon could not sense the real threat of quick commerce in India, and it is now trying to catch up."

Amazon was very powerful in India until around 2019, but three sophisticated quick-commerce players (Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart-type competitors) entered and competed vigorously while Amazon's global, standardized playbook was too slow to adapt.

**Disney+ Hotstar is an even sharper story.** After Disney acquired 21st Century Fox (which came with Hotstar), top-level changes pushed out the local leaders who had actually built Hotstar's success. Adaptation "went for a toss," a potent local competitor (Jio) grabbed the opening, and Jio ultimately **swallowed the entire Disney+ Hotstar entity, converting it into "Disney" at a much lower valuation.**

> "The divisional heads, the product heads, can become very powerful and are so far removed from the markets that they say: give me scale, and then I will change -- otherwise I need this much minimum, I will not change. But that means you lose out country by country to local competitors."

**Arbitrage gone too far -- Europe's defense dependence, and a Dutch chip firm:**

> "Look at European countries' dependence on the US for their entire defense. That is the pure arbitrage situation, where Europe thought the US has huge markets, huge capabilities, and scale, so the US can provide defense technology far more efficiently."

Now those same European countries are struggling to build sovereign defense systems of their own. In a related, very recent real example, a Chinese-controlled chip company had shifted its manufacturing to China; the Dutch government, worried the firm could be used to exercise Chinese control, seized control of the company -- but the managers in China simply stopped listening to the Dutch headquarters, because they were now under Chinese influence.

> "So this real critical function is now not in your control. How will you operate?"

## Part 5: The Case -- Philips vs. Matsushita

The professor described this as one of those rare, special teaching cases -- like Newell paired with Danaher -- that can teach something no other case can. **Why unique?** Because it captures **two direct global competitors, Philips and Matsushita (known best by its Panasonic brand), competing head-to-head for five to six decades**, starting at completely opposite ends of internationalization strategy and eventually converging toward very similar models.

Only the **Philips side** was covered in this session -- Matsushita will be covered at the start of the next class.

### How Philips Started

A student answered correctly: Philips's core business was the **electric light bulb**, started around **1891** (the professor said "1990" verbally but meant the 1890s). Philips continuously improved its bulbs and upgraded plants as new technology became available.

**Why did Philips need to internationalize?** A student answered: the **Netherlands is a very small country** -- there simply weren't enough bulbs to sell locally. Three factors made early expansion possible:

1. Bulbs are highly **standardized items**, easy to sell across many different countries without adaptation.
2. In the **post-World War I era**, tariff barriers into the **US** were relatively low.
3. **Europe, by contrast, always had high entry barriers**, and Europe had not yet become an "aggregated market" -- so Philips had to build separate manufacturing plants inside individual European countries just to sell there, sacrificing scale advantage from the very start.

### Two-in-a-Box Leadership

Philips was originally very good at both **innovating** and **commercializing** that innovation -- powered by a classic "two in a box" leadership model: one person superb at technology, the other superb at commercializing and running the business.

> "Most successful technology firms have this. Look at Honda. Look at Intel. Look at Meta -- Mark Zuckerberg on one side, and on the other side you had this... Sheryl [Sandberg], who was CFO, a very good business person."

A student added that mobile phone companies followed the same pattern (a technical leader plus a commercial leader). The professor also cited **Dell**: even though Michael Dell was "a college dropout," not a technology genius, the moment Dell faced one quarter of losses early on, he immediately hired capable business people around him.

> "Entrepreneurial and technological capability combined with commercial and business acumen -- when that happens, you typically see companies succeeding."

### The Great Depression, World War II, and the Point of No Return

Because of the **Great Depression**, tariff barriers rose tremendously, forcing Philips to actually build manufacturing and a listed corporate presence inside the **US** just to keep selling there.

Then, **just before World War II**, Philips assessed that its home headquarters and plants were highly vulnerable to attack. To survive, the company **transferred its global headquarters to the US, and its European headquarters to the UK.**

This single wartime decision changed everything that followed, and the professor delivered the session's sharpest one-liner about it:

> "Giving away power is very easy -- not very easy, but it is possible. But taking back the distributed power is very difficult."

### Post-War: National Organizations Take Over

In the post-war era, country-level managers -- called **National Organizations (NOs)** -- became extremely powerful. Each NO had high autonomy, its own decision-making freedom, its own resources, its own manufacturing facilities, and the ability to adapt products freely to its own market.

For a while, this genuinely worked as an advantage. TVs, for example, needed real local adaptation: Canadian customers preferred different features than others; there were competing transmission standards (PAL versus NTSC); some countries liked TVs housed in cabinets, others didn't; screen-size preferences varied by country.

But then the world -- especially the US, fueled by the baby boom -- started growing very fast, and trade barriers began falling dramatically. Internationalization became easy for everyone, which meant **aggregation-based firms and arbitrage-based firms both gained a cost advantage** that Philips, stuck in its adaptation model, could not match. Japanese competitors entered, then Korean competitors, then (much later) Chinese competitors -- all with lower cost structures and bigger scale.

### The Central Puzzle: Why Couldn't Innovation Save Philips?

The professor posed the key diagnostic question directly: Philips was still highly innovative -- so why couldn't it counter cost-based competition through **differentiation** and premium pricing?

Several students offered partial answers:

- **One student:** national-organization managers had all the power and control over assets, and simply didn't want to give it up -- explaining why re-**aggregating** became politically difficult.
- **Another student (Manish):** Philips had capacity shortages -- demand outstripped what Philips could actually produce, which should have let it charge premium prices (the professor compared this to Google telling Meta it simply doesn't have enough compute capacity to sell it more -- a position of strength, if you are genuinely differentiated).
- **Another angle:** competitors were moving to low-wage regions to cut costs while Philips stayed in high-cost regions -- but this doesn't explain why Philips's own **innovation** couldn't be commercialized in time.

The professor's overarching frame for the whole discussion:

> "As we always say in strategy: your past success will sow the seeds for your future failure if you are not careful."

### The Real Answer: A Broken Value Chain

The professor mapped Philips's value chain as a simple flow: **fundamental research to product development to local adaptation to local manufacturing to marketing.** The real problem was about **who owned which link** in that chain.

| Layer | What it owned | What it should have owned but didn't |
|---|---|---|
| **R&D** | Basic research and development, centralized mostly in the Netherlands and elsewhere in Europe | Any real feedback loop from the market telling it what to prioritize |
| **Product Divisions (PDs)** | Almost nothing of real power | Should have had a strong say in development (since R&D's output becomes the PD's "baby") and in local adaptability -- but had neither |
| **National Organizations (NOs)** | Manufacturing, budgets, and direct market/customer understanding | Ended up making all adaptation decisions themselves, with zero accountability to whether that adaptation helped or hurt scale anywhere else |

R&D was so powerful it answered to nobody: "We have our own pet projects, we will develop our own technologies -- you decide how to commercialize that, it is not our job." Product Divisions were squeezed from both sides -- unable to influence R&D and unable to control adaptation, which NOs had seized entirely. Some NOs, purely to make their own local job easier, even **supported a direct competitor of Philips** in their own country, because that was more efficient for them locally -- actively hurting Philips elsewhere.

> "The bridge between R&D and the market was broken on both sides. Product Divisions were hanging without any significant organizational power, no information connection, no decision-making power -- nothing."

The result: Philips remained innovative **on paper**, but struggled to bring that innovation to market. Many innovations became meaningless because they were completely disconnected from market realities -- destroying the very differentiation advantage that could have countered the cost threat.

> "You don't have differentiation, you don't have cost. Which means you have a competitive disadvantage with respect to your competitors -- and that is why Philips was continuously struggling, restructuring, selling businesses, just to survive."

**Class Q&A -- why didn't adaptation via National Organizations actually work well, if adaptation is normally a good thing?** A student (Pri) pushed on this directly: NOs, being local, should logically have been *good* at bringing in local character -- so why did this fail? The professor's answer used the **US National Organization** as the clearest example:

- The US NO was the *most* powerful of all the NOs, with a large, homogeneous home market -- decent scale, but nowhere close to the scale Japanese, Korean, or Chinese exporters achieved by serving the entire world.
- Because NOs had **no incentive** to locate manufacturing in low-cost countries, they gained no arbitrage advantage either.
- Because NOs only served their own country, they had no access to the global market either -- so no aggregation advantage.
- Limited markets meant limited R&D scale, so NOs also couldn't produce truly cutting-edge global innovation.

> "So neither could they differentiate at a global competitive level, nor could they operate at globally competitive cost structures. More and more they got squeezed -- but even then, they were not ready to let go of their own control."

Eventually (the professor said "87 or 97," uncertain of the exact year), Philips headquarters had to **buy back roughly 700 million dollars worth of stock in its own US subsidiary** just to regain a higher level of control -- but by then a lot of competitiveness had already been lost.

### The "Dutch Mafia"

The NO managers became an institutionalized power bloc the professor called, straight from the case, the **"Dutch mafia."** They:

- Controlled plants, budgets, and salespeople directly.
- Had the real pulse of the customer and market.
- Required nothing from headquarters.
- Had **direct access to the board** through a dedicated **International Concern Council.**
- Formed the most lucrative career path inside the company -- any ambitious executive started their career inside one of the NOs and rose to the board through that route.

> "It was a fiefdom. It was a completely independent kind of company on its own. This was an institutionalized, sedimented way of supporting the power of the NOs. It is very difficult to dislodge this."

**Contrast flagged for next session (Manish's observation):** in Matsushita, the picture was the opposite -- R&D labs were chronically underfunded, while national heads always reported back to Japan and were kept under tight control. The professor confirmed this is exactly why the two cases are so powerful *together*: Matsushita's R&D got only **partial funding from corporate**, and had to secure the rest by convincing Product Division heads -- which forced R&D to actually align with what the market (via PDs) wanted. That partial dependence is precisely the missing link that Philips never built.

> "If only PDs provide the entire funding, that will be harmful too. But if part of the funding comes from PDs, R&D cannot become arrogant, cannot become completely detached from the market."

### What Philips Actually Needed by 2010 -- and Why It Was So Hard

By 2010, Philips needed all three things it had never had: **innovation reaching the market fast, scale advantage through consolidated plants, and low-cost-country manufacturing (arbitrage) with global supply chains.**

**Student question (Pratik) -- Vendor Cloos's restructuring:** a student asked about the CEO who tried to fix this by creating **four global divisions** out of what had been highly decentralized National Organizations, alongside joint ventures for non-core businesses. Why didn't this raise profits the way it should have?

The professor's answer: the *direction* was correct -- consolidating into global divisions was the right instinct. But **creating a division is not the same as actually achieving scale.** Cloos was not able to relocate plants into low-cost regions and achieve real arbitrage-driven scale. Structure changed; the underlying economics did not.

> "Just creating a division is not enough. Ultimately you have to create scale and arbitrage together -- then you can fight back. But you have not done that."

Cloos was, in the end, **fired** despite being on the right strategic path -- because unanticipated losses hit during his tenure, and boards rarely give CEOs the time to work through a complex organization's inertia before a crisis forces a change.

### The Professor's Philosophical Turn: Reality Is Messier Than Any Model

This case discussion produced one of the richest tangents of the semester. Several threads, all tied together:

**On CEOs and organizational inertia:**

> "In such a complex setup, you may have thought of something, but you don't know how that complex setup will fight back -- its inertia will fight back. You may have intended something, but the actual outcome may be very different. By the time some crisis happens, the board will fire you... and the new CEO will have to show they are doing something different than the predecessor."

**On Anthropic as a live real-world parallel:** the professor described how Anthropic tried to position itself by saying its models were so capable it would release them in a very limited, controlled way, and even published a long blog arguing AI companies should be controlled -- and the moment it launched more widely, the US government stepped in with its own controls. Whether that regulatory reaction was caused by Anthropic's own positioning is genuinely unclear -- exactly the kind of unanticipated, unintended consequence that complex systems produce.

> "Project reality is always messier than our clean models. Our clean models will give you some direction -- beyond that, how much of this complex reality is under one person's control is always debatable."

**The population ecology view (offered as "wearing a philosopher's hat," not a strategist's hat):** the professor described a body of research that treats organizational forms like **moths that appear after the rain** -- they emerge in a particular era, grow powerful, but become rigid over time because of their own past investments and past success. When a new business reality arrives, the older forms cannot adapt and they die out, while new forms survive.

> "If I say companies can be looked at as flies, you will boycott my course -- so I will not do that. But look at the Fortune 500 list from the 1950s versus today -- maybe one or two companies survived; all others are gone. Where will Google and Meta be in 50 years? We don't know."

**On why Japanese firms themselves eventually declined too (raised by a student):** the professor confirmed that Matsushita's original Japanese export-driven model *itself* later came under threat -- exactly what the class will discuss next session. Nothing stays imprinted as an advantage forever.

**The one framework that never fails, when all else feels too messy:**

> **Willingness to Pay minus Cost.**

> "There is no sophistication required there. Common sense: either offer your customer something others can't offer, or offer the same thing at a cost structure others can't match. Everything else is a sophistication built on top of that."

**On why Japan/Korea/China succeeded so completely:** the professor argued the West had become arrogant -- dismissing these countries as unable to compete -- while those countries had a clear alternative and no other way to grow, and by the time the West woke up to the threat, it was too late to fight back.

### Rapid Real-World Tangents Raised in Q&A

- **Persistent's acquisition of Nagarro (live news at the time):** the professor was openly skeptical, noting Persistent hadn't clearly articulated the strategic benefit, had paid a high multiple in cash during a period of pressure on the whole IT services sector, and had never acquired a company this large before. His blunt read: "If I am cautious, I would sell Persistent stock -- which is what has happened." (The stock fell roughly 11 percent, later corrected in discussion to about 2 percent on the day.)
- **Is the "National Organization becomes too powerful" problem unique to Philips?** No -- the professor gave several parallels: **ITC** has always remained independent from British American Tobacco's stake; **Hindustan Unilever (HUL)** held much higher relative power for a long time before Unilever began asserting more control; **Nestlé** was comparatively more integrated, even though Maggi itself was an India-specific innovation. Many **US companies operating in China** must adapt heavily because of mandatory local partners and a difficult, highly competitive policy environment -- making local country managers there very powerful too, to the point that **global banks have stopped giving their own Hong Kong offices access to powerful AI models**, fearing loss of control if those offices extend access to Chinese firms.

**The meta-lesson of Session 10:** internationalization strategy is never just "go global" or "stay local" -- it is a genuine trade-off between scale (aggregation), responsiveness (adaptation), and location-specific advantage (arbitrage), and the *era* in which you first internationalize can imprint a control structure onto your organization that outlives its own usefulness by decades. Philips is the textbook cautionary tale: a company that inherited an extremely decentralized, NO-controlled structure from its wartime survival decisions, and then found that structure impossible to unwind even after the world had moved on to rewarding scale and cost. The single biggest lesson: reorganizing on paper (Vendor Cloos's four divisions) means nothing if you cannot also deliver the underlying economics (scale, arbitrage) that the reorganization was meant to unlock.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Imprinting** | The idea that the historical era in which a firm internationalizes shapes its control structure and strategy for decades afterward |
| **Aggregation** | Centralized R&D and production, standardized products, sold globally to capture economies of scale (GE aircraft engines, CT scanners) |
| **Adaptation** | Responding to country-specific customer, cultural, and regulatory needs; competitive advantage is responsiveness, not scale (McDonald's aloo tikki, Haldiram's London spice dilemma) |
| **Arbitrage** | Locating different value chain stages in different countries to exploit local cost or capability advantages (iPhone design in the US, manufacturing in China/India/Vietnam) |
| **Capability Cluster** | An evolved, more sophisticated form of arbitrage advantage, beyond pure cost -- India's IT sector moving from cheap labor to genuine chip-design capability |
| **Just-in-Case Supply Chains** | The post-geopolitical-shock shift away from "just-in-time" sourcing, driving both re-centralization and forced localization depending on who holds the resource leverage |
| **National Organization (NO)** | Philips's country-level unit, which accumulated manufacturing, budget, and market control and became an independently powerful "fiefdom" |
| **Dutch Mafia** | The professor's (and the case's) term for the entrenched, board-connected network of Philips's NO managers |
| **International Concern Council** | The Philips board mechanism that gave NO managers direct access to the board, cementing their power |
| **Product Division (PD)** | Philips's product-category management layer, left powerless -- squeezed between an unaccountable R&D function and unaccountable NOs |
| **Broken Value Chain (Philips)** | R&D disconnected from market feedback, PDs with no power in either development or adaptation, NOs controlling adaptation without accountability to global scale |
| **Vendor Cloos's Four Divisions** | Philips's attempt to consolidate NOs into global divisions -- directionally correct, but failed because scale and arbitrage were never actually delivered |
| **Population Ecology View** | The research tradition treating organizational forms like short-lived species that rise, become rigid from past success, and die when the environment changes |
| **Willingness to Pay minus Cost** | The professor's fallback "no framework needed" answer: a firm survives only by offering something others can't, or the same thing cheaper than others can match |
