# Session 12: P&G's Structure Evolves -- Country Kings, the Matrix Wars, and the Company of Three Companies

*Picking up exactly where Session 11 left off, the professor walks the class through diagram after diagram of P&G's decades-long structural evolution -- Europe's country-manager kingdom, the 1987 US matrix, Europe's product-category push, the global matrix with its oddly-bypassing dotted line, and finally the most radical structure of all: Organization 2005, P&G's "three companies within one company." Along the way, a room full of working professionals turns a lecture on matrix structures into a genuine group-therapy session about their own bosses.*

---

## Quick Recap: Where We Left Off in Session 11

| What We Covered | Key Takeaway |
|----------------|--------------|
| Matsushita's full story | 250-year plan, powerful Product Divisions, 60/40 profit rule, suffocating central control, failed decentralization experiment |
| P&G case opens | 1955 US structure: product divisions (Foods, Toilet Goods, Soaps and Detergents), brand managers as "mini-CEOs" with responsibility but no formal control |
| Three structural dimensions introduced | Product (scope/scale), Geography (adaptability), Function (efficiency) -- every structure favors one over the others |
| Strategic driver in 1955 | Land-grab -- grow market share fast, don't worry about cost, in a homogeneous, fast-growing US market |

Session 12 opens by briefly re-confirming the 1955 US structure, then crosses the Atlantic to Europe -- and never really stops moving after that.

## Part 1: Diagram Three -- P&G's Europe, and Why It Had to Be Different

The professor puts up **diagram three**: P&G's early European structure. He asks the class directly -- is this the same kind of structure as the US, or different, and why?

Students converge fast: this is a **geographical divisional structure**. Instead of product or brand managers, everything sits under a **country manager**. Profit and loss responsibility belongs to the country manager, not a product head.

> "Europe is not smaller." The professor pushes back hard when a student says Europe is small and varied. "Countries are smaller," the student corrects -- and that distinction turns out to matter a lot.

**Why country size (and variety) drives the structure:** a student points to Belgium speaking French and German, while its neighbor Germany speaks only German -- real linguistic and cultural variation packed into a small geographic footprint. Multiply that across a continent, and you get real heterogeneity that a single US-style product structure could never handle.

A student pushes the logic further with an Indian parallel: if India has states speaking different languages, does that mean P&G India should also run a geographical structure? The professor doesn't fully know P&G India's actual structure, but agrees that "by that logic, yes, we should" expect something similar.

### The Structure Itself

| Layer | Role |
|---|---|
| **Country Manager** | Owns profit and loss for the entire country -- sales, marketing, distribution, manufacturing, HR, finance, purchasing |
| **Centralized functions** | Core R&D, product design, and process design stay centralized at headquarters |
| **Local customization** | Packaging, messaging, fragrance, and branding are adapted to local taste, even though the underlying product (say, Tide) stays common |

A student summarizes it as a **hub-and-spoke model**: the European Technical Center acts as the hub, and each country is a spoke, with the country manager owning marketing strategy and profitability for that spoke.

> The professor confirms: "Market development, sales and distribution development, packaging, language-based messages, culturally sensitive messages -- those adaptations are in the hands of the country managers."

Another student adds the nuance that made this structure genuinely mixed rather than pure geography: **shared corporate functions** (R&D, process design) reduce overhead even while the divisional axis is geography. The professor agrees but is firm that this doesn't change the core classification.

> "True, but even in the US there were shared functions -- like here also there are shared functions, to create some degree of economies of scope. But otherwise this is purely a geographical divisional structure, clearly."

## Part 2: The Pampers Puzzle -- Why Silos Weren't (Yet) a Problem

A student, Vikas, raises the case's famous detail: **Pampers took 11 years to globalize** under one of these structures. If top management could see all the finances and product lines, why would a wildly successful diaper brand take over a decade to reach other markets?

The professor's first correction is important: **that 11-year delay actually happened later, in the global matrix structure** -- a structure meant to be more sophisticated than this one, and it still happened. So the puzzle only deepens as the case goes on.

**Why local power centers resist new products.** As organizations grow large and bureaucratic, local country managers develop their own semi-independent power bases. Their incentive is simple: protect their own country-level profitability.

> "Any new product that gets introduced typically requires 3 to 5 years to become profitable. So their immediate profitability would get hurt, and therefore their immediate incentives would get hurt. So why should they cooperate? They are making profits for all the existing brands."

This is the professor's broader point about bureaucracy -- a genuine double edge:

> "On one hand, bureaucracy allows you to handle unbelievably large structures. On the other hand, it creates lots of barriers to change, entrepreneurship, innovation, and interdepartmental or interdivisional cooperation. The whole struggle is how to overcome the weakness of a particular structure and strengthen it where it is creating a problem."

A student (NR) asks the sharper version of the question: if country managers are working in silos, purely protecting their own profit margins, why isn't that a problem *at this specific point in time*?

**The class's own answer, confirmed by the professor:** because the strategic imperative of that era was market development and penetration, not efficiency. Country managers were busy building sales and distribution, launching brand campaigns, adapting packaging and fragrance, and clearing regulatory barriers -- exactly the work this structure was built to do well.

> "Strategic imperative at that point of time was to develop and penetrate specific country-based markets. So this was a good structure for that. It was not the worry of P&G at that point of time to launch global brands in every country -- that was not the imperative."

### Manish's Philips Parallel: Giving Power Is Easy, Taking It Back Is Hard

Manish connects this straight back to the previous case: the European hub gave country managers huge autonomy from day one -- was that hands-off approach itself the mistake?

> "Giving power is easier, but taking back is much more difficult," Manish observes, echoing the exact lesson from Philips.

The professor agrees completely, and adds two reasons this happened:

1. **European management style favored delegation** by default.
2. **Political sensitivity across countries.** Trying to control a French manager while favoring a German one risks a revolt, given real country-based rivalry inside a Brussels-run, multi-nationality management team. The "safer" choice was to give everyone roughly equal power.

There's also a timing story here that's genuinely fascinating:

> "The whole global-efficiency-based competition had not evolved yet. It started evolving post-1960s, when Japanese companies started exporting in a big way. But why Japanese companies were competitive -- that was understood only in the '70s. Can you believe that? In the late '60s, early '70s, people believed the Japanese were subsidizing their own companies, that the yen was weak, and their actual cost structure was higher and unprofitable. Gradually European and American companies realized the Japanese were actually very efficient and very profitable, and the source of that advantage was different -- economies of scope and scale. But that understanding took time to evolve, and by then the power had already been distributed. Taking it back would require a struggle."

A student adds the media/technology angle: at this point in history, there was no social media and no shared newspapers across borders, so markets were genuinely isolated. P&G could position the same brand very differently in Germany versus another country without any spillover effect. The same logic applied to supply chains -- local production was locally sourced by necessity.

## Part 3: Why Not Just Centralize Everything?

The professor poses a sharp counterfactual: why not centralize all functions at Brussels, and only manufacture locally to clear tariff barriers -- giving country heads minimal power, just enough to "manufacture what we tell you and sell"?

**His own answer reveals the real genius of the geographical structure.** If Brussels controlled everything centrally, it would naturally prioritize easy, large markets first -- UK, France, Germany, and maybe Spain or Italy -- while smaller or harder-to-enter countries would simply wait.

But under the actual geographical divisional structure, **each country manager's only path to growth was to saturate their own market with every possible P&G brand.** That single incentive did something remarkable:

> "By the time Europe started becoming a homogeneous market and all these tariff barriers went out, all the major brands of P&G were already established, the entire sales and distribution structure was ready, and marketing campaigns were fairly fine-tuned. So when there was a possibility of sourcing-level efficiencies or higher coordination from Brussels, you had already achieved country-specific penetration."

The flip side of that major advantage: country managers ended up with a lot of power -- power that would later need a completely different mechanism to coordinate, control, and convert into efficiency and scale.

## Part 4: The Matrix Structure -- A Room Full of Scar Tissue

Before returning to P&G's actual 1987 structure, the professor opens a broader question to the class: why would *any* company adopt a matrix structure, and what problems come with it? What follows is one of the richest, most personal discussions of the session.

**The professor's own framing of the core problems:**

> "Two major issues with any matrix structure. One is that you have got two or more bosses -- managing one boss is difficult, two or more is impossible. Second issue is subordinates are very smart -- they will play one boss against the other."

### The Class Speaks From Experience

| Student | Their Take |
|---|---|
| **Prasoon** | Has "faced more trouble than benefits" -- weak sense of ownership, functional heads who keep both reporting lines "in the dark," genuinely difficult to validate who's accountable for what. Benefit: shared resources reduce cost, and specialists can do good work regardless of who their people-manager is. |
| **Manish** | Matrix works best in project-based organizations -- a project boss plus functional departments allows resource sharing and better communication, especially in consulting-style firms combining, say, a technology expert and a strategy expert on one engagement. |
| **Unnamed student** | Employees exposed to a matrix get broader visibility across functions -- genuine career growth benefit, more clarity, more knowledge of how the wider organization works. |
| **Another student** | The "two bosses" problem can be solved with a better HR review system, where feedback comes from *both* managers -- that alone can make the structure work much better. |
| **Vikas** | Matrix structures suit **startups and smaller organizations**, where founders can see everything directly. Structural problems only emerge once organizations grow layers of middle management and priorities start to diverge. |
| **NR** | There are two entirely separate angles here -- the *employee's* point of view (all the pain everyone described) and the *organization's* point of view (which the professor thanks him for raising). |
| **NR (continued)** | Gives a real onsite/offshore example: an offshore employee's formal manager sets their KPIs, but the onsite team has no formal authority over them -- yet informally, the onsite team can still shape behavior because they control day-to-day visibility and appraisal input. Real benefit: keeps the resource aligned to business needs. Real risk: becomes a political tool if visibility isn't shared honestly with the formal manager. |
| **Chaitanya** | After years in a matrix reporting to two different streamline managers, faced slow decision-making and unclear instructions personally -- but organizationally, it improves coordination, keeps people task-focused, and forces a more balanced view when two managers disagree, so people stay aligned with top leadership's actual goals. |

The professor's own read on why more experienced people default to criticism:

> "Whenever I raise this matrix structure for less-experienced, full-time MBA students, they always start with the positives. But because all of you are experienced people, you always start with the problems, because you have suffered what this particular structure can do. I completely agree with all your points."

**What actually makes a matrix structure work**, per the professor's own three-point checklist:

1. **Clearly defined KPIs.**
2. **A clear rule for which dimension is subordinated to the other** when there's a genuine conflict.
3. **A company culture** where bosses don't use the subordinate as a pawn in their own political games.

> "Making this structure work is not always very easy. It requires some effort. But you will rarely see a large multinational organization without some matrix kind of reporting relationships. The actual intended benefits are quite significant -- you need to coordinate two different priorities or knowledge, and there is no other way to do it. Not all companies, teams, or bosses can make it happen -- that is why you see success stories and horror stories both."

## Part 5: P&G's Own 1987 Matrix (US) -- Two Changes at Once

The professor now returns to P&G specifically. By **1987**, the strategic era has genuinely changed:

> "By now, between 1955 and '87, the era of land-grab is over. The market growth rate has become more stable, and whatever market shares your brands acquired in this high-growth era are now hard boundaries you have to manage. Competition has increased. So earlier you were just focusing on increasing market share -- now you have to worry about costs and efficiencies."

Two structural changes happen simultaneously, and it's easy to only notice one of them.

### Change One: A New Top Layer -- Product Category

Compare the top row of the 1955 structure (Soaps and Detergents) with 1987: now the top row is **Laundry Division VP**, and Detergent GM sits one level below. P&G has inserted a **product category** layer above individual products, which sit above individual brands -- a genuine three-tier product structure.

> "P&G was the first organization to launch the product category as the highest level."

**Why this layer helps:** related products (detergents, specialized detergents, fabric softener) share far more manufacturing and R&D best practices with *each other* than they do with, say, household cleaning products. Grouping them under one division lets economies of scope and scale actually get captured -- best practices spread easily *within* a product category, much less so across unrelated ones.

### Change Two: The Dotted-Line Functional Matrix

The second change is the introduction of **dotted-line relationships** between product-side management and a parallel functional structure.

**Worked example -- manufacturing:** the Detergent GM is the solid-line boss for the Detergent Manufacturing head -- the one who decides incentives and promotions. But there's now a second, dotted-line boss: the **Manufacturing Laundry Director**.

> Imagine the Tide brand manager pushing to fulfill a big order regardless of batch size -- cost efficiency be damned, timelines must be hit. That is exactly the moment the Manufacturing Laundry Director's role kicks in: highlighting batch-size efficiencies, optimal capacity utilization, continuous improvement (kaizen), and best-practice sharing across plants.

The Manufacturing Laundry Director doesn't have final authority -- the Detergent GM still outranks them on paper -- but they can escalate concerns, prepare reports, and make the case to the Laundry Division VP if something isn't working. The same logic applies to R&D: an **R&D Laundry Director** oversees requests like new lab equipment or conference attendance across all the laundry-related R&D teams.

> "Earlier the only focus was on market share. Now you have got one cost focus, although with some less amount of authority. Between the Detergent GM and the Manufacturing Laundry Director there's a clear higher authority with the Detergent GM over the divisional functions -- but there is some kind of a suggestion power."

**Which dimension is dominant here?** Product is still clearly dominant -- profit and line responsibility live there. Function is the subordinated dimension, but for the first time it's playing an *active* efficiency-pushing role, not just sitting as a passive shared service.

| Cost-Reduction Lever | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Product category layer** | Captures economies of scope and scale by grouping related products, enabling best-practice and knowledge transfer within the category |
| **Functional dotted-line matrix** | Functional directors (manufacturing, R&D) push efficiency, batch-size discipline, and continuous improvement across product lines, with suggestion power upward if ignored |

## Part 6: Diagram Four -- Europe Gets Product Categories, But No Matrix

In the **1980s**, Europe gets the product-category layer too -- but crucially, **no functional dotted-line matrix.** The professor asks the class directly: what's the purpose here, and what happens to the country manager's role?

**The new structure:** Europe President sits above Europe Laundry Division, which sits above a Germany Detergent GM, under whom sits an Ariel brand manager. Country-based product roles now exist *inside* each product category rather than product roles existing inside country divisions.

**What happened to the country managers?** Their freedom got curtailed -- and **many of them resigned.** By this point, Europe had become more of a common market, country-level trade barriers had come down, and real economies of scope and scale (especially in the supply chain) were finally achievable.

A back-and-forth in class helps pin down exactly what changed. One student notices the diagram doesn't clearly show where the country manager role went anymore. The professor admits the diagram is ambiguous, but another student finds the answer directly in the case text:

> "Country GMs were replaced with multiple country product GMs" -- positions were established and assigned continent-wide divisional profit and loss responsibility.

So the country manager didn't disappear -- it got **repositioned into a country-specific product GM role.** Germany still has its own Detergent GM (unlike the US, which has just one Detergent GM for the whole country) -- there's real **duplication of roles across countries**, even while the top-level classification has shifted from geography-first to product-category-first.

**Why no functional matrix here yet?** Introducing a product category layer into an *already product-based* US structure is straightforward -- it doesn't create too much disturbance. But introducing product category into an *otherwise geographical* structure is already a major disturbance on its own.

> "If you also introduce functional-level dotted-line relationships on top of that disturbance, it will create unbelievable complexity. You don't want to do that initially, and that is the reason why you have not done that."

The result: country-specific requirements on sales, distribution, marketing, and packaging remain intact, but the back end -- sourcing, in particular -- starts chasing genuine efficiencies.

## Part 7: The Global Matrix Structure -- and Its Strange Bypassing Dotted Line

The professor calls this next structure something that was once treated, wrongly, as the endpoint of organizational evolution:

> "At some point of time, this was considered the nirvana for any multinational structure -- that this is the structure towards which all companies evolve, the best structure possible. Which of course is not true."

**Why it exists:** the earlier structures were each poor at one specific thing -- **fast introduction of new products across markets.** P&G hoped a true global matrix, combining product, geography, and function all at once, could finally fix that.

**The structure, worked example (detergent, Germany):** Europe President sits above Europe Detergent VP, above Germany Detergent GM, above brand manager -- still, technically, a geographical divisional structure at the very top (Europe has its own Laundry Division). But in *every* practical sense, Europe itself now runs on a product-category basis internally.

**The new global layer:** a **Global Detergent Category Leader** now exists, with a **Global R&D Detergent VP** reporting solid-line to them (and dotted-line to the region-level R&D function).

**Here's the puzzle the professor puts to the class directly.** There's a dotted line running from the Global Detergent Category Leader all the way down to the Germany Detergent GM -- and it *bypasses* the Europe Detergent VP entirely. Why would you skip the more logical, adjacent level?

A student's first guess is knowledge and best-practice sharing. The professor says that's part of it, but the real driver is something sharper:

> "The primary objective of this structure and this particular dotted line relationship is to push country-level GMs to introduce new products -- because that was the biggest strategic objective. Successful products developed elsewhere -- like this diaper brand, Pampers, which took 11 years to get introduced across the world. Had it been launched earlier, look at how much lost profitability and lost revenue."

**Why country GMs resist new launches:** new products hurt near-term profitability, and country GMs are judged on exactly that. So how do you convince them? Through **leverage, not authority.**

> "Any product-level R&D that a country GM wants access to -- that person will have to go to the Global Detergent Category Leader and convince them. And that is where the global leader will counter-negotiate: I will handle your R&D if you launch this new product in your geography."

Once the country GM agrees and launches, ownership of that product -- and the KPI risk if it fails -- transfers entirely to them. The global leader's job is done; they don't have to babysit the rollout.

> "Please understand this is still a negotiated process. This will still take time. This is not a very fast, automatic launch happening across geographies. Those geographical heads can still resist. But at least there was nobody who was pushing for new product launches across the globe before. Now there is one dotted-line relationship that is pushing for that."

### Centralized R&D vs. Localized R&D -- The Lemon Example

A student, Manish, pushes back: doesn't the Detergent R&D director in Europe sit parallel to, not under, the Detergent GM? Would they even cooperate?

The professor clarifies there are genuinely **two different tiers of R&D**:

- **Major, product-level R&D** (like the historic shift from chemical-based to enzyme-based washing powder) is handled only in P&G's **centralized R&D laboratories** -- this never happens at the regional level.
- **Small, localized R&D** -- fragrance tweaks and similar cultural adaptations -- can happen locally, but only within a narrow band, because local labs simply don't have the budget, equipment, or capability for anything bigger.

> The professor's own vivid example: Vim (a P&G dish-cleaning brand in India) leans heavily on a lemon fragrance, because Indians associate sour lemon with making steel utensils brighter. In Europe, where dishware is glass-based, that association means nothing at all -- so "which fragrance you use" becomes a purely localized R&D and adaptation question, never a central R&D topic.

For anything bigger than that, the country GM has to convince the global category head -- who then weighs two things: **how replicable is this change elsewhere** (scope/scale value), and **can I use this as leverage to push a new product into this geography.**

## Part 8: Organization 2005 -- The Most Radical Structure of All

The professor introduces the final structure with real drama: launched in **1999**, officially called **Organization 2005**, so controversial that the CEO who launched it **got fired** over it. His successor chose not to blame the structure, stuck with it, and by around **2005** it was proven successful.

> "It was as far as I know, apart from P&G, I don't think there is any major organization that has been successful in adopting this kind of structure. HL copied this structure, tried to manage it for two years, and then went back to its original global matrix structure."

**What makes it radical:** the matrix is gone entirely -- **there is effectively no dotted line anywhere.** The structure still has three dimensions -- geography, product, and function -- but instead of overlapping them into one matrix, P&G split them into **three entirely separate, parallel organizations**, operating like independent companies under one corporate umbrella.

| Unit | Owns | Does Not Own |
|---|---|---|
| **GBUs (Global Business Units)** | Product dimension -- R&D, manufacturing, product profitability | Direct control over sales, pricing, and distribution |
| **MDOs (Market Development Organizations)** | Geography/adaptation dimension -- revenue, sales, marketing, local adaptation | Manufacturing or R&D |
| **GBS (Global Business Services)** | Functional dimension -- shared services like IT/ERP consolidation (54 different ERPs globally rationalized into one) and global supply chain | Product or geography-level decisions |

> "PandG has now got three different companies. One company is designing and manufacturing products. It hands over those products to sales and distribution companies, which ensure sales happen in specific geographies, along with any local adaptation. And then there are these global functions, ensuring the entire company remains cost efficient. It's a very unique structure -- no textbook, nowhere else will you find this kind of structure."

### The Conflict-Resolution Problem, Made Concrete

The professor draws out a specific worked example: in Germany, there's a GM Sales under the MDO, and a separate GM Manufacturing under the GBU. If there's a conflict or negotiation between these two, **where does it get resolved?**

> "They meet only at the highest level -- that is at the CEO level. Can you see that? This is a very difficult structure. If there is a lower-level conflict, there is no way that conflict gets resolved at that level through the organizational structure itself."

**The CEO's actual stance**, as described in class, was blunt and deliberately harsh:

> "Any conflict that would reach up to the CEO's level, the CEO would say: I will not intervene here. If a second time such conflicts come to me, you will get fired."

**How the company actually made this work,** since the formal structure offered zero help:

- Heavy **people-level transfers** between GBUs and MDOs, so managers directly experience "the other side's reality" before returning to their own unit.
- Deliberate cultivation of **personal-level networks** across the two vertical organizations, so that coordination happens informally, at the working level, rather than being pushed up.

> "It requires lots of interpersonal relations and interpersonal networks at the lower level. Then this structure will work."

A student presses further: doesn't the CEO's "don't come back twice" rule effectively force a *hidden* matrix, just an informal one instead of a formal one? The professor agrees, with an important distinction:

> "Yes, but those are interpersonal networks rather than a formal, structural reporting relationship, which can become a kind of ritual, or which can create some kind of power struggle. Here, in that interpersonal network, if something doesn't get solved, there is no point of having that particular relationship -- it is crucial for that person's survival."

He adds a further nuance: previous dotted lines in the case were all **vertical** (one function to another, up a hierarchy). This new informal coordination is **horizontal** -- from a GBU role directly to its matching MDO role, more like a buyer-supplier relationship inside the same company.

> "I need something from you, you need something from me. Let us solve this issue at our level, because both of us will benefit if we solve it at our level. Why push it up to the hierarchy? It will not get solved there."

### The Professor's Honest Skepticism

The professor is unusually candid about the limits of his own confidence in this final structure:

> "As a theory, I have never seen such a structure, and there is no theory that proposes such a structure. But the proof is in the pudding -- because this company made this structure successful, that is the reason we are saying it can succeed. Otherwise it is very difficult to believe that such a structure succeeds."

He goes further, offering a genuinely personal, unverified prediction:

> "My second bet is that, by now, a lot of dotted lines would have come back. That is my bet. I seriously doubt this structure would have got preserved in this exact form. But I have no proof for that -- I don't have data after 2006, because getting this kind of high-level access to create such a case is not possible, at least for me."

**One more genuinely important nuance,** raised by a student and confirmed by the professor: unlike every earlier P&G structure, where one dimension was always clearly dominant and the other two subordinated to it, **Organization 2005 claims all three dimensions are equally important -- with no subordinate dimension at all.**

The professor pushes back gently on how "equal" this really is:

> "Although I believe that ultimately the dimension that has got the profit responsibility is still the most powerful dimension. But can you see that dimension cannot achieve profitability on its own? It has it is dependent on the sales revenue and adaptation of the MDO."

That mutual dependence -- GBU needs MDO for revenue, MDO needs GBU for product -- is simultaneously the structure's greatest strength and its most fragile point, because **the actual resolution mechanism for that dependence is left entirely to informal, interpersonal relationships rather than any formal structure.**

> "That's a very questionable way to create an organization structure. It worked during this era, fine. But can it work for the long term? Would a company this large be able to maintain three vertical companies within the same company indefinitely? I seriously doubt it -- but for that I would need additional data I simply don't have."

**The meta-lesson of Session 12:** structure is never solved once -- it is re-solved every time strategy changes. P&G moved from land-grab (product structure), to market penetration (country/geographical structure), to cost-efficiency (matrix and product-category layers), to speed-to-market for new products (global matrix), to genuine simultaneous balance across all three dimensions (Organization 2005) -- and every single move traded one kind of problem for another. No structure is ever complete or perfect; the only real question, echoed across the whole session, is whether a given structure's benefits outweigh its costs for the strategic problem you actually have right now.

| **Centralized vs. Localized R&D** | Major R&D breakthroughs (e.g., enzyme-based detergent) stay centralized; only minor local adaptations (e.g., fragrance) are handled locally, due to budget/capability limits |
| **Organization 2005** | Launched 1999, radical structure splitting P&G into three independent, parallel organizations: GBUs, MDOs, and GBS -- with no formal dotted lines at all |
| **GBU (Global Business Unit)** | Owns product dimension: R&D, manufacturing, product profitability -- no direct control over sales or pricing |
| **MDO (Market Development Organization)** | Owns geography/adaptation dimension: sales, distribution, marketing, local revenue |
| **GBS (Global Business Services)** | Owns functional dimension: shared services like consolidating 54 global ERPs and managing global supply chain |
| **"Don't Come to Me Twice" Rule** | The Organization 2005 CEO's stance that unresolved conflicts reaching him a second time would get the manager fired, forcing informal resolution |
| **Interpersonal/Horizontal Networks** | Organization 2005's real conflict-resolution mechanism -- personal relationships and cross-unit transfers, replacing any formal reporting linkage |
| **HL's Two-Year Experiment** | A Unilever entity that copied Organization 2005, tried it for two years, and reverted to a standard global matrix structure |

## The Real-World Takeaway

Priya had been at the company for exactly fourteen months when she started keeping two calendars -- one for Rahul, her country marketing head in Mumbai, and one for Lena, her global brand lead sitting in a Frankfurt office six time zones away. On paper, both were her boss. In practice, every Tuesday felt like she worked for two different companies that happened to share a logo.

It came to a head over a shampoo relaunch. Rahul wanted the bottle redesigned around a jasmine scent and a price point that would move volume in tier-two Indian cities before the festive season. Lena wanted the exact same global packaging rolled out unchanged, because the brand's worldwide research said consistency was driving loyalty in every other market. Priya sat in a call with both of them, muted on her end, watching Rahul's Hindi-inflected urgency crash into Lena's calm German insistence on "the brand architecture," and she remembers thinking: I am the rope in a tug-of-war, and both sides are pulling hard enough that something in the middle is about to snap.

For a few weeks, she did what most people in her seat do -- she quietly told Rahul what he wanted to hear, quietly told Lena what she wanted to hear, and hoped the two versions never sat in the same room. It worked until it didn't. Lena found out about the jasmine variant from a regional sales deck, not from Priya, and the trust drained out of that relationship in about four minutes.

What actually fixed it wasn't a heart-to-heart or a personality fix. It was her new VP, a blunt woman named Ines, sitting the three of them down and doing something almost boringly simple: she wrote out, on an actual whiteboard, which decisions belonged to Rahul alone, which belonged to Lena alone, and which required both sign-offs -- and for that last bucket, she named who broke the tie if they couldn't agree within one week. Packaging and fragrance: local call, Rahul's. Core formula and global positioning: Lena's call, no debate. Pricing architecture: joint, and if unresolved in seven days, it escalated to Ines and stayed there, not in Priya's inbox.

Priya still reports to two people. That part never changed. But she stopped being the one absorbing the collision, because the collision now had an actual address to go to instead of landing on her desk disguised as "just loop me in." The jasmine variant shipped, with global packaging cues intact, on a timeline both bosses had actually agreed to in writing.

**A matrix without a rulebook turns your best people into shock absorbers -- give the tug-of-war a referee, and it becomes a system instead of a fight.**
