# Session 11: Matsushita's Mirror Image, and P&G's First Divisional Structure

*Class opens with a quick MCQ clarification, then finishes the Philips vs. Matsushita story by unpacking Matsushita's side -- a 250-year business plan, a founder's audacity, and a structure that is almost the exact mirror image of Philips's. A Panasonic veteran in the room adds nine years of real insider detail. Then the professor pivots to a brand-new, much-loved case -- Procter & Gamble -- and opens it with the very first structure P&G ever built: the 1955 US product-division structure. The session closes on a rich tangent about why new CEOs love to restructure everything they touch.*

Before starting, two students raised doubts about the midterm multiple-choice questions.

**Doubt 1 -- the Tata/arbitrage question.** A student pointed out a typo: one answer option involving "arbitrage" wasn't actually available as a choice, so the question felt confusing. The professor agreed there was a typo but held firm that it changed nothing about the correct answer:

> "The best answer was still the same... one strategy is suboptimal, then two, and three is better than two. So I agreed there was a typo, but it did not lead to any other answer."

**Doubt 2 -- Porter's attractiveness test and capability.** A student asked how an "unattractive industry" answer could still be correct if the acquirer's own capability mattered. The professor's answer: capability is *already baked into* Porter's test.

> Porter's attractiveness test isn't just "is the industry attractive on its own" -- a capable acquirer can *make* an unattractive industry attractive. Either the new business must add value to the existing portfolio, or the corporate office must add value to the new business. Either way, there has to be synergy -- and that synergy question is where capability lives inside the test.

The professor also used this moment to make a bigger point about course design:

> "If the two diversification cases I taught aren't consistent with a prominent framework in the field, what's the point of teaching either the framework or the cases? They have to be consistent."

## Part 1: Matsushita -- The Direct Mirror of Philips

### The Founder's Audacity: A 250-Year Business Plan

Most real business plans run 3-5 years; the professor said he'd "never even heard of" one longer than 7 years. Matsushita's founder set a **250-year business plan**, broken into **25-year blocks**.

> Give credit where it's due: in the very first 25-year block, Matsushita overtook Philips and became the category leader in products like the VCR. That's a genuinely commendable achievement.

### Why Japan Internationalized: The Post-War Story

A student (Manish) opened with two reasons: **domestic decline after the war**, and it being **part of the second 25-year plan**. The professor built out the full historical picture underneath that answer.

Think of Japan right after World War II as a country with world-class manufacturing muscle and absolutely nowhere to flex it:

- Japan had joined the Axis (with Germany and Italy), attacked the US, fought a long, bitter war, and surrendered after the two atomic bombings.
- Post-war US controls plus **hyperinflation** crashed the domestic market completely.
- But Japan's manufacturing and engineering capability -- honed hard during the war, when most of the country's capacity was diverted to war production -- was still intact.
- The **yen was extremely weak**, and Japan desperately needed foreign currency (dollars) to survive and grow -- this was still the gold-standard era, before full dollarization.

In response, Japan built a **centralized, export-driven push**, through a ministry (the professor referenced something like MITI, comparable to a Ministry of Commerce/Industry) that decided which business groups could produce how much of what, and how much foreign currency exporting would earn them.

Because Japanese plants were centralized inside a low-currency, high-manufacturing-capability country, and because Matsushita itself was nicknamed **"Manishita" -- meaning copycat or fast follower** -- the company would rapidly re-engineer whatever succeeded elsewhere and relaunch it. Honda and other big Japanese names first targeted the US, then Europe, as European market barriers began coming down.

> "Till that moment, Western arrogance was such that their business models, their management know-how, their way of running factories, were considered far too sophisticated to ever be conquered. Japan proved them completely wrong -- and that shook Western confidence." The professor pointed to the famous **Honda A and Honda B case series**, where the British government commissioned BCG to figure out why British motorcycle manufacturers were being pushed out of the US market entirely.

**First mode of internationalization: primarily Aggregation, boosted by home-country Arbitrage.** The weak yen made producing in Japan cost-efficient purely on exchange-rate grounds. On top of that, Japanese firms pioneered **total quality management and continuous improvement** -- practices barely known anywhere else at the time -- creating a second layer of cost advantage rooted purely in *being* in Japan, with Japanese employees and Japanese management practice.

### Manish's Follow-Up: Why Relocate to East Asia Later?

Manish pushed further: Japan later relocated basic-component manufacturing to East Asian countries while keeping advanced components in Japan -- **was that by choice?**

The professor's answer, dug out of a dense case: **it was forced, not chosen.**

- Countries buying from Japan started running large **balance-of-payment deficits** (the professor compared it directly to China's export-heavy trade relationships today) and began erecting barriers, forcing Japan to open local plants just to keep market access.
- So this relocation was really a **forced adaptation**, triggered by regulatory pressure -- not a deliberate arbitrage strategy.
- It likely *did* produce some incidental arbitrage benefit anyway, since the yen was appreciating fast while East Asian currencies stayed weak -- but that benefit eroded over time.

### The Matsushita Organizational Structure

Even after being forced into some manufacturing decentralization, Matsushita's setup stayed **heavily centralized** -- but its internal wiring was structured completely differently from Philips's.

| Layer | Role | Philips equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Central Research Laboratories (CRL)** | Centralized, like Philips's R&D | Philips's R&D |
| **Product Divisions (PDs)** | The *most powerful* entities in the whole company -- had a strong say in both R&D priorities and product development | Philips's Product Divisions, which had almost no power |
| **Marketing organization** | Separate but well-connected, handling sales and distribution in each geography, feeding local ideas back through internal channels | Philips's National Organizations |

A student (Manish) explained the key mechanism: **CRL was not fully funded by the corporate center** -- part of its funding came from the Product Divisions themselves. That financial dependency forced CRL to develop what PDs actually wanted.

> The professor sharpened this: the primary driver wasn't really "adaptability." It was that **PDs held the purse strings**, so CRL had little choice but to cooperate -- and since capable PD leaders eventually rose into corporate management themselves, they informally pressured everyone else to fall in line too.

Another student (Vikas) added the missing piece versus Philips: Matsushita's PD had **strong bonding both with marketing and with R&D** -- a genuine triangle -- whereas Philips's National Organizations only had a weak link to a separate, disconnected R&D.

> "If you're going to be a copycat or fast follower, you need that tight linkage. You have to identify a successful idea fast, reverse-engineer it fast, conduct R&D fast, launch fast, and scale fast. That's a *must* for any fast follower."

### Adaptability: Matsushita's Real Weakness

A student asked directly: where does local adaptability actually get built into this loop? The professor's answer was blunt -- **adaptability was incorporated, but not well.**

> By the end of the case's second 25-year block (around 2010-11), even Matsushita's own corporate management admitted the company was fine at adaptation and somewhat okay at arbitrage -- **adaptation to specific market realities was its clear weakness.**

### The Mirror Image, Stated Directly

This is the single most important comparison of the session:

> "Philips was very good at adaptation, but not so good at aggregation. Matsushita was very good at aggregation, but had weaknesses in adaptation. And one company was very poor at arbitrage because it was never forced into it -- while the other was forced into arbitrage but never took full advantage of it. From the arbitrage angle both aren't that different. But on aggregation and adaptation, they are a mirror image of each other."

| | Philips | Matsushita |
|---|---|---|
| **Aggregation** | Weak | Strong |
| **Adaptation** | Strong (often forced) | Weak |
| **Arbitrage** | Weak (never forced into it) | Forced into it, but never fully capitalized on it |

### The Panasonic Insider's Testimony (Anish)

A student who had worked at Panasonic for nine years, and also at LG, shared direct DNA-level detail:

- Matsushita's structure was built roughly 100 years ago around **manufacturing**, and it worked beautifully through the 1980s-90s -- the founding belief was: *protect manufacturing and R&D, and market success follows.*
- **China's 1977 liberalization** eventually forced the company to shift operational core competencies toward China, step by step, while keeping core development and corporate identity (Matsushita/Panasonic) at home.
- The critical flaw: **Matsushita never gave real importance to its sales subsidiaries.**

**The LG vs. Panasonic margin story, told in class:** when LG manufactures something (say, a compressor) and passes it to its own sales subsidiary, it takes only a thin margin -- around **7 percent** -- letting the local subsidiary keep the rest to stay competitive locally. Panasonic did the opposite: it secured its **full profit at the manufacturing level first**, and that inflated number became the base cost the sales subsidiary had to build customs duties and everything else on top of.

> "That was the main default -- or fault line -- with Matsushita, because of which many plants have also been shut down, and everything effectively moved to China."

The professor's response sharpened the diagnosis: this wasn't just about pricing, it was about **never letting the local/regional part of the organization build its own competitiveness or retain any profit.** He added a very current data point: after the case ends (2010-11), Matsushita reportedly **sold a majority stake to a Chinese company** -- recent news at the time of the class, reflecting exactly this cost-competitiveness struggle against Chinese rivals.

**A tangent on LG and Samsung today:** the professor speculated that even these two Korean giants have lost some of their old grip -- more fragmented competition now, from premium players like Bosch above and low-cost challengers below, with white-goods margins broadly saturated. Anish, drawing on direct market knowledge, corrected the record with real numbers:

- **LG has strong profitability**; Samsung is struggling in consumer electronics and survives mainly on mobiles.
- **LG's market share in home appliances: roughly 18-20 percent** -- a decent share.
- **LG's market share in home entertainment: roughly 12-13 percent.**

### The Profit-Split Rule and the "Only Japanese" Positions

Manish raised two more details straight from the case:

> **Matsushita subsidiaries had to transfer 60 percent of profit to headquarters, and could retain only 40 percent for working capital and fixed assets.**

He also noted Matsushita was **the first company in Japan to adopt a divisional structure**, and flagged something telling: certain overseas subsidiary positions were **reserved exclusively for Japanese nationals** -- regardless of a local employee's fluency in Japanese or familiarity with the culture. Manish's read: this either constrained local adaptation directly, or reflected a deeper central control instinct that prevented real local adaptation from ever happening.

### Why Divisional Structure Matters (The Professor's Aside)

The professor used this moment to zoom out on structure logic in general -- a preview of the P&G discussion to come:

- A pure **functional structure** struggles to adapt to a specific product-market and struggles to build economies of scope across related product categories.
- Being an early adopter of a **product division structure** let Matsushita capture economies of scope, focus on market share, and make fast, coordinated, cross-functional decisions for each product category.
- A **geographical division structure**, by contrast, is great at local adaptability but poor at transferring success from one market to another, or capturing global scale/scope.
- **Divisional structures are modular** -- you can plug in new businesses (or new geographies) relatively easily -- but they duplicate resources and sacrifice some functional-level scale.
- The balance is achievable through **shared services** (functions genuinely shared across divisions) and **global sourcing/technology/IT functions**.

> "Which dimension is the *most* important dimension for you? For Philips, it was the geographical dimension. For Matsushita, it was the product dimension."

### "It Depends" -- There Is No One Best Strategy

A student (Prus) asked directly: for market-responsive innovation, is centralized or localized R&D the better approach? The professor's answer became one of the session's most repeated lines:

> "By now you should be very clear about one thing: in strategy, the only right answer is *it depends.* No one strategy overpowers everything else. Each strategy has strengths and weaknesses, and each can be attacked by another strategy."

- Philips's adaptation strategy (by choice, chance, or force) was attacked by Matsushita's aggregation strategy.
- A country-driven globalization model naturally leans toward aggregation, and aggressive export growth only works when your own currency is weak -- so it's really a **combination of aggregation and arbitrage.**
- **Can you sell a standardized product worldwide?** That's the key variable. Products like VCRs became globally standardized (agreed cassette sizes, agreed technology standards) -- so aggregation won that battle over adaptation, because the adaptation upside had already been engineered away.
- But wherever *some* adaptation would genuinely help, that becomes a source of competitive advantage over rivals who don't adapt.

> "Sometimes an intelligent combination of two or three approaches is what matters most. If one factor gives you 80 percent of the advantage, how do you layer in another factor for the remaining 20 percent? That's the real question. We are not saying Matsushita should abandon its aggregation advantage -- we're asking: while keeping aggregation, can it also get better at adaptation, better at arbitrage?"

### Panasonic's 2005-10 Decentralization Experiment (Anish's Second Story)

Anish shared another real insider story: from **2005 to around 2008-09**, Panasonic went to **complete decentralization**, turning every vertical (TV, washing machines, microwaves, audio) into its own fully separate profit center -- separate capex, separate opex, entirely independent of the Panasonic corporate parent.

**The "Technics" example:** Panasonic had two verticals making audio -- one for home consumer durables, one for auto (with a strong Toyota deal for Camry, etc.). When the home-audio Technics brand was revived in 2015, the team spent **millions of dollars** on R&D for something that -- it turned out -- **already existed** inside the company's own auto-audio division.

> "It's because of the silos and 100 percent decentralization. It was a major issue, and we wasted millions of dollars."

The professor immediately generalized this into a textbook example:

> "This is a great example of the weakness of a pure divisional structure -- 100 percent divisional, with tight vertical silos that don't talk to each other at all. There's no way to transfer knowledge or share resources across them. That's exactly when this kind of duplication problem arises."

Anish added one more real detail: from **2008 onward**, when new leadership (referred to as "Kamura") took over, **internal power politics** intensified significantly -- also a major contributing factor.

### Why the Professor Teaches Unfamiliar Companies, Not Yours

This sparked a genuinely reflective detour. In corporate training programs, employees often ask: *why not teach cases from our own company?* The professor's answer is a mini-lesson in itself:

> "Any company has extremely rich, deep internal detail -- leadership changes, internal politics, subsidiary-level trouble -- that has nothing to do with the topic you're studying. If you're too close to a case, you get stuck explaining away every failure with 'oh, but the *real* problem was this political issue,' and you never reach the underlying pattern."

The technique being taught is called **analogical reasoning**: understand an unfamiliar setting through an abstracted framework, then **re-contextualize that same abstracted learning** into your own, familiar company. Too much familiarity with the source case breaks this transfer process.

> As an example, the professor mentioned another internationalization case, **Kendall Chemical**, dense with personal-level detail (unsuitable leaders, mismatched boards) that can confuse a reader for pages -- "but the moment you apply the AAA framework, everything becomes crystal clear: the problem is that adaptation was needed but the company was aggregating, or aggregation was needed but the company was adapting."

### Chirag's TV Example: Why "Generic" Products Still Need Adaptation

A student pushed back: didn't Philips mostly sell generic products that didn't need much country-level differentiation? The professor firmly disagreed, using **color TV** as the running example, and listed out multiple, very real adaptation triggers:

1. **Customer taste differences**: cabinet-style TVs versus free-standing TVs; large versus small screen preferences (varying across Canada, US, Australia).
2. **Technical standards**: PAL versus NTSC transmission -- forcing hardware compatibility differences by country.
3. **Local retail/market realities**: in 1980s India, TVs were sold through small specialized shops, often with long waits, black-and-white transmission only, and just one channel available -- completely different from other markets.
4. **Local competitive dynamics**: a competitor with a hugely popular brand in an adjacent category (branding, distribution economies of scope) enters your category and becomes highly cost-competitive locally -- forcing a localized response.

> "Adaptiveness can be thrust upon you for multiple reasons -- but that doesn't mean you convert your entire aggregation into adaptation. It means you preserve your aggregation *and* allow adaptation to happen alongside it."

### Hotstar and Amazon: Adaptation Failures, Repeated with New Numbers

The professor returned to two live examples (first raised in Session 10), this time with sharper numbers:

**Disney+ Hotstar:** Hotstar was valued at **15 billion dollars** when Disney acquired 21st Century Fox (which brought Hotstar along as part of the portfolio). Disney's apathy pushed out the two prominent local leaders who had actually made Hotstar successful; Disney then let go of key content deals (HBO) and lost IPL broadcast rights. Combine all of it, and Hotstar's valuation collapsed to roughly **1 billion dollars**, forcing Disney to sell **70 percent of its stake to Jio**.

> "That's a complete failure of not having adaptation and local leadership."

**Amazon:** Amazon failed to respond to India's quick-commerce challenge at the right time and is now playing catch-up, because **local leadership moved to the US**, and the new CEO showed far less interest in e-commerce than in the AI business, largely ignoring what the Indian team was flagging.

> "In that process, Amazon is now a laggard."

**The synthesis point:** genuine adaptiveness gets forced on you by local technology standards, regulation, competitive dynamics, and customer preference -- but the answer is never to abandon aggregation entirely. **Disney+ combining its own global content-sourcing power with local adaptation (a Hotstar-style local play in India)** would let it compete far better than either extreme alone.

> "We will spend much more time on centralization versus decentralization in the next case. When we say strategy *and* structure, structure is really all about managing the tension between centralization and decentralization -- one of the major tensions structure exists to manage."

### Matsushita: Strengths, Organizational Mechanisms, and the Innovation Trap

**Strategic competence:**
- High economies of scale, initially from centralized Japanese manufacturing, later supplemented (only partially) by East Asian relocation.
- Fast-follower speed to market for innovations.

**Organizational mechanisms behind it:**
- Strong, centralized Product Divisions, with R&D partially funded by the corporate office and partially by PDs -- keeping R&D-to-PD linkage strong.
- PDs stayed connected to the market through **two channels**: direct signal capture from **25,000 franchisee shops**, and internal merchandise shows where overseas country managers showcased new product ideas.
- But **PDs had final say** on whether to invest in any new idea -- and being scale-and-scope focused, they systematically deprioritized low-scale, country-specific ideas, even good ones.

**Why the East Asian relocation advantage was smaller than it should have been:** Matsushita forced its foreign subsidiaries to source components entirely from Japanese factories. As Japan's own cost structure rose, those components became expensive -- eating away the low-cost advantage those subsidiaries should have enjoyed.

**Why subsidiaries never became genuinely innovative,** despite top management's wishes -- a full anatomy of control:

- Subsidiary heads were **appointed from Japan.**
- A middle-level manager or accounting person from Japan was embedded as "the eyes and ears" of headquarters.
- **A senior/mid-level Japanese manager visited every single day of the year.**
- Subsidiaries had **zero strategic planning freedom** without headquarters approval.
- They had to **source all components from Japan.**
- They could not enter a new product category without **Product Division approval.**

> "There is no real independence, no adaptability, through the organizational mechanism built for these subsidiaries. They can't innovate because the whole environment is extremely tight-control-oriented and suffocating for any kind of innovation. The moment you deviate from the numbers, you'll face very tough questions."

**The macro blow: the Plaza Accord.** Post oil-shock, and after US pressure (the **Plaza Accord**), the yen appreciated significantly, ending Japan's cost competitiveness in a phenomenon the professor described as the reverse of stagflation -- essentially **no growth plus a strong currency**, which persisted for roughly **30-34 years**. Slower to adapt than they should have been, this let **South Korean competitors** start competing seriously against Japanese firms.

> "Why such organizational disability? Weak national subsidiaries lacking both adaptability and innovativeness. Philips had great adaptability but sacrificed aggregation and arbitrage. Matsushita had great aggregation and (initially) arbitrage, but its arbitrage advantage suffered because of tight control requirements, and adaptability in terms of new product development was simply missing."

### Could Matsushita Have Just Acquired Innovative Companies?

Manish asked directly: for the adaptation gap, couldn't Matsushita have simply acquired innovative local companies? The professor's answer was a firm **"difficult"**:

> "It's very difficult, when you're so controlling-oriented from your headquarters, to let somebody work independently. Sometimes that fails -- you need to build trust in local leadership instead. There's a very tight cultural control in many Japanese companies, and that doesn't work well with high innovativeness."

He extended this into a broader, candid opinion about Japanese corporate culture:

> "Japanese companies are considered world-class at quality, process improvement, and discipline -- extremely good at that. But are they very creative, out-of-the-box, entrepreneurial risk-takers, capable of quantum jumps in innovativeness? Maybe some are -- but that's not their natural organizational-level strength. They'll quickly convert any creative spark into a systematic, process-driven success story, rather than letting it survive as a raw entrepreneurial jump -- which is exactly what you'll see in Silicon Valley firms instead."

> He cited Meta's own stated philosophy as the polar opposite mindset: **"we break and we grow."**

## Part 2: A New Case Begins -- Procter & Gamble (P&G)

The professor called P&G **"one of the best cases available"** to learn structure -- long, dense, but deeply rewarding by the end. It teaches a structure the professor says **no other company has successfully replicated** -- Unilever reportedly tried to copy it and had to revert to a global matrix structure instead.

> Timeline covered: **1950s to 2006.** Across that span, P&G will change structure repeatedly, and every single change tracks directly to a change in corporate strategy.

### Diagram 1: The 1955 US Structure

The professor showed the case's first diagram (US divisional structure, 1955) and asked the class to identify the structure type. Students initially guessed **divisional** (by product/segment), then **functional**, then briefly **matrix** -- before converging on the right description.

**How to read this kind of diagram:** look at the level directly below the CEO/President -- that determines the structure's *name*. Boxes with **bold outlines** denote **P&L (profit) responsibility.**

Below the **US President** sat three product-category Vice Presidents: **Foods**, **Toilet Goods**, and **Soaps & Detergents.** Below each VP sat individual **brands** (Tide and Ariel both sit under the "washing powder" product category, for instance). And underneath each *division*, every one of the core functions repeats: **R&D, brand management, manufacturing, and sales & distribution.**

> **This is a Product Division structure, with a Functional structure nested inside each division.**

### The Three Structural Dimensions (A Framework for Everything That Follows)

The professor introduced a framework that will recur across the entire case:

| Dimension | What It Delivers |
|---|---|
| **Product dimension** | Economies of scope (and some scale) |
| **Geographical dimension** | Local adaptability |
| **Functional dimension** | Economies of scale and functional-level efficiency |

> "In every one of these structures, one dimension will be dominant over the others. Here, product is dominant, with function nested under it."

### Why Duplicate Functions Across Divisions At All?

Under this structure, each division runs its own dedicated manufacturing, R&D, and sales -- no sharing across, say, Soaps & Detergents and Toilet Goods.

**Why accept the duplication?** Because it eliminates **inter-divisional conflict** (no fighting over shared plants, shared budgets, shared capacity) and makes each division's decisions fast and fully coordinated at the product level -- essentially letting each division run as if it were its **own single-business company, with its own CEO.**

> "There are many CEOs here of their own single-business company -- but this single-business company happens to be part of a multi-business corporation." (Confirmed in class: yes, these are effectively **verticals.**)

A student (Chirag) asked directly whether R&D and sales might sometimes be shared across products. The professor's answer distinguished two very different layers:

- **Basic/fundamental research** sits at the highest functional level and is a genuine **shared service** -- like corporate tax, legal, and HR, used by every division.
- **Operational, product-specific R&D and manufacturing** are duplicated separately inside each division -- Soaps & Detergents has its own R&D team, Toilet Goods has an entirely separate one.

The student pointed out the obvious cost problem, and the professor agreed instantly:

> "Yes -- definitely. This is not a cost-efficient structure. And innovation from one division may not circulate to another." (Exactly the failure Anish's Panasonic "Technics" story illustrated earlier in the session.)

**When would you club two categories into one division instead?** When they need very high coordination or share heavy common resources (like a shared R&D base) -- otherwise, keeping them separate lets each respond fully to its own distinct competitive dynamics: different competitors, different differentiation levers, different advertising logic, different channels, different regulatory realities.

### Why Isn't Everything Under the Brand Manager?

If a **brand manager** owns brand-level profitability (Ariel's manager owns Ariel's numbers, Tide's manager owns Tide's numbers), why doesn't that manager also control R&D, manufacturing, and sales hierarchically?

> Because giving each *brand* its own full functional stack would be **unbelievably costly** -- you'd lose all the shared-resource efficiency that exists at the product-division level. So functions sit *parallel* to brand managers, not underneath them.

Instead, the brand manager's real job is **daily cross-functional coordination**: ensuring enough sales push and marketing pull, no stockouts from manufacturing, and R&D finishing new product launches on schedule -- driving profitable sales without holding hierarchical authority over any of those functions.

### The Strategic Objective Behind This Structure: Land-Grab

The professor asked: under what strategic objective does this exact structure make sense? His answer:

> **"Grow as much as possible, and capture as much market share as possible. Don't worry about cost."**

In 1955, competition wasn't stiff, the total addressable market was expanding fast (the post-war baby boom), and whoever captured the most territory early would hold a durable advantage once that expansion eventually slowed down. Under this structure, **Tide and Ariel's brand managers could -- and did -- compete against each other**, with no guarantee of cooperation. At the time, **P&G was completely fine with that.**

### Why This Worked in the US but Wouldn't in Europe

This structure fit the **US** because the US was a large, **homogeneous market** -- no state-by-state barriers, no meaningfully different regulations or customer preferences to adapt to. **Europe, by contrast, was highly heterogeneous** -- different languages, trade barriers, customs, product and packaging preferences -- meaning a pure product-division/aggregation approach wouldn't work there at all. (This European contrast is exactly what next session will unpack.)

### Manish's Question: Is Divisional Structure Always the Default?

> **Yes, for most multi-business enterprises**, because divisional structure is **modular** -- new businesses can be plugged in relatively easily. A functional structure, by contrast, gets disrupted every time you add a new business, and as hierarchy grows, inter-functional conflict becomes very hard to manage.

But if a single dimension (product, geography, or function) isn't enough on its own, you need a more complex, **matrix-type** structure that coordinates across multiple dimensions at once -- exactly what P&G will be forced into later in the case, as it has to coordinate product, geography, *and* function simultaneously.

**One clarifying nuance Manish raised:** technically, even the 1955 US structure is *nominally* geographical at the very top (the "US President" title is a geography label) -- but if you isolate just the US market itself, underneath that title, it's a **pure product structure.** The US being a homogeneous market is precisely what made a pure product structure feasible there.

## Part 3: Why New CEOs Love to Restructure Everything

With time running short, the discussion drifted into a rich, real-world tangent about organizational change and CEO politics -- sparked by students sharing their own company experiences.

**Manish's observation:** he's seen a pattern where every new CEO changes everything and labels the old regime's practices "the big bath" or something similar.

**The P&G story, foreshadowed:** the professor revealed that P&G itself went through exactly this. When a new CEO took over from a fired predecessor, he chose *not* to blame the structure:

> "That person was very wise. He absorbed the pressure, protected the people in his organization, and understood the real problem was industry cyclicality and a bubble burst -- not the organizational structure. He gave the new structure a chance to prove its own worth. It so happened that the structure P&G eventually adopted appears in no textbook, no competitor, nowhere else -- a genuinely radical idea. But the CEO was mature enough to stick with it, and by around 2005, he was proven right, and P&G became successful again."

**Why do new CEOs restructure regardless?** The professor laid out the underlying change-management logic plainly, treating it as a separate topic worth flagging even if not fully taught here:

- Restructuring **destabilizes existing, entrenched power equations** -- which, deliberately or not, hands the incoming CEO more relative power.
- It sends a **strong internal signal** that "we mean business," creating enough confusion that nobody can clearly tell whether results are improving or not -- useful cover, internally and externally.
- It gives the CEO room to **deflect blame** later onto technology shifts, regulation, or competitors, rather than themselves.
- Under a genuine **turnaround mandate** (survival pressure, stagnation, board anxiety), CEOs typically **fire the first layer, promote select second-layer people, and bring in their own loyalists from outside** -- ensuring the people reporting to them are either brand new or have had their loyalty secured.
- How much power a new CEO can wield depends partly on **how much reputation they bring with them.** The professor's example: if **Jack Welch** joined some other company as CEO, his personal reputation alone would transfer enormous power to that new organization.

> "There is no perfect structure. It will never happen -- there will always be some problem with whatever you do. The question is only whether what you're doing creates more benefit than problems. As long as that's true, you adopt that structure."

A student shared a direct parallel from their own company: a new leader completely reshuffled everything two years ago; the sector has since stabilized, and leadership is now celebrating a four-year strategic vision -- "I hope by that time the CEO's tenure will be over" -- while noting every new CEO they've observed follows this same restructuring pattern.

**The meta-lesson of Session 11:** there is no universally superior internationalization strategy or organizational structure -- only fit-to-context. Matsushita and Philips are mirror images precisely because each optimized hard for one dimension (aggregation vs. adaptation) at the expense of the other, and each got attacked exactly where it was weak. The same logic carries straight into structure: P&G's 1955 US product-division design was *right* for a homogeneous, fast-growing, land-grab market -- but that same design would be wrong the moment the market turned heterogeneous, slow-growing, or cost-sensitive. Structure must always follow strategy, and strategy must always follow context.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **250-Year Business Plan** | Matsushita founder's audacious long-range plan, split into 25-year blocks; Matsushita overtook Philips in category leadership within the first block |
| **Central Research Laboratories (CRL)** | Matsushita's centralized R&D, partially funded by corporate and partially by Product Divisions -- keeping it market-connected, unlike Philips's fully-funded, detached R&D |
| **Product Divisions (PDs), Matsushita** | The most powerful entities in Matsushita, holding final say on new product investment and strong ties to both R&D and marketing |
| **Mirror Image (Philips vs. Matsushita)** | Philips: strong adaptation, weak aggregation. Matsushita: strong aggregation, weak adaptation. Both weak on genuinely capturing arbitrage |
| **"Manishita" / Fast Follower** | Matsushita's nickname, reflecting its strategy of rapidly reverse-engineering and relaunching successful products |
| **Forced Arbitrage** | Matsushita's East Asian relocation, driven by host-country balance-of-payment barriers rather than deliberate strategy -- and undercut by forcing subsidiaries to buy components from Japan |
| **"Only Japanese" Positions** | Certain Matsushita overseas roles reserved exclusively for Japanese nationals, reinforcing tight central control |
| **60/40 Profit Rule** | Matsushita subsidiaries had to transfer 60 percent of profit to headquarters, retaining only 40 percent for working capital and fixed assets |
| **Plaza Accord Effect** | Post-oil-shock, US-pressured yen appreciation that erased Japan's cost advantage for roughly 30-34 years, opening the door to South Korean competitors |
| **Analogical Reasoning** | Teaching technique of learning a framework from an unfamiliar company's case, then re-contextualizing that abstracted learning into a familiar company |
| **P&G 1955 US Structure** | A product-division structure (Foods, Toilet Goods, Soaps & Detergents) with a nested functional structure (R&D, brand management, manufacturing, sales) inside each division |
| **Three Structural Dimensions** | Product (economies of scope/scale), Geography (local adaptability), Function (economies of scale/efficiency) -- every structure emphasizes one over the others |
| **Shared Services** | Functions genuinely shared across all divisions, such as basic research, corporate tax, legal, and HR |
| **Land-Grab Strategy** | P&G's 1955 objective: maximize growth and market share while cost efficiency is secondary, appropriate in a fast-expanding, low-competition, homogeneous market |
| **Restructuring as Power Play** | The change-management pattern where new CEOs restructure to destabilize entrenched power, signal change, and create room to deflect future blame |
