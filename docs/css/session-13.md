## Part 1: Closing the Loop -- Functional, Divisional, and Matrix Structures, One Last Time

Before starting anything new, the professor does a fast, clean recap of the three core structure types -- useful as a standalone exam summary in itself.

| Structure | Best For | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | Homogeneous, single business, stable environment | High efficiency -- each function specializes and standardizes | Poor at coordination, fast response, cross-functional innovation; needs bolt-ons like task forces or liaison roles in dynamic settings |
| **Divisional** (product, geography, or customer-based) | Multi-business firms where the functional structure no longer fits | High responsiveness on the chosen dimension -- product divisions capture economies of scope/scale for standardized global products; geography divisions adapt well to different regulations, customer needs, and compliance regimes; customer-based divisions suit big-account service firms (advertising, law) | Duplication of resources across divisions -- less efficient than a functional structure |
| **Matrix** | Need to integrate two knowledge types at once -- geography plus product, product plus function, or geography plus function | Genuinely unavoidable in large, diversified, internationalized corporations | Hard to manage -- requires the right capabilities, culture, people, processes, and KPIs to succeed |

> "Divisional structures may be less efficient as compared to functional structures, but the benefit is a high degree of responsiveness for the dimension that you have chosen."

Even divisional structures typically hand **profit responsibility** to divisional heads -- since tight corporate oversight of every division isn't efficient, giving heads P&L ownership aligns divisional priorities with corporate ones.

The professor also flags **horizontal team-based structures** (project-specific cross-functional teams, common in consulting, IT, or large construction firms like L&T) as a fourth type, but doesn't go deep -- these are task-specific, temporary teams that disband back into their home functions/divisions once the project ends.

### Student Question: What Does "Domain and Function" Even Mean in a Matrix?

A student (Ash) asks: geography and product make sense as two matrix dimensions, but what exactly would "domain and function" mean, since domain and function often sound like the same thing?

The professor answers with a **consulting-industry example**: **domain** could be something like the telecom sector, while **function** would be the specific expertise applied to it -- strategic management, chain management, organization design, operations management. A consulting firm can run a matrix across exactly these two dimensions: domain expertise on one axis, functional expertise on the other.

## Part 2: Organizing Is Bigger Than Just Structure

The professor makes a foundational point before moving on: **corporate advantage requires coordination among three things** -- your portfolio of businesses, your capabilities, and your organizing. Structure is only *one part* of organizing.

> "If you create a structure but if you don't have the other parts fitting to your structure, then it may not work."

**The other parts of organizing** that must stay consistent with your structure:

- **Planning and control systems** -- strategic planning, budgeting, MIS, resource allocation, transfer prices
- **HRM aspects** -- the type of personnel you hire, reward/incentive schemes, KPIs, balanced scorecards
- **Culture and style** -- top management's role, symbolic actions, management style

These topics overlap with macro OB coursework (culture, power and politics, change management, leadership), but the key exam point is: **structure alone is never sufficient.** Everything else in the organization has to evolve in step with it -- which is the exact bridge into the next topic.

## Part 3: The McKinsey 7S Framework (a Guided Tour, Thanks to Anish's Question)

A student, Anish, asks the professor to explain the **McKinsey 7S framework**, since many organizations currently lean on it heavily. The professor agrees to give a **quick introduction only** -- a proper treatment, he says, needs a full case plus a change-management context, and belongs in a Strategy Execution course.

### The Seven S's

| S | What It Covers | Story / Example Given in Class |
|---|---|---|
| **Superordinate Goals / Shared Values** | Vision, mission, values -- who we are, why we exist, what we want to achieve long-term | Referenced back to the Robin Hood case discussed earlier in the course |
| **Strategy** | How to achieve those shared goals | Must be consistent with the shared values above it |
| **Structure** | How you coordinate, control, specialize, and group activities | Must align with strategic priorities -- this is everything covered in Parts 1 and in Session 12 |
| **Systems** | Management control processes, MIS, planning, budgeting, resource allocation, performance measurement, transfer pricing | Creates vertical information flow to top management, ensures control and goal alignment across levels and functions |
| **Staff** | The people you have and their capabilities | Example: a traditional company shifting its hiring mix from diploma-based to degree-based senior staff; how quickly positions get filled; how redundancies (e.g., from adopting AI) get handled |
| **Style** | Leadership capabilities and priorities | Are leaders people-oriented or task-oriented? Can they be transformational in a turnaround? How do they build a second line? How do they handle power, politics, and internal change-blockers? |
| **Skills** | Capabilities at both the individual level and the organizational-process level | Example: building generative AI skills isn't just about individual training -- it's also about building the *workflows* that let people actually use those skills to move faster or more competitively |

> "All these sevens have to be consistent and have to evolve in step. When companies are changing, or when you have to implement change, you have to ensure that all these sevens evolve in a way that maintains consistency among each other."

### Hard S vs. Soft S

| Hard S (structural/formal) | Soft S (people/cultural) |
|---|---|
| Strategy | Skills |
| Structure | Style |
| Systems | Staff |

> "Soft is hard to change, and hard is also hard to change -- both are hard when you are managing a very difficult transformation."

**The professor's honest caveat:** this was a brief introduction only. A proper application needs real change-management cases where all seven S's actually shifted together -- that's a topic for a Strategy Execution course. He promises to try to find such cases, noting that a good one would combine **three things at once**: the firm's strategy, the 7S model, and a change-management framework.

### Follow-Up: The Influence Model

The same student follows up: does the **influence model** (role modeling, mentoring, and how leadership style cascades) fit inside the 7S framework?

> "So 7S framework will not cover everything. You'll have to fit something else into this 7S framework."

The professor's blunt answer: 7S is useful but not exhaustive -- other frameworks like the influence model need to be layered in separately, they don't simply nest inside one of the seven S's.

## Part 4: New Topic -- Strategic Analysis of Acquisitions

With that detour closed, the professor pivots to the session's real topic: **strategic analysis of acquisitions**, using the **Cadbury Schweppes case series.**

> "This course has very special cases. Each case is very unique, or very important to understand. Cadbury Schweppes is one such case series."

**Structure of the case series:** there are actually **four parts (A, B, C, D)**, but the course only assigns the **first two**. The remaining two parts cover **post-acquisition integration** -- an important topic, but one that belongs in a specialized M&A course or a Strategy Execution course. Both later parts are also extremely numbers-heavy, and the professor didn't want to overload the reading list beyond what's needed to extract the course's key learning points.

### The Backstory: Why "Cadbury Schweppes" Is Already a Cautionary Tale

**Cadbury** was originally a pure **chocolate and confectionery** business -- chocolates, KitKat-style wafer-and-chocolate bars, small wrapped chocolates sold in packets, and other confectioneries like toffees. It grew mainly by acquiring related businesses in that same category.

**Schweppes** was originally a **soft-drinks** business. At some point, the two merged -- and the professor calls it one of the **most inappropriate mergers** on record: even though the merger happened, **no significant benefit came to either side.**

**Why the Cadbury-Schweppes merger itself fails Porter's three tests (in hindsight):**

- **Attractiveness test fails:** soft drinks *was* an attractive industry -- but for Coke and Pepsi, not for Schweppes. Schweppes was a **distant third**, struggling, with falling margins and falling revenues, and had introduced only **one new product in seven years.**
- **Cost of entry vs. benefit fails:** the cost of entering was high relative to what the combined entity could realistically capture.
- **Better-off test fails:** there was **no real synergy** between chocolates and soft drinks -- organizing cost ended up higher than any benefit captured.

The two businesses struggled together for roughly **two and a half decades**, before **demerging around 2007-2008.** That demerger eventually feeds into a later case in this series (Mondelez), where the confectionery-only piece of Kraft split off and effectively became Mondelez, later acquiring the Cadbury-only business. Today's case is **the precursor to that story**: how Cadbury Schweppes came to acquire Adams in the first place.

### Meet Adams: The Gum Business Nobody Invested In

**Adams** was in the **chewing gum** business. It had genuinely excellent R&D capability, extremely innovative gum products, and was the **world's number two** player overall -- and **number one in many specific national markets.**

But Adams was **not independent.** It had first been acquired by a pharmaceutical firm, which itself then got acquired by another pharma firm. Passed between corporate parents this way, Adams never got the funding or corporate attention it needed -- lots of internal capability, but no major recent success stories to show for it.

> Think of Adams as a **stepchild**: adopted by a new parent that has zero interest in keeping this child in the family -- it's keeping the business in the portfolio for a maximum of two years, **purely for tax reasons.** Once that deadline passes, it will be sold. This company cannot remain independent -- if Cadbury Schweppes doesn't buy it, somebody else will.

### The Three Questions Porter's Tests Will Answer

1. **Should** Cadbury Schweppes acquire Adams? (the **attractiveness test**)
2. If yes, **at what valuation**? (the **cost of entry test**)
3. What **synergies** justify that valuation? (the **better-off test**)

## Part 5: The Options on the Table

The class works through what the real strategic choices are, given that Cadbury Schweppes has money available to invest (through its own operating cash, borrowing, or an equity transaction):

| Option | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **1. Do nothing** | Remain as Cadbury Schweppes; Adams stays independent or gets acquired by someone else; continue organic growth plus small related acquisitions in your own category |
| **2. Acquire Adams** | At a specific valuation, enter the gum business |
| **3. Reinvest that same money into your own chocolate business** | Grow more aggressively organically instead of diversifying |
| **4. Strategic alliance instead of acquisition** (raised by student Abhishek) | Considered, but ultimately ruled out for this specific case |

A student points out that Option 3 sounds a lot like Option 1 -- the professor agrees they're similar, but the comparison being drawn is deliberately about a **fixed pot of money**: should that pot go into acquiring Adams, or into growing your own business more aggressively?

### Why the Strategic Alliance Option Doesn't Work Here

The professor walks through why an alliance is infeasible in this specific situation, even though the general principle -- *"if an alliance is feasible, you should go for that rather than acquire"* -- is valid:

- Adams **must** be sold (the "stepchild," tax-driven timeline) -- it cannot remain independent for an alliance to even attach to.
- The most likely buyers are either a **private equity firm**, a **competitor** (e.g., Nestle), or **Wrigley** (a major gum rival) -- but Wrigley acquiring Adams would create serious competitive concentration concerns, making regulatory approval unlikely.
- If a **competitor** buys Adams, a strategic alliance with that entity becomes impossible by definition.
- Even setting aside who buys it: what would the actual **basis** for a tie-up even be, if the two businesses (chocolate and gum) don't share obvious synergy? That question, the professor notes, can only really be answered once the synergy analysis (Part 8 below) is done.

> "It is very unlikely that this company will remain independent... If it gets acquired by one of your competitors, you can definitely not enter into a strategic alliance with that person."

### The Class Divide: Gorav vs. Subha

Two competing student views frame the whole debate:

> **Gorav's view:** "They should have invested in their own business. They were not in the top three, they had very less share. Considering their current positioning, they should have reinvested and tried to acquire more market share. Once they've reached top two or top three and don't have more growth potential there, then they should think of acquiring some other business."

> **Subha's counter-view:** "Everybody was already struggling to secure more market share in chocolate, because it was not even in the top three and the major players were far ahead. If it enters a gum business, it will give it a more diversified, bigger market and maybe more capability to fight its competitors who are already far ahead."

The professor frames these as the **two classic rationales for diversification**:

> "One view is that if you have not achieved the desired success in your own business, why invest elsewhere. The new view is that if you can invest in another attractive business, why invest in your own unattractive business."

He doesn't resolve the debate immediately -- instead, the class spends the rest of the session building the actual analysis needed to answer it properly.

## Part 6: Porter's Five Forces -- Gum Business vs. Chocolate Business (a Comparative Analysis)

**Crucial methodological point** before diving in: this is a **comparative**, not absolute, industry analysis. Cadbury Schweppes has money to invest either way -- the real question is simply: *compared to chocolate, how attractive does gum look?*

> "We don't have absolute information. We will have a comparative information. Compared to chocolate, how does the gum business look?"

The professor also insists on being **conservative**: benefit of the doubt is never given to the gum business. Only forces where gum looks *decisively, unambiguously* more attractive count as a clear win -- everything else defaults to "tie" or "unclear."

### Force 1: Threat of Entry

| Dimension | Gum Business | Chocolate Business |
|---|---|---|
| R&D intensity | High -- roughly 2% of revenue; center-filled gums, medicinal/functional gums in the pipeline; **patents** on gum bases and specific flavors (raised by student Maitri) that even rivals struggle to replicate; new flavors take a **minimum of 16 months** to bring to market | Low R&D intensity |
| Distribution/shelf space | Sold from the scarce **"hot zone"** -- the small rack near the checkout counter (raised by student Manish) -- fast-moving, impulse-purchase real estate | Sold from wide confectionery aisles -- much more shelf space overall |
| Two-firm concentration ratio | Adams and Wrigley together hold **more than 50% of global market share** | No single firm holds more than **12-13%** market share anywhere |
| Capital expenditure | High -- large plants serving many countries at once, enabling an **aggregation strategy** (standardized global product) | Lower -- smaller, country-specific plants suited to an **adaptation strategy** (localized taste and recipe) |
| Advertising intensity | Around **16%** of revenue spent on marketing and distribution build-out | Comparatively lower, more fragmented spend |

> "The higher the two-firm concentration ratio, the more difficult it is to enter that business -- because of the scale advantage, market share advantage, and branding advantage, everywhere. Countering that as a new entrant would be next to impossible, or would take unbelievable resource investment, time, and struggle."

**Verdict: gum business has far higher entry barriers -- clearly more attractive on this force.**

### Force 2: Buyer Power (the Retailers, Not the End Consumer)

Both businesses face the same scarce "hot zone" shelf-space problem in front of large organized retailers. But gum has real counters to that retailer power:

- High two-firm concentration means retailers have few alternative brands to stock in that hot zone
- Gum products are small, so they don't consume much shelf space
- Gum carries **very high margins** (Wrigley's margin was cited at **20%**, versus lower chocolate margins) -- a commission-based benefit for the retailer
- Gum items are extremely fast-moving and heavily advertised, so retailers want them regardless of negotiating leverage

> "If my MO can kill me, my MO can kill you -- and therefore nobody's going to kill each other."

Chocolate, by contrast, has wider aisle space (less scarcity pressure) but a far more fragmented set of manufacturers with lower individual market share, so less collective countervailing power against retailers.

**Verdict: roughly a tie.** The professor deliberately does **not** credit gum with a clean win here -- buyer power is structurally high in gum but kept in check, and structurally more moderate but less countered in chocolate. Comparatively, they land in similar territory.

### Force 3: Supplier Power

- **Gum:** raw materials are essentially **bulk industrial chemicals**, increasingly synthesized rather than sourced naturally -- negligible supplier power. (A student, Chirag, connects this back to the earlier **Coca-Cola concentrate case**, where the same conclusion was reached about bulk chemical inputs -- the professor confirms the parallel holds.)
- **Chocolate:** heavily dependent on **cocoa bean** supply, a genuine commodity risk. The professor's own real-world example: in the two years following COVID, **cocoa futures rose more than the S&P 500 and the "Magnificent Seven" stocks combined** -- illustrating just how volatile and powerful cocoa supply constraints can become.

**Verdict: gum clearly more attractive -- much lower supplier risk.**

### Force 4: Threat of Substitutes

- **Gum:** sugarless/sugar-free gum is becoming steadily more popular. Crucially, **gum is substituting other products**, not the other way around -- it's increasingly used as a **delivery platform** for medicine, minerals like calcium, teeth-whitening agents, and other benefits, because gum spends more physical time in the mouth than almost any comparable product. Natural alternatives (cloves and other traditional fresheners, raised by student Senthil) exist but are only culturally understood and accessible in specific markets like India, limiting their reach as effective substitutes elsewhere.
- **Chocolate:** facing real substitution pressure -- rising health consciousness, huge product variety pulling demand in every direction, and even **gifting substitution** (dry fruits and other items increasingly replacing chocolate as a gift, especially for adults). Chocolate still retains some cultural and seasonal "craze" potential (the professor's aside about investing in Kunafa being more profitable than cocoa or coffee beans over a recent period, and the French cultural habit of pairing dark chocolate with coffee and brandy after a meal) -- but the overall global trend is one of chocolate being increasingly questioned and substituted.

**Verdict: gum much more attractive** -- substitution dynamics run decisively in gum's favor.

### Force 5: Rivalry

- **Chocolate:** high rivalry -- a fragmented market with global brands and countless local ones competing at every price point, country by country, with no real scale advantage protecting anyone.
- **Gum:** only two major global players, Wrigley and Adams -- but they've effectively **split the market by segment**: Wrigley dominates **sugar-based** gums, Adams dominates **sugarless** gums. Despite the high concentration, actual head-to-head rivalry is low because the two giants aren't really fighting over the same customers.

**Verdict: gum much more attractive -- lower actual rivalry intensity despite the concentrated structure.**

### The Scorecard

| Force | Winner |
|---|---|
| Threat of Entry | **Gum** (clearly) |
| Buyer Power | Tie |
| Supplier Power | **Gum** (clearly) |
| Threat of Substitutes | **Gum** (clearly) |
| Rivalry | **Gum** (clearly) |

> "So the first taste of Michael Porter -- are you entering into a business which is attractive? The answer is yes."

**Porter's Test 1 (Attractiveness) passes: the gum business is comparatively more attractive than staying purely in chocolate.**

## Part 7: Why This Is a "Historical" Opportunity

Attractiveness alone doesn't justify the deal -- the professor adds a crucial contextual layer. Since the gum business is attractive but **cannot be entered organically** (building a brand from scratch against a duopoly holding more than 50% combined share is, in his words, "next to impossible," or would require unbelievable resources and time), **the only way in is by acquiring an existing top player.**

Adams -- a genuine top-two global player -- happens to be for sale, and at what the professor calls **an extremely attractive valuation**, precisely *because* it's a neglected stepchild, not because its true potential is exhausted.

> "So your family will be benefited significantly if you acquire this business."

## Part 8: Porter's Test 2 -- Market Position and the Case Against Acquiring

This is where the class works through the genuine counterarguments -- the professor is explicit that these matter and deserve fair treatment.

### The Misleading Headline Number

After acquisition, the combined entity's **confectionery market share would be 6.9%** -- the **highest** in the world for "confectionery" as a category.

> "But is this the right market share to look at? The answer is no. Confectionery market share has got no meaning."

**Why it's meaningless:** "confectionery" artificially lumps gum and chocolate together, even though they're two entirely separate, unrelated competitive categories with different customers, channels, and dynamics.

**The market share that actually matters, category by category:**

| Category | Position After Acquisition |
|---|---|
| **Gum** | **Number 2** in the world |
| **Chocolate** | **Number 4** in the world |

### The Counterargument: No Number-One Position Anywhere

- The acquisition doesn't create a **number-one position in any single relevant category** -- number 2 in gum (a position Cadbury Schweppes genuinely could not have built organically), and number 4 in chocolate, which is **no material improvement** on the company's already-weaker original position.
- If judged purely on the "does this grab market leadership" metric, the acquisition doesn't deliver a clean win.

### Cost of Acquisition and Genuine Uncertainty

The professor argues the **true cost should be low** relative to the opportunity, because:

- Adams's valuation is depressed precisely because its parent starved it of investment.
- It has a failed recent product launch, but also **ready, undeveloped products and patents** -- including center-filled gum products that even Wrigley, the market leader, would struggle to quickly match.
- All that's really needed to unlock this value is **management focus and budget** -- not a fundamental turnaround.

But there's real risk too: this would be **the largest acquisition Cadbury Schweppes has ever made.**

> "What if the benefits are less than anticipated and they do not outweigh the acquisition cost? What may provide more certain and higher returns -- investing into Adams's acquisition, or investing into strengthening Cadbury Schweppes's own underperforming businesses? There is no clear-cut answer to this. That is your top management judgment call."

## Part 9: Porter's Test 3 -- Cost Synergies vs. Revenue Synergies

The professor introduces the two fundamentally different types of acquisition synergy, each with its own story:

| Type | What It Means | Story Example | Risk Profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost synergies** | Reducing duplication of resources to save money | Cadbury has a plant in Canada, Adams has a plant in Canada -- combine the two facilities, let go of redundant staff the Adams plant no longer needs, cut duplicate machinery, maintenance, and engineering support -- pure economies of scope | Immediate, certain, and less risky |
| **Revenue synergies** | Using existing assets to generate *new* revenue, not to cut costs | Cadbury has already built a sales and distribution network in a country Adams wasn't previously present in -- Adams "piggybacks" on that existing network to enter, generating additional revenue using Cadbury's existing assets | Long-term, requires real work, and is more risky |

> "The higher the cost synergies in a particular acquisition scenario, the more easily you will get the support from your shareholders, stock analysts, etc. The more uncertain those benefits are -- which is what revenue synergies are -- the harder that support becomes."

### Applying This to Cadbury and Adams Specifically

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Geography** | Complementary -- Cadbury strong in Europe and former Commonwealth countries; Adams strong in the US, the Americas, and developing countries |
| **Distribution channel** | Complementary -- Cadbury strong in the confectionery aisle; Adams strong in the "hot zone" checkout counter |
| **Capabilities** | Complementary -- Adams strong in R&D and product innovation; Cadbury Schweppes strong in marketing, commercialization, sales, and distribution |
| **Manufacturing** | **Not complementary at all** -- chocolate and gum manufacturing processes are fundamentally different, so there's little manufacturing-level synergy |

**The key structural takeaway:**

> "High complementarity, but low asset overlap. If the asset overlap is high, then you can reduce that overlap and immediately achieve cost synergies. If the asset overlap is low, then you'll have to go for revenue synergies."

**Conclusion: this deal has genuine complementary strengths, but low physical asset overlap -- meaning the immediate, low-risk cost synergies will be modest, and most of the value has to come from harder, slower, riskier revenue synergies.** This is precisely the tension the top-management judgment call in Part 8 has to weigh.

## Part 10: The Hershey/Wrigley Side-Story -- Why Nestle Was the Real Threat

A student, Manish, asks about a related historical episode: wasn't **Hershey** also up for sale around this time, with Wrigley interested in buying it?

The professor tells a genuinely surprising real-world story he only recently pieced together himself, from a **Financial Times article about OpenAI:**

> "Hershey management, or the trust that manages Hershey, wanted to sell Hershey and then went back on that very quickly. Why? Both OpenAI and [Hershey's controlling entity] have got a nonprofit, trust-based structure at the very top level, under which they've got the profit-making entities. Nonprofit companies come under the purview of a local authority -- some collector-equivalent figure in the US -- who has a say in deciding what a trust can and can't do."

When the controlling trust wanted to sell Hershey, the relevant local authority **feared political backlash** -- if plants left the locality as a result of the sale, there would be real local economic fallout -- and simply **did not approve** the sale. Hershey stayed independent, for a reason entirely unrelated to strategy or valuation.

> "The article said that if push comes to shove, even OpenAI may face the same question, if it is not able to manage that particular authority which is relevant for it."

**What this meant for the Adams situation:**

- **Hershey** was not for sale, and its management wasn't interested in buying Adams anyway.
- **Wrigley** *was* interested in Adams, but combining the two dominant gum players would face serious **regulatory/antitrust risk.**
- That left **Nestle** as the practically most likely rival bidder for Adams.

### How Cadbury Schweppes Preempted Nestle

The case's Part A describes, in detail, how carefully Cadbury Schweppes prepared its bid to specifically shut Nestle out:

> "Their entire focus was that if the bidding war happens, Nestle will win. But we don't want to give that opportunity to Nestle."

Cadbury Schweppes knew Nestle's structural weakness firsthand -- they had previously **jointly bid for Hershey together with Nestle**, and understood that Nestle's US subsidiary had to get every major decision approved by its **European headquarters**, which slowed down its bidding and counterbidding process.

**Their strategy:** submit a very **clean, fast, attractive** bid, and pressure Adams's parent company to commit to accepting the offer within roughly **one month** -- deliberately using speed to push Nestle out of the process before Nestle's slower approval chain could even react.

> "That is what Cadbury Schweppes did to force their kind of decision-making on the Adams's parent company."

**The meta-lesson of Session 13:** Porter's three tests -- is the industry attractive, what does it cost to enter, and are you genuinely better off -- aren't abstract theory here. They get applied with real rigor: a comparative five-forces analysis force by force, a market-share sanity check that exposes a misleading headline number, and a synergy framework that distinguishes immediate cost savings from slower, riskier revenue gains. The professor's own closing point about Cadbury and Schweppes's original merger is the cautionary bookend: apply these same three tests honestly, and you can explain both a merger that failed for two and a half decades, and one (Adams) that might actually work.

## Key Terms Table

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Functional Structure** | Best for homogeneous, single businesses in stable environments; high efficiency, poor at cross-functional coordination and fast response |
| **Divisional Structure** | Product-, geography-, or customer-based; high responsiveness on the chosen dimension, but resource duplication and lower efficiency |
| **Matrix Structure** | Needed to integrate two knowledge/capability types at once; hard to manage but often unavoidable in large, diversified, international firms |
| **Domain vs. Function Matrix** | A consulting-industry matrix example -- domain (e.g., telecom) on one axis, functional expertise (e.g., strategy, chain management) on the other |
| **Organizing Beyond Structure** | Planning/control systems, HRM (staffing, incentives, KPIs), and culture/style -- all must stay consistent with the chosen structure |
| **McKinsey 7S Framework** | Shared Values, Strategy, Structure, Systems, Staff, Style, Skills -- all seven must evolve consistently together during change |
| **Hard S** | Strategy, Structure, Systems -- the more formal, structural elements |
| **Soft S** | Skills, Style, Staff -- the people/cultural elements; both hard and soft S are difficult to change |
| **Influence Model** | Role modeling, mentoring, and leadership cascade -- doesn't fit neatly inside 7S; needs to be layered in separately |
| **Cadbury Schweppes Merger (historical)** | An earlier, largely unsuccessful merger between chocolate-maker Cadbury and soft-drink maker Schweppes; failed Porter's three tests; the two later demerged around 2007-2008 |
| **Adams** | A leading gum maker, world number two overall and number one in many specific markets, but neglected and starved of investment by its pharma-company parent -- the "stepchild" up for forced sale |
| **Porter's Three Tests** | (1) Attractiveness test -- is the industry attractive; (2) Cost of entry test -- what does entry cost relative to the benefit; (3) Better-off test -- are there real synergies that justify the deal |
| **Comparative Industry Analysis** | Since Cadbury has money to invest either way, the five-forces analysis compares gum vs. chocolate relatively, not in absolute terms, and stays conservative about crediting gum with a "win" |
| **Two-Firm Concentration Ratio** | Combined market share of the top two players in an industry; more than 50% in gum (Adams and Wrigley) vs. no firm above 12-13% in chocolate |
| **Hot Zone** | The scarce checkout-counter shelf space where gum is sold -- fast-moving, impulse-purchase real estate that acts as both an entry barrier and a source of (checked) buyer power |
| **Aggregation vs. Adaptation Strategy** | Gum's large, scale-driven plants serving many countries (aggregation) vs. chocolate's smaller, country-specific plants (adaptation) |
| **Confectionery Market Share Trap** | The misleading 6.9% "number one in confectionery" headline figure, which artificially combines two unrelated categories (gum and chocolate) |
| **Category-Specific Market Share** | The real metric that matters -- number 2 in gum, number 4 in chocolate, after the Adams acquisition |
| **Cost Synergies** | Savings from reducing duplicated resources (e.g., combining overlapping plants); immediate, certain, and lower-risk |
| **Revenue Synergies** | New revenue generated by leveraging existing assets in a new market (e.g., Adams piggybacking on Cadbury's distribution network); long-term, uncertain, and higher-risk |
| **High Complementarity, Low Asset Overlap** | The Cadbury-Adams synergy profile -- strong complementary strengths in geography, distribution, and capabilities, but very different manufacturing processes, meaning limited cost synergy and greater reliance on revenue synergy |
| **Hershey's Nonprofit Trust Structure** | The reason Hershey stayed independent -- a local regulatory authority overseeing its controlling nonprofit trust blocked a sale over feared local job-loss backlash |
| **Nestle as the Real Rival Bidder** | With Hershey unavailable and Wrigley facing antitrust risk, Nestle was the most realistic competing bidder for Adams |
| **Cadbury Schweppes's Preemptive Bid Strategy** | A fast, clean bid with a roughly one-month acceptance deadline, designed to exploit Nestle's slower Europe-approval-dependent bidding process and win Adams before a bidding war could start |
